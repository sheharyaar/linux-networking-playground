Chapter 5

Netfilter and conntrack: hooks, priorities, tuples, and NAT

~RT5 min · L3 · Spine: our skb is inside the PRE_ROUTING call that chapter 4 treated as a black box. This chapter opens it: what nf_hook_slow walks, in what order, and what conntrack and NAT write into the skb and into their own tables before the routing decision runs.

The list, and the numbers that order it

Chapter 0 showed the two gates in front of a hook. When both pass, nf_hook_slow (net/netfilter/core.c:612) walks an array of hook entries, each a function pointer and a priority, sorted by priority when the array is rebuilt on registration (nf_hook_entries_grow, line 97). The loop at lines 620 to 640 calls each entry and acts on its verdict: accept moves to the next entry; drop frees the skb with reason NETFILTER_DROP and returns; queue sends the skb to a user-space program and returns; stolen returns without freeing, because the entry now owns the skb. When the loop runs out of entries the macro that called it runs the continuation, ip_rcv_finish for PRE_ROUTING.

The priorities are plain integers, and the whole "raw, mangle, nat, filter" order of the chart is just these numbers from include/uapi/linux/netfilter_ipv4.h:29 to 45:

RAW-300nftables chains declared priority raw; the place for notrack and for nftrace
CONNTRACK-200ipv4_conntrack_in at PRE_ROUTING, ipv4_conntrack_local at LOCAL_OUT

The router trace makes the table concrete. At PRE_ROUTING on r0 the SYN visits, in order: ipv4_conntrack_defrag, then nft_do_chain_inet for the lab's raw chain, then ipv4_conntrack_in, then nf_nat_ipv4_pre_routing, then nft_do_chain_inet again for the filter chain. Minus 400, minus 300, minus 200, minus 100, zero.

An nftables chain is one entry

Each nftables base chain, a chain declared with a hook and a priority, is registered as one entry whose function is nft_do_chain_ipv4 or nft_do_chain_inet (net/netfilter/nft_chain_filter.c:15). It calls nft_do_chain (net/netfilter/nf_tables_core.c:250), which walks the rules (line 275), evaluates each rule's expressions, and acts on the rule's verdict: continue to the next rule, accept or drop the packet, or jump to another chain and come back through a small stack (line 322). When the walk finishes without a verdict, the chain's policy applies.

This is the layer nft monitor trace shows. A rule containing meta nftrace set 1 sets the bit at skb->nf_trace (net/netfilter/nft_meta.c:501), and every chain the skb enters afterwards reports through nft_trace_packet (lines 297 and 340) and nft_trace_notify (net/netfilter/nf_tables_trace.c:238). The bench output for one SYN reads, one line per event: the raw chain saw the packet on r0; the rule that set the trace bit matched with verdict continue; the raw chain's policy was accept; the prerouting chain's policy was accept; the forward chain saw it with iif r0 oif r1; the rule ct state new counter matched; and so on through postrouting. pwru saw those chains as five nft_do_chain_inet lines. The two tools are looking at the same walk from two altitudes.

Conntrack: a tuple, an entry, and a pointer in the skb

The entry at minus 200 is ipv4_conntrack_in (net/netfilter/nf_conntrack_proto.c:208), which calls nf_conntrack_in (net/netfilter/nf_conntrack_core.c:2004). Its first move (lines 2011 to 2018) is to check whether the skb already carries a conntrack pointer; a packet that has been through this hook before, for example one that came back in through lo, is accepted without a second lookup. Otherwise resolve_normal_ct (line 1862) builds the tuple: source and destination address, protocol, source and destination port (nf_ct_get_tuple, line 1876), and looks it up in the conntrack hash table.

On a miss, init_conntrack (line 1898) allocates a conntrack entry, struct nf_conn (include/net/netfilter/nf_conntrack.h:74). The entry holds two tuples in tuplehash (line 96): the original direction, and the reply tuple, which starts as the original with source and destination swapped and is what a returning packet will be matched against. It also holds a status bitmap (line 99) and a timeout (line 89). Then lines 1907 to 1920 classify the packet: a reply-direction match is IP_CT_ESTABLISHED_REPLY; an original-direction match on an entry that has seen a reply is IP_CT_ESTABLISHED; an expected connection is IP_CT_RELATED; a fresh entry is IP_CT_NEW. nf_ct_set (line 1921) packs the entry pointer and that classification into skb->_nfct, the field from chapter 0's table, and nf_ct_get (nf_conntrack.h:169) unpacks it. A protocol handler, nf_conntrack_tcp_packet in the trace, then runs the TCP state machine on the entry.

The entry is not yet in the hash table. It is unconfirmed until the packet reaches the entry with priority INT_MAX, nf_confirm, registered at both LOCAL_IN and POST_ROUTING (nf_conntrack_proto.c:240 to 265), which calls __nf_conntrack_confirm (nf_conntrack_core.c:1179) to insert it. The delay is deliberate. A packet that a filter chain drops never creates an entry, and NAT gets a chance to rewrite the reply tuple before anyone can look it up. Meanwhile every chain with a priority above minus 200 can read ct state from skb->_nfct: that is why ct state new works in a filter chain at zero and matches nothing useful in a raw chain at minus 300, where skb->_nfct is still empty and the expression evaluates to invalid (net/netfilter/nft_ct.c:74).

The conntrack -L line for the lab connection reads the entry back: protocol, timeout, TCP state, the original tuple, then the reply tuple, then [ASSURED] once both directions have been seen. When no NAT is involved the reply tuple is the mirror of the original. The next section changes that.

NAT: decided once, applied every time

NAT is four hook entries, nf_nat_ipv4_ops (net/netfilter/nf_nat_proto.c:766 to 795): destination NAT at PRE_ROUTING and LOCAL_OUT with priority minus 100, source NAT at POST_ROUTING and LOCAL_IN with priority 100. All four call nf_nat_inet_fn (net/netfilter/nf_nat_core.c:898). It reads the conntrack entry from the skb (line 907) and switches on the classification (line 918).

For a NEW packet whose entry has no NAT mapping yet for this direction (lines 918 to 941), it runs the nat chains registered at this hook, the nftables chains declared type nat, through nft_nat_do_chain. A rule such as masquerade calls nf_nat_setup_info (line 765), which chooses the new address and port and rewrites the entry's reply tuple to match. If no rule matched, a null mapping is recorded (lines 942 to 946) so the chains are never consulted again for this connection. Then, for this packet and for every later packet in either direction (the established branch at lines 955 to 960), nf_nat_packet (line 860) calls nf_nat_manip_pkt (nf_nat_proto.c:357) to rewrite the addresses, ports and checksums in the header. The mapping lives in the conntrack entry; the rules were consulted once.

The bench shows it in the tuple column. With a masquerade rule on the router's natpost chain, the SYN reaches POST_ROUTING on r1 reading 10.5.1.2:57158 to 10.5.2.2:8080, passes the filter chain, enters nf_nat_ipv4_out, nf_nat_inet_fn, nft_nat_do_chain, nf_nat_masquerade_ipv4, then nf_nat_manip_pkt and two checksum updates, and from the next line reads 10.5.2.1:57158 to 10.5.2.2:8080. Then nf_confirm inserts the entry with its rewritten reply tuple, which conntrack -L now shows as src=10.5.2.2 dst=10.5.2.1. When the SYN-ACK comes back on r1 addressed to 10.5.2.1, PRE_ROUTING finds the entry by that reply tuple, classifies the packet as an established reply, and nf_nat_inet_fn applies the inverse rewrite through nf_nat_manip_pkt with no chain walk at all; the tuple column flips back to 10.5.1.2 before ip_rcv_finish_core runs. That last clause is the reason the reverse translation lives at PRE_ROUTING: the routing decision must see the real destination, or it would deliver the SYN-ACK to the router's own stack.

Try it

Watch the rule walk and the tuple rewrite on the router from chapter 4.

# terminal 1: kernel functions in the router namespace, all modules included
sudo pwru --all-kmods --filter-netns /run/netns/pp-rt 'tcp and port 8080'
# terminal 2: the nftables rule walk (stdbuf keeps the output flowing when redirected)
sudo ip netns exec pp-rt stdbuf -oL nft monitor trace
# terminal 3: one request, then read the state
sudo ip netns exec pp-c curl -s -o /dev/null http://10.5.2.2:8080/
sudo ip netns exec pp-rt nft list chain inet lab forward
sudo ip netns exec pp-rt conntrack -L
# now add masquerade and repeat; watch the tuple column in terminal 1 change at POST_ROUTING
sudo ip netns exec pp-rt nft add rule inet lab natpost oifname r1 masquerade
sudo ip netns exec pp-rt conntrack -F
sudo ip netns exec pp-c curl -s -o /dev/null http://10.5.2.2:8080/
sudo ip netns exec pp-rt conntrack -L

Before masquerade the entry's reply tuple is the mirror of the original. After it, the reply tuple's destination is 10.5.2.1, and the server's log shows requests from 10.5.2.1 instead of 10.5.1.2. The ct state new counter grows by one per connection; the established,related counter by one per packet after the first.

Recap

A hook is an array of entries sorted by integer priority, walked by nf_hook_slow. Each nftables base chain is one entry. Conntrack, at minus 200, builds a tuple, finds or allocates an entry with an original and a reply tuple, and stores the entry and its classification in skb->_nfct; the entry is confirmed into the table only at the very end, by nf_confirm at INT_MAX. NAT, at minus 100 and plus 100, consults its chains only for the first packet of a connection, stores the mapping in the entry's reply tuple, and rewrites every packet from that mapping. Destination NAT and the reverse of source NAT run at PRE_ROUTING because the routing decision must see the translated address.

New words in this chapter

hook entry, priority
A function pointer plus an integer; entries are walked in ascending order by nf_hook_slow.
base chain
An nftables chain bound to a hook and priority; registered as one hook entry running nft_do_chain.
tuple
Source address, destination address, protocol, source port, destination port; the conntrack key.
conntrack entry, nf_conn
The tracked connection: original and reply tuples, status bits, timeout.
reply tuple
The tuple a returning packet must match; rewritten by NAT, mirror of the original otherwise.
ctinfo: NEW, ESTABLISHED, RELATED, ESTABLISHED_REPLY
The classification packed into skb->_nfct next to the entry pointer.
unconfirmed, nf_confirm
An entry exists but is not in the hash table until the last entry at POST_ROUTING or LOCAL_IN inserts it.
nf_nat_inet_fn
The one function behind all four NAT hook entries: run nat chains on the first packet, then rewrite from the mapping.
nf_nat_setup_info, nf_nat_manip_pkt
Choose the mapping and store it in the reply tuple; rewrite one packet's header and checksums.
nftrace, nf_trace
The bit a rule sets on the skb so every later chain reports its rule walk to nft monitor trace.

Check yourself

1. You move a ct state new accept rule from a chain with priority filter into a chain with priority raw on the same hook, and it stops matching. Nothing else changed. Why?

Answer

At priority minus 300 the conntrack entry at minus 200 has not run yet, so skb->_nfct is still empty and the ct state expression evaluates to the invalid state (net/netfilter/nft_ct.c:74). Order within a hook is the integer priority, walked ascending by nf_hook_slow (net/netfilter/core.c:620). Anything that reads conntrack state must sit above minus 200; anything that wants to stop tracking, notrack, must sit below it, which is what raw is for.

The near miss is "raw chains cannot use ct expressions". They can; the expression runs and returns invalid. The rule is not rejected, it is silently useless, which is worse.

2. After you add a masquerade rule, the nat chain's counter shows one packet per connection while the filter chain's counter shows dozens. A colleague concludes NAT is being skipped for most packets and the source addresses must be wrong. Are they?

Answer

No. nf_nat_inet_fn (net/netfilter/nf_nat_core.c:918 to 960) runs the nat chains only for a NEW packet whose entry has no mapping yet; it records the mapping in the entry's reply tuple through nf_nat_setup_info, and every later packet in both directions goes straight to nf_nat_packet and nf_nat_manip_pkt without touching a rule. The counter measures rule evaluations, and there is one per connection. Confirm with conntrack -L: the reply tuple carries the translated address for the whole connection.

The near miss is expecting NAT counters to behave like filter counters. Filter chains run per packet; nat chains are a per-connection decision. That is also why changing a nat rule does not affect connections that already exist.

3. Trace the SYN-ACK of a masqueraded connection as it enters the router on r1, addressed to the router's own address 10.5.2.1. Name the entry that rewrites it and explain what would happen if that rewrite ran after the routing decision instead of before it.

Answer

nf_nat_ipv4_pre_routing at priority minus 100, through nf_nat_inet_fn, which finds the entry by the reply tuple, sees an established reply, and calls nf_nat_manip_pkt to put 10.5.1.2 back as the destination. This happens inside ip_rcv's hook call, before ip_rcv_finish runs ip_route_input_slow. If the rewrite came after routing, the lookup would see 10.5.2.1, a local address, return RTN_LOCAL, and deliver the SYN-ACK to the router's own TCP, which has no such connection and would answer with a reset. The bench trace shows the tuple column flip back to 10.5.1.2 two lines before ip_rcv_finish_core.

The near miss is "the reverse NAT happens at POST_ROUTING, mirroring where the forward NAT happened". Source NAT's reverse is a destination rewrite, so it lives with destination NAT, at PRE_ROUTING, on the way in.
