Chapter 1

Birth of an skb: the NIC, the interrupt, NAPI, and the backlog

~RT1 min · HW · Spine: a frame carrying our TCP segment has just arrived at the network card. There is no skb yet. By the end of this chapter there is one, and it is standing at the door of the L2 receive loop.

This chapter skips link training, PHY negotiation, and the PCIe transaction layer. All real, all irrelevant here. It treats one Ethernet driver, igb for Intel gigabit cards, as the worked example, because its receive loop is short enough to read. Your laptop's Wi-Fi driver takes a longer road through the wireless stack and arrives at the same door; the last section shows where.

The ring: the card writes before anyone asks

The driver does not fetch packets. It prepares empty buffers in advance and the card fills them. The structure that holds this arrangement is the receive ring: a circular array of descriptors, each a small record with the physical address of one buffer and, after the card has used it, the length and status of the frame it wrote there. In igb the ring is struct igb_ring (drivers/net/ethernet/intel/igb/igb.h:319), with the descriptor array at desc, the ring size at count, and two indices: next_to_use, the next slot the driver will hand to the card, and next_to_clean, the next slot the driver expects the card to have filled. The gap between them is the number of frames waiting.

The card writes into those buffers by DMA, direct memory access: it moves bytes into RAM without the CPU touching them, then writes the length into the descriptor. The buffers come from a page pool, a per-ring recycling allocator that hands out page fragments already mapped for DMA, so the driver does not pay a mapping cost per frame. igb_alloc_rx_buffers (igb_main.c:9205) refills empty slots. When the ring is full of unread frames and the card has nowhere to write, it drops on the wire side and increments a counter you read with ethtool -S.

The interrupt does almost nothing

When a frame lands, the card raises an interrupt. The handler that runs, igb_msix_ring (igb_main.c:7153), is a hardirq: it executes with interrupts disabled on that CPU, so it must be short. It does one thing, at line 7160: napi_schedule_irqoff. That marks the ring's NAPI instance as needing service, adds it to this CPU's poll list, raises a software interrupt, and returns. The card's interrupt is left masked. No packet has been looked at.

NAPI, the "new API" that is now twenty years old, is the contract between drivers and the core. Instead of one interrupt per frame, the driver takes one interrupt, then polls the ring in a loop until it is empty or a quota is spent, and only then re-enables the interrupt. Under load the interrupt fires rarely and the CPU spends its time in the poll. Under light load each frame costs an interrupt plus a short poll. The quota per poll call is the NAPI weight, 64 by default.

The softirq: budgets and the poll

A softirq is deferred work that runs with interrupts enabled, either right after the hardirq returns or in the ksoftirqd kernel thread when there is too much of it. Networking receive is NET_RX_SOFTIRQ, and its handler is net_rx_action (net/core/dev.c:7920). It sets two limits at the top: a budget of packets, netdev_budget, default 300 (line 7926), and a time limit, netdev_budget_usecs, default 2,000 microseconds (line 7923). Then it takes NAPI instances off the poll list one at a time and calls napi_poll (line 7958), subtracting what each one processed. When the budget or the time is gone it stops, increments the time squeeze counter (lines 7964 to 7970, visible as the third column of /proc/net/softnet_stat), and re-raises itself so the rest is handled later.

For our ring, napi_poll calls the driver's poll function, igb_poll (igb_main.c:8278), which calls igb_clean_rx_irq with the weight as its budget (line 8300). That function is the loop that turns descriptors into skbs (igb_main.c:9016). Read its head, lines 9035 to 9058: refill the ring if enough slots have been consumed; read the descriptor at next_to_clean; if its length field is zero the card has not written it yet, so stop; otherwise issue a dma_rmb so the CPU does not read the buffer before it has read the length that says the buffer is ready.

The skb is born

Now the skb appears. igb_build_skb (igb_main.c:8664) allocates only the metadata block, through napi_build_skb, and points it at the page fragment the card already filled. The bytes are not copied. This is the general rule for modern drivers: the box was filled by DMA, and the driver only clips a label to it. igb_process_skb_fields (line 9131) fills in the label: skb->protocol from the Ethernet type through eth_type_trans (line 8929), the hardware checksum result, the receive hash, any VLAN tag. Then, at line 9133, the driver hands the skb to the core with napi_gro_receive.

That name is a small lie in 7.2. napi_gro_receive is now an inline wrapper (include/linux/netdevice.h:4311) around gro_receive_skb (net/core/gro.c:638), so when you trace it with bpftrace you probe the second name. What it does is GRO, generic receive offload: it holds back consecutive segments of the same TCP flow and merges them into one larger skb, so the rest of the stack does its work once per merged packet instead of once per wire frame. Merging is bounded at about 64 KB, and the merged skb keeps the original frames as a list of fragments. When a flow's merged skb is complete, or the poll is ending, gro_normal_one (include/net/gro.h:537) adds it to a per-NAPI list, and gro_normal_list (line 519) flushes that list into netif_receive_skb_list_internal after 8 skbs, the gro_normal_batch default.

netif_receive_skb_list_internal (net/core/dev.c:6420) is where the driver's world ends. It checks once whether receive packet steering wants this skb on another CPU (below), and otherwise calls __netif_receive_skb_list, which hands each skb to __netif_receive_skb_core, the loop that chapter 3 is about.

The second door: netif_rx and the backlog

Not every skb arrives from a poll. Sometimes a piece of code already holding a fully formed skb wants the receive path to run on it: the loopback driver, a veth, a tunnel decapsulating an inner packet, a driver too old to have NAPI. For those there is netif_rx, and its variant __netif_rx for callers already in softirq context (net/core/dev.c:5778 and 5746). Both call netif_rx_internal (line 5706), and it calls enqueue_to_backlog (line 5381).

The backlog is a per-CPU queue, softnet_data.input_pkt_queue (include/linux/netdevice.h:3622), with a per-CPU fake NAPI instance attached to it, softnet_data.backlog (line 3624). enqueue_to_backlog checks the queue against netdev_max_backlog, default 1,000 (read at line 5396 as net_hotdata.max_backlog), drops if full, appends the skb (line 5414), and if the queue was empty schedules the fake NAPI (line 5413). From there the backlog is a device like any other: net_rx_action polls it, and its poll function is process_backlog (line 6658), which splices the input queue into a processing queue (line 6704) and calls __netif_receive_skb on each skb (line 6680). So netif_rx is not a way around NAPI. It is NAPI with a software queue in front and no hardware behind.

Keep that shape in mind. When chapter 9 shows a packet entering the far end of a veth, the trace will read __netif_rx, netif_rx_internal, enqueue_to_backlog, then __netif_receive_skb. Every one of those names is on this page.

Which CPU: RSS and RPS

RSS, receive side scaling, is the card's job: it hashes each frame's addresses and ports and picks one of several rings, each with its own interrupt and NAPI instance, so flows spread across CPUs while each flow stays on one. RPS, receive packet steering, is the software copy of the same idea for cards with one ring: in netif_receive_skb_list_internal and in netif_rx_internal, get_rps_cpu (lines 6438 and 5721) may pick another CPU, in which case the skb goes to that CPU's backlog through enqueue_to_backlog and an inter-processor interrupt wakes it. That is the one case where a frame from a real NIC shows enqueue_to_backlog in a trace, and it is a diagnostic worth remembering.

Your NIC is a Wi-Fi card

The mt76 driver behind mt7921e uses NAPI too: mt76_dma_rx_poll (drivers/net/wireless/mediatek/mt76/dma.c:1073) is its poll function. But a Wi-Fi frame is not an Ethernet frame; it goes through the mac80211 stack first, which decrypts it and rewrites the 802.11 header into an Ethernet header. The skb re-enters the core in mt76_rx_complete (mac80211.c:1514), through napi_gro_receive at line 1550 when a NAPI instance is attached and through netif_receive_skb_list at line 1544 when one is not. Same door, longer corridor. Everything from this chapter's third section onward is identical.

Try it

Count which door each device uses, then watch one softirq run as a call tree.

# ten seconds of counting, by device: NAPI door versus netif_rx door
sudo bpftrace -e '
kprobe:gro_receive_skb   { @napi[((struct sk_buff *)arg1)->dev->name] = count(); }
kprobe:netif_rx_internal { @netif_rx[((struct sk_buff *)arg0)->dev->name] = count(); }
interval:s:10 { exit(); }'
# budget exhaustion: third column is time_squeeze, one row per CPU
cat /proc/net/softnet_stat
# ring sizes and queue count; many Wi-Fi drivers answer "Operation not supported"
ethtool -g wlan0; ethtool -l wlan0
# one softirq run as a call tree; stop with Ctrl-C, then restore the nop tracer
cd /sys/kernel/tracing && echo net_rx_action > set_graph_function && echo function_graph > current_tracer && head -80 trace_pipe; echo nop > current_tracer

On this laptop the first command shows wlan0 only under @napi, and lo plus every veth only under @netif_rx. In the function graph, look for __napi_poll calling your driver's poll, and under it the GRO calls, then netif_receive_skb_list_internal appearing once for a batch of frames.

Recap

The card fills pre-posted buffers by DMA and records lengths in ring descriptors. The hardirq only schedules NAPI. net_rx_action polls under a budget of 300 packets and 2 ms, calling each driver's poll with weight 64. The poll builds skbs around the filled buffers without copying and hands them to GRO, which merges same-flow segments and flushes batches into netif_receive_skb_list_internal. Callers that already hold an skb use netif_rx, which queues into the per-CPU backlog and gets polled the same way. RPS uses that same backlog to move frames between CPUs.

New words in this chapter

receive ring, descriptor
Circular array of records, each pointing at a buffer; the card writes frame lengths into them.
DMA
The card writing frame bytes into RAM without the CPU.
page pool
Per-ring recycling allocator of DMA-mapped page fragments for receive buffers.
hardirq
The interrupt handler proper; runs with interrupts off; here it only schedules NAPI.
softirq, NET_RX_SOFTIRQ
Deferred work with interrupts on; the receive softirq's handler is net_rx_action.
NAPI, poll, weight
Interrupt once, then poll the ring up to weight (64) frames per call.
budget, time squeeze
net_rx_action's limits: 300 packets or 2,000 us; exceeding them increments time_squeeze.
GRO
Generic receive offload: merging consecutive same-flow segments into one skb inside the poll.
backlog, softnet_data
Per-CPU queue input_pkt_queue plus a fake NAPI; filled by netif_rx, drained by process_backlog.
netif_rx
The entry for callers already holding an skb; queues into the backlog.
RSS, RPS
Hardware and software spreading of flows across CPUs; RPS uses another CPU's backlog.

Check yourself

1. Predict the value: a burst of 1,000 frames sits in one ring with weight 64 and default budgets. How many times does igb_poll run inside the first net_rx_action, and how many net_rx_action runs does the burst need?

Answer

Five polls in the first run, and four runs in total. net_rx_action subtracts each poll's work from a budget of 300 and stops when the budget is zero or negative (net/core/dev.c:7958 to 7966): 300 minus 64 four times leaves 44, still positive, so a fifth poll runs and takes the budget to minus 20, then the loop breaks. Five polls of 64 is 320 frames per run; 1,000 frames need three full runs (960) plus one short run.

The near miss is "four polls, because four times 64 is 256 and a fifth would exceed 300". The check is on the budget after the poll, so a poll is allowed to overshoot. Each break also increments time_squeeze, so this burst leaves that counter three higher in /proc/net/softnet_stat.

2. A trace of a packet arriving on your wired Ethernet card shows enqueue_to_backlog and process_backlog before __netif_receive_skb. Nothing in the driver calls netif_rx. What happened?

Answer

Receive packet steering moved the frame to another CPU. netif_receive_skb_list_internal asked get_rps_cpu (net/core/dev.c:6438), it chose a different CPU, and the skb was queued into that CPU's input_pkt_queue by enqueue_to_backlog (line 6444); the far CPU's process_backlog then delivered it. Check /sys/class/net/eth0/queues/rx-0/rps_cpus; a non-zero mask confirms it. The pwru CPU column also changes between the two lines.

The wrong answer is "the driver is a legacy driver without NAPI". Those exist, but you would see netif_rx or netif_rx_internal in the trace before enqueue_to_backlog, and the RPS path does not pass through them.

3. Predict the value: you download 1 MB over TCP with a 1,448-byte payload per segment. Roughly how many skbs does the driver's poll hand to the core with GRO on, and how many with GRO off?

Answer

About 720 with GRO off, and at best around 16 with GRO on. One megabyte at 1,448 bytes per segment is about 724 segments, and with GRO off each becomes its own skb and its own trip through the stack. GRO merges up to about 64 KB per skb (gro_receive_skb, net/core/gro.c:638), so 1,048,576 divided by 65,536 is 16 merged skbs at the ideal. Real numbers land in between, because a merge is flushed whenever the poll ends or a segment arrives out of order.

The near miss is counting GRO as a hardware feature you cannot turn off. It is software in the poll loop, toggled with ethtool -K DEV gro off, and turning it on for a veth is how chapter 9 flips that device into a different receive mode.
