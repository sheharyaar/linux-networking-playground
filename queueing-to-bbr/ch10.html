Chapter 10 · before you read

BBR

~6 min before · pp. 58–66 (~1.5 h) · ~23 min after · CC
One flow on a 10 Mbit/s, 40 ms path: the paper's own example (Figs 4, 6 and 7, pp. 62–64) and the bench's defaults. The sender measures those two numbers from its acks, keeps one pipe of about 50 KB in flight, and paces at a multiple of the measured rate.

The question this chapter answers

Every sender in this reading list so far finds its rate by filling the bottleneck until it overflows. The congestion-avoidance chapter's Reno flow peaked at 83 packets, the 33-packet pipe plus the 50-packet queue. On the same bench, Linux's default, CUBIC, kept a median of 43 packets queued and a median RTT of 93 ms on a 41 ms path, for 9.53 Mbit/s (measured for this chapter, 25 s). The queue bought no throughput. It added 52 ms to every packet.

The queueing-maths chapter showed where one sender should sit instead: a window of exactly one pipe, the link full and nothing queued, the operating point where Kleinrock's power peaks. The TCP pacing chapter showed why a loss-driven sender cannot sit there: with nothing queued it hears nothing, and its first signal comes when the link is already overloaded, a late congestion signal. In 1981 Jaffe proved that no distributed algorithm can converge to Kleinrock's point (p. 60).

This paper stops asking the network for a signal and measures the path instead. Seen from TCP, any path is described by two numbers: how fast its slowest link drains, and how long a round trip takes with no queue anywhere. Measure both, one after the other, keep one pipe in flight, and pace at the measured rate. On the spine both numbers are known in advance, so every estimate the sender makes can be checked against the truth.

Words you will meet

BBR
Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time: a congestion control that sets its rate from a measured model of the path instead of from losses (p. 60). In Linux, the tcp_bbr module.
BtlBw (bottleneck bandwidth)
The rate of the path's slowest link, the pipe's "minimum diameter" (p. 58). BBR's estimate is the largest recent delivery rate.
RTprop (round-trip propagation time)
The round-trip time with no queue anywhere, the pipe's "length" (p. 58). BBR's estimate is the smallest recent RTT.
inflight
Data sent but not yet acknowledged (p. 58). The BDP is \(\text{BtlBw} \times \text{RTprop}\), the inflight that exactly fills the pipe.
rate balance and full pipe
The paper's two conditions for the best operating point: packets reach the bottleneck at exactly BtlBw, and inflight equals one BDP. Both must hold at once (p. 60).
delivery rate
Data delivered divided by the time it took, measured over the interval from a packet's send to its ack: \(\Delta\text{delivered}/\Delta t\) (p. 61).
app-limited (application-limited)
The application had nothing to send, so the network was not the limit. Delivery-rate samples taken then are marked (p. 61).
windowed filter (windowed max, windowed min)
The largest, or smallest, sample seen within a recent window of time or of round trips. Older samples fall out of the window.
pacing gain (pacing_gain)
The multiplier on BtlBw that sets the pacing rate: \(\text{pacing rate} = \text{pacing\_gain} \times \text{BtlBw}\). BBR's primary control (pp. 61–62).
cwnd gain (cwnd_gain)
The multiplier on the BDP that caps inflight: \(\text{cwnd} = \text{cwnd\_gain} \times \text{BDP}\). BBR's secondary control (p. 61).
BBR states (Startup, Drain, ProbeBW, ProbeRTT)
Four modes, each a table of fixed gains with an exit rule (p. 62): find the rate, empty the queue that finding it built, cycle the gain around the rate, and briefly cut inflight to re-measure RTprop (pp. 62–64).

Chapter 10 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. Why can a sender not measure RTprop and BtlBw at the same moment, and what does BBR do about it? pp. 61–64

Answer

Because each is visible on one side of the BDP only. Below one BDP nothing queues, so the RTT shows RTprop, but delivery shows only what was sent; above it delivery shows BtlBw, but the queue inflates every RTT: the "uncertainty principle" (p. 61). So BBR measures in turns, the 1.25 phase pushing inflight past the BDP and ProbeRTT cutting it to four packets (pp. 62–64). Near miss: averaging the RTT, which any queue biases upward.

2. If BBR does not use loss as its signal, what sets its sending rate, and what is cwnd still for? pp. 61–62, 66

Answer

The pacing rate, pacing_gain times BtlBw, with the gain chosen by the state (Fig. 3, p. 62); every packet leaves on a timer. cwnd is a cap at twice the BDP, so that BBR keeps sending when acks are delayed by up to one RTT (p. 66) and an overestimated BtlBw cannot fill the buffer (Fig. 5, p. 63). Near miss: "cwnd sets the rate"; on the spine cwnd sits near 74 while inflight is about 34.

3. Why does ProbeBW cycle 1.25, 0.75 and six 1s, and what does each phase do to the queue? pp. 62, 66

Answer

The 1.25 phase probes: for one RTprop it sends 1.25 BDP while the link forwards one, so with no new bandwidth it queues a quarter BDP, 8.5 segments and 10 ms on the spine, Fig. 4's RTT bump (p. 62); with new bandwidth the max filter takes the higher rate. The 0.75 phase removes the quarter BDP, the six 1s cruise, and the mean gain is 1. The 1.25 came from experience: smaller peaks left cellular flows stuck (p. 66).

The chapter on one screen

The paper blames bufferbloat and low throughput on reading loss as congestion (p. 58). Seen from TCP, any path is one link with two constraints, RTprop and BtlBw (pp. 58–60). Loss-based control operates where Fig. 1's bandwidth-limited region ends, at one BDP plus the buffer; Kleinrock's optimum is where it starts, at one BDP. Jaffe's impossibility rests on measurement ambiguity, which goes away if the two are measured in turn (p. 60).

The optimum needs rate balance and a full pipe at once (p. 60). RTT samples can only overestimate RTprop and delivery-rate samples underestimate BtlBw, so a windowed min and a windowed max estimate them, app-limited samples kept out; the two are never visible together (p. 61).

On each ack BBR updates the filters (Fig. 2); on each send it paces at pacing_gain times BtlBw under a cap of cwnd_gain times the BDP (Fig. 3, pp. 61–62). ProbeBW cycles the gain through 1.25, 0.75 and six 1s, finding new bandwidth fast and dropping an overestimate slowly (Figs 4–5, pp. 62–63). Startup doubles the rate each round, Drain empties the queue that built, and ProbeRTT cuts inflight to four packets every few seconds, which synchronizes flows around an empty queue (pp. 63–64).

On Google's B4 network BBR's throughput is 2 to 25 times CUBIC's (p. 64); it holds the link rate up to 5% random loss and cut YouTube's median RTT by 53% (p. 65). The last page explains the 1.25 peak and the cwnd gain of 2 (p. 66).

Two numbers and the point between them

From TCP's side, any path behaves like one link with two numbers, RTprop and BtlBw (p. 58). Below one BDP in flight the RTT stays at RTprop and delivery grows with inflight; above it delivery is stuck at BtlBw and every extra packet queues; past the BDP plus the buffer, packets drop (Fig. 1, pp. 59–60). Loss-based control operates at that far end. The best point is the near end, inflight equal to one BDP (p. 59).

Put the spine in. BtlBw is 10 Mbit/s on the wire, 9.56 Mbit/s of payload (1448 of every 1514 bytes): 825.6 segments per second. RTprop for a full-size packet is the 40 ms path plus its 1.21 ms frame time, 41.2 ms. The BDP is \(825.6 \times 0.0412 = 34.0\) segments, 49 KB. So the RTT is flat at 41.2 ms up to 34 segments in flight, then rises 1.21 ms per segment to 101.7 ms at 84, where the 50-packet queue is full (Diagram 10.2).

That near end is the queueing-maths chapter's operating point, where a Reno flow with its window held fixed reached peak power at 36 packets. The paper credits Kleinrock (1979) with the point, and Jaffe (1981) with the proof that no distributed algorithm can converge to it: no single measurement tells a longer path from a slower link or another flow's queue (p. 60).

Two conditions define that point, and each alone fails (p. 60). With rate balance only, a 10-packet initial window sent into a 5-packet BDP at exactly the bottleneck rate leaves 5 packets queued for good, the queue-delay chapter's standing queue. With a full pipe only, one BDP sent as two half-BDP bursts keeps the link busy with a queue that averages a quarter BDP, 8.5 segments and 10 ms on the spine.

Derivation · the quarter-BDP queue of half-BDP bursts

A burst of \(\text{BDP}/2\) arrives at once every \(\text{RTprop}/2\). The bottleneck drains it at BtlBw, which takes \((\text{BDP}/2)/\text{BtlBw} = \text{RTprop}/2\), so the queue falls linearly from \(\text{BDP}/2\) to zero just as the next burst lands. The link never idles, and the queue is a sawtooth between \(\text{BDP}/2\) and 0 whose mean is \(\text{BDP}/4\). On the spine: \(34/4 = 8.5\) segments, \(8.5 \times 1.21 = 10.3\) ms.

Each estimate errs one way

An RTT sample can only be too high and a delivery-rate sample can only be too low, so BBR estimates RTprop with a windowed min of RTT and BtlBw with a windowed max of delivery rate (pp. 60–61). The paper writes \(\text{RTT}_t = \text{RTprop}_t + \eta_t\) with \(\eta \ge 0\): queues, delayed acks and ack aggregation only add time. For the rate, the data delivered is exact, so the error is all in \(\Delta t\), which can only be too long (p. 61).

Fig. 2 (p. 60) is the whole sampler. A packet records, when sent, the delivered count and the time of the latest ack; at its ack, the sample is the data delivered since over the time since. On the spine a packet's ack returns after the 34 segments ahead of it are delivered, 41.2 ms later: 825 segments per second. If the application paused, the interval holds idle time and the sample is low, so Fig. 2's if keeps app-limited samples out unless they beat the max (p. 61).

The paper's windows are "tens of seconds to minutes" for RTprop and six to ten RTTs for BtlBw (p. 61); Linux uses 10 s and 10 round trips (net/ipv4/tcp_bbr.c:134–136). Its round trips are packet-timed, ending when a packet sent after the previous round ended is acked (bbr_update_bw(), line 762), so the bandwidth window stretches when the RTT grows. The max is Kathleen Nichols' three-sample filter (lib/win_minmax.c:67); the min is one value and a timestamp (line 942).

Since the paper. The Linux sampler moved in v7.0: Eric Dumazet dissolved net/ipv4/tcp_rate.c in January 2026 (f10ab9d3a7ea, 670ade3bfae6, b814bdcecd79, bc1f0b1c98f5). tcp_rate_skb_sent() (tcp_output.c:1473) stamps each skb with Fig. 3's delivered, delivered_time and app_limited; tcp_rate_skb_delivered() (tcp_input.c:1808) picks the latest skb an ack covers; tcp_rate_gen() (line 1724) divides by the longer of the send and ack phases (line 1767), a guard against compressed acks that Fig. 2 lacks. tcp_rate_check_app_limited() (tcp.c:1099) is Fig. 3's app_limited_until = inflight. The IETF draft of 6 July 2026 specifies the same sampler (draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-06, §4.1.1.2.4).

A rate from the model, a cap from cwnd

BBR's primary control is the pacing rate, \(\text{pacing\_gain} \times \text{BtlBw}\); cwnd, at \(\text{cwnd\_gain} \times \text{BDP}\), only bounds inflight (pp. 61–62). Fig. 3's send() stops at the cap, and otherwise ships one packet and sets the next send time to now plus its size over the paced rate (p. 62). At gain 1 on the spine that is \(1448 \times 8 / (9.56\ \text{Mbit/s}) = 1.21\) ms, the bottleneck's own frame time: rate balance by construction.

This is the small-queues chapter's pacing rate, computed another way. Reno and CUBIC derive it from the window; BBR writes sk_pacing_rate from the model in bbr_set_pacing_rate() (tcp_bbr.c:287), bandwidth times MSS times gain, less 1% "to reduce queue at bottleneck" (line 148). The departure-times chapter's machinery enforces it: tcp_update_skb_after_send() (tcp_output.c:1446) moves the departure clock on by each skb's length over that rate, and fq holds each skb until its stamp. Without fq TCP's own timer does (tcp_pacing_check(), line 2815), because bbr_init() sets SK_PACING_NEEDED (tcp_bbr.c:1079).

That chapter showed why paced Reno lost: pacing removed the early queue that was its only signal. BBR's signal is the model, refreshed by every ack, queue or none. Pacing pays off once the control signal is a model.

The cap is 2 BDP because of delayed and bunched acks (p. 66): pacing at BtlBw while acks are held back for up to one RTT puts \(\text{BDP} + \text{BtlBw} \times \text{RTT} = 2\ \text{BDP}\) in flight, and a one-BDP limit stalled cellular and Wi-Fi flows. On the spine that is 68 segments; Linux adds three two-segment skbs for TSO, plus 2 in the 1.25 phase (bbr_quantization_budget(), line 396): 74, or 76. It binds only when BtlBw is overestimated, as after Fig. 5's drop (p. 63).

A quarter up, a quarter down

In ProbeBW, BBR spends one RTprop at pacing_gain 1.25, the next at 0.75, and six at 1 (Fig. 4, p. 62). The 1.25 phase finds bandwidth if there is more; if not, the extra quarter queues and the 0.75 phase drains it. The mean gain is 1, so no standing queue is left (p. 62).

On the spine, 1.25 for one RTprop sends 42.5 segments while the bottleneck forwards 34, so 8.5 queue and the RTT rises by \(8.5 \times 1.21 = 10.3\) ms, to about 51 ms. Fig. 4, 700 ms of this same flow, shows the RTT stepping from 42.5 to about 51 ms one RTT after the gain changes: the gain applies when data is sent, and the ack reports it a round trip later (p. 62).

Up is fast and down is slow (Fig. 5, p. 63). Double the bottleneck to 20 Mbit/s and each 1.25 phase delivers more, which the max filter takes at once: in the model the estimate goes 9.56, 11.9, 14.7, 18.2 Mbit/s in three cycles of \(8 \times 41\) ms (the figure: "1.95x (=1.25³) in 3 cycles"). Halve it, and the max filter learns nothing until its old samples leave the 10-round window, while cwnd_gain holds inflight at twice the old BDP, 134 segments, and the RTT at 164 ms. With a 1,000-packet buffer the model's stale estimate times out after 1.7 s; the figure shows about 1.8 s.

Derivation · the cycle's arithmetic

Queue added by one RTprop at gain \(g\) with no new bandwidth: the sender puts in \(g \cdot \text{BDP}\) while the link takes out one BDP, so \((g-1)\,\text{BDP}\), a quarter BDP at 1.25. The 0.75 phase removes \((1 - 0.75)\,\text{BDP}\), the same amount. Mean gain: \((1.25 + 0.75 + 6 \times 1)/8 = 1\). Growth with new bandwidth: each cycle multiplies the estimate by up to 1.25, so doubling needs \(\ln 2/\ln 1.25 = 3.1\) cycles; \(1.25^3 = 1.95\). Forgetting after a drop: 10 rounds at the inflated RTT, \(10 \times 0.164 = 1.6\) s, plus the part of a round in which the drop lands.

Linux adds rules to Fig. 4 (bbr_is_next_cycle_phase(), tcp_bbr.c:555): 1.25 lasts until inflight reaches 1.25 BDP or a loss appears, 0.75 ends once inflight is one BDP, and each flow starts at a random phase, never 0.75 (line 623), so flows do not probe in step.

Startup, Drain and ProbeRTT

RTprop is visible only to the left of the BDP and BtlBw only to the right, so "whenever one can be measured, the other cannot" (p. 61). The conclusion's way around Jaffe is to estimate the two in turn (p. 66), and the four states are the schedule (pp. 62–64).

Startup doubles the sending rate every round with gain \(2/\ln 2 = 2.885\), a binary search that finds BtlBw in \(\log_2 \text{BDP}\) rounds and builds up to 2 BDP of queue (p. 63). Linux uses \(739/256\) (line 155) and leaves after three rounds of under 25% growth (bbr_check_full_bw_reached(), line 874). On the spine those rounds push toward 68 segments of queue against a 50-packet buffer, so the lab's model drops 105 packets; Fig. 6, with a 250 ms buffer, peaks near 120 ms, three RTprops. Drain paces at the inverse, 0.347 (Linux \(88/256\), line 159), until inflight is one BDP (bbr_check_drain(), line 894): a full 50-packet queue empties in \(50 / ((1 - 0.344) \times 825.6) = 92\) ms.

ProbeRTT handles the other side. If RTprop has not been lowered for 10 s, cwnd drops to 4 packets for at least 200 ms and one round (bbr_update_min_rtt(), lines 942–985; "many seconds", pp. 63–64). That costs about 2%, 0.24 s at \(4 \times 1448 \times 8 / 0.041\ \text{s} = 1.1\) Mbit/s every 10 s, and buys an empty queue, in which the other flows see a lower RTT and their estimates expire together: "BBR synchronizes flows around the desirable event of an empty bottleneck queue" (p. 64). Loss-based flows synchronize around overflow, the TCP pacing chapter's synchronized drops.

Since the paper. Upstream tcp_bbr.c in v7.2 still runs this algorithm: its header comment (lines 2–59) gives the same model and four states and cites the Queue version of this article; recent commits change interfaces only (d1e59a469737, v7.1). The IETF draft of 6 July 2026 specifies a third version, out of tree (Google's bbr-v3-2026-09-16-01 branch). It keeps the 10 s RTprop window, but enters ProbeRTT at most every 5 s and only halves inflight (§2.16, §5.3.4.2). The next chapter, on where this model breaks, reads why.

Loss stops being the signal

BBR's rate comes from the model, so a loss changes nothing unless losses keep the max filter from seeing the true BtlBw. On a 100 Mbit/s, 100 ms link with random loss, BBR stays at the limit, the link rate times \((1 - \text{loss})\), up to 5% loss and near it up to 15%, while CUBIC falls tenfold at 0.1% (Fig. 10, p. 65). BBR's loss tolerance is a configuration choice, its peak gain; CUBIC's is built into the algorithm (p. 65).

This reverses the congestion-avoidance chapter's founding assumption: Jacobson read a timeout as a full queue because damage loss was under 1% (his p. 163), which this paper says held "because of technology limitations" (p. 58). The Mathis formula prices it: a loss-driven flow fills a pipe of \(W\) packets only if \(C/\sqrt p \ge W\), so \(p \le 1.5/W^2\), about one loss per \(\text{BDP}^2\) packets (p. 64). On the spine that is one in 775, as the queueing-maths chapter computed; on B4's long, fast paths it is out of reach, hence 2 to 25 times CUBIC's throughput.

The paper's slip. Page 64's example, "one loss per 30 million packets for a 10Gbps/100ms path", is too frequent by more than a hundred times. With 1500-byte packets that path holds \(10^{10} \times 0.1 / (8 \times 1500) = 83{,}333\) packets, so \(1/\text{BDP}^2\) is one loss per \(6.9 \times 10^9\), or \(4.6 \times 10^9\) with Mathis's \(C^2 = 1.5\); RFC 3649 (§1) gives "one congestion event every 5,000,000,000 packets" for the same path. No packet size rescues it: 30 million needs a BDP of 5,477 packets of 22.8 KB. The figure fits a path ten times slower, 1 Gbit/s at 100 ms: 8,333 packets, one loss per 46 to 69 million.

On the bench the contrast is Fig. 7's (p. 64). CUBIC held a median of 42 of the 50 packets queued and a median smoothed RTT of 91 ms; the lab's model of BBR keeps the queue between 0 and 10 packets and the RTT between 41 and 51 ms.

Two problems stay open on the last page (p. 66): token-bucket policers, which made the gain cycle lose packets continuously and led to policer detection in the code, and loss-based competitors in buffers of several BDPs. Both are the next chapter's subject.

Real systems

Meet the system · Linux tcp_bbr, read through ss -ti

What it is. Linux's BBR module, net/ipv4/tcp_bbr.c, 1,200 lines in v7.2, by four of the paper's authors. A socket picks it with TCP_CONGESTION or the net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control sysctl, and ss -ti prints its model as bbr:(bw:…,mrtt:…,pacing_gain:…,cwnd_gain:…).

Why it exists. Google built it for its B4 backbone and its web and video servers (pp. 64–65), and merged it in Linux 4.9 in 2016 (0f8782ea1497).

The parts you need.

  • struct bbr (line 90): bw (a windowed max), min_rtt_us, mode, the two gains and cycle_idx.
  • bbr_main() (line 1028), the hook tcp_ack() calls after building the rate sample (tcp_input.c:4439–4440): update the model, set the pacing rate, set cwnd.
  • The rate sampler, which fills struct rate_sample (include/net/tcp.h:1306).
  • bbr_get_info() (line 1108), which copies bw, min_rtt and the gains into struct tcp_bbr_info (include/uapi/linux/inet_diag.h:234) for ss and getsockopt(TCP_CC_INFO).

Where this chapter lives inside it. The two numbers are bw:, BtlBw in payload bits per second, and mrtt:, RTprop in ms; bbr_bdp() (line 361) multiplies them. The one-sided filters are minmax_running_max() over 10 rounds behind the app-limited gate (lines 799–801) and a 10 s stamp on min_rtt_us (line 949), so mrtt can differ from ss's minrtt, TCP's own 300 s minimum. The rate and the cap are pacing_rate, \(0.99 \times \text{pacing\_gain} \times \text{bw}\), and cwnd, near \(2 \times \text{BDP} + 6\). The state is the gain pair (bbr_update_gains(), line 988): 2.88672 and 2.88672 Startup, 0.34375 and 2.88672 Drain, 1.25, 0.75 or 1 with 2 ProbeBW, 1 and 1 ProbeRTT. Loss shows in retrans:; bw: does not move with it.

Hint 2 · mechanism

The payload rate is \(10 \times 1448/1514\) Mbit/s, and one frame takes 1.21 ms. A gain \(g\) held for one RTprop adds \((g-1)\,\text{BDP}\) of queue. Linux's cwnd is \(2 \times \text{BDP}\) plus three two-segment skbs, rounded to even, plus 2 in the 1.25 phase. ProbeRTT keeps 4 segments in flight per RTprop for about 0.24 s.

Hint 3 · nearly there

(f): \(10^{10} \times 0.1 / (8 \times 1500) = 83{,}333\) packets in flight. Square it. Then ask which path speed would give 30 million.

Solution 
(a) BtlBw  = 10e6 x 1448/1514 / (1448 x 8) = 825.6 segments/s (9.56 Mbit/s of payload)
    RTprop = 40 + 1.21 = 41.2 ms
    BDP    = 825.6 x 0.0412 = 34.0 segments = 49.3 KB
(b) queue  = 0.25 x 34.0 = 8.5 segments;  RTT = 41.2 + 8.5 x 1.21 = 51.5 ms
(c) cwnd   = 2 x 34 + 3 x 2 = 74, already even;  76 in the 1.25 phase
(d) ProbeRTT: 4 x 1448 x 8 / 0.0412 = 1.12 Mbit/s for about 0.24 s
    lost per 10 s: 0.24 x (9.56 - 1.12) = 2.0 Mbit of 95.6 = 2.1%
(e) 1 ProbeBW, the 0.75 slot   2 Startup (bw still climbing; mrtt is the SYN's 40.1)
    3 ProbeRTT                  4 Drain
(f) 83,333^2 = 6.9e9: one loss per 6.9 billion packets (4.6 billion with C^2 = 1.5)
    30 million = BDP^2 needs BDP = 5,477 packets: 657 Mbit/s at 100 ms, so about 1 Gbit/s
    (1 Gbit/s, 100 ms: 8,333 packets, one loss per 46 to 69 million)

What this shows. Every number BBR uses comes from two measurements and a few constants: the BDP from the two numbers (p. 60), the probe queue from the gain (p. 62), the cap from cwnd_gain (p. 66), the ProbeRTT cost from four packets and 200 ms (pp. 63–64). The state is readable from the gains alone, as the systems card says.

Common wrong turn. Reading bw:9.56Mbps as an underestimate of a 10 Mbit/s link. BBR counts delivered segments of payload (bbr_get_info() multiplies by the MSS), and 1448 of every 1514 bytes are payload. A BDP computed from the wire rate in frames, 33, and one computed in segments, 34.5 at 40 ms, are the same pipe.

Hint 1 · direction

Fig. 2, line by line: the RTT, the min filter, the delivered count, the delivery rate, the max filter. The max filter's window is counted in round trips, not seconds (the learning on one-sided estimates).

Hint 2 · mechanism

Keep s.samples as a dict from round number to the best rate seen in that round. A round starts when the acked packet's p['delivered'] has reached s.next_round; then set s.next_round = s.delivered. Replace RTprop when a sample is lower or the stamp is 10 s old. The pacer: next_send = max(now, next_send) + 1 / pacing_rate.

Hint 3 · nearly there

The rate sample is (s.delivered - p['delivered']) / (now - p['delivered_time']). Round the RTT to microseconds, round(now - p['sendtime'], 6), as Linux does: otherwise floating-point noise keeps lowering RTprop by a few attoseconds, the estimate never expires, and ProbeRTT never runs.

Solution 
    def btlbw(s):
        return max([r for k, r in s.samples.items() if k > s.round - 10] or [0.0])

    def update_model(s, now, p):
        s.last_rtt = rtt = round(now - p['sendtime'], 6)
        expired = now - s.rtprop_stamp > 10.0
        if rtt < s.rtprop or expired:                         # windowed min, 10 s
            s.rtprop, s.rtprop_stamp = rtt, now
        s.delivered += 1; s.delivered_time = now
        s.last_rate = rate = (s.delivered - p['delivered']) / (now - p['delivered_time'])
        round_start = p['delivered'] >= s.next_round
        if round_start:
            s.round += 1; s.next_round = s.delivered
        s.samples[s.round] = max(rate, s.samples.get(s.round, 0.0))   # windowed max, 10 rounds
        s.samples = {k: r for k, r in s.samples.items() if k > s.round - 10}
        return round_start, expired

    def advance_cycle(s, now):
        if now - s.cycle_stamp > s.rtprop:
            s.idx, s.cycle_stamp = (s.idx + 1) % 8, now

    def on_send(s, now):
        s.inflight += 1
        s.next_send = max(now, s.next_send) + 1 / s.pacing_rate
        return {'sendtime': now, 'delivered': s.delivered, 'delivered_time': s.delivered_time}

Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 plus a gain table are enough to keep one BDP in flight on a path the sender was never told about: 34 segments, a queue that only rises to 10 in the 1.25 slot, and a ProbeRTT 10 s after the handshake set RTprop (pp. 60–64). Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: a BBR flow crosses a 100 Mbit/s path whose RTprop is 20 ms, with 1448-byte segments in 1514-byte frames. What are the BDP, the queue and RTT bump at the end of a 1.25 phase, and the pacing_rate ss prints then?

Answer

165 segments; 41 segments and 5 ms; 118 Mbit/s. The payload rate is 95.6 Mbit/s, 8,256 segments per second, times 0.02 s. A quarter BDP is 41 segments of 0.121 ms, 5 ms, always a quarter of RTprop (p. 62). pacing_rate is \(0.99 \times 1.25 \times 95.6 = 118.3\) Mbit/s (tcp_bbr.c:245). Near miss: 125, which forgets the payload share and the 1% margin.

2. Here is a symptom: a long BBR upload dips for a quarter of a second every 10 s, and then ss shows pacing_gain:1,cwnd_gain:1 and cwnd:4. A colleague files a bug about timeouts. What is happening?

Answer

ProbeRTT, by design. RTprop had not been lowered for 10 s, so BBR holds cwnd at 4 for at least 200 ms and one round, to drain the queue and measure RTprop again (bbr_update_min_rtt(), tcp_bbr.c:942; pp. 63–64). It costs about 2%. Near miss: a timeout, which would show ca_state loss, a backoff and a doubled rto.

3. Here is a symptom: a BBR server's root qdisc is fq_codel. tc -s shows nothing throttled, yet a capture shows packets 1.21 ms apart on a 10 Mbit/s path, and 0.98 ms apart in some slots. Who paces?

Answer

TCP itself. bbr_init() sets SK_PACING_NEEDED (tcp_bbr.c:1079), so without fq the stack holds each skb until its departure time on a per-socket hrtimer (tcp_pacing_check(), tcp_output.c:2815), which the header comment says "may use more resources" (lines 56–59). The 0.98 ms slots are the 1.25 gain. Near miss: the ack clock, which cannot tighten the spacing by itself.

4. Here is a symptom: an RPC service sends a 20 KB reply every 500 ms on a BBR connection that earlier carried a bulk upload. ss shows app_limited and a low delivery_rate, yet bw: still shows the upload's rate minutes later. Is the filter broken?

Answer

No. App-limited samples stay out of the max filter unless they beat it (Fig. 2's if, p. 61; tcp_bbr.c:799), and the window moves only when a sample goes in, so the old maximum stays: "proceed with the best network rate no matter how long" (lines 788–798). Near miss: "ten rounds passed, so it expired"; no sample entered the filter to push it out.

5. Choose and defend: one long upload shares a 10 Mbit/s, 40 ms uplink with a 50-packet drop-tail buffer and a video call, and you control only the sender. CUBIC or BBR?

Answer

BBR. On the bench CUBIC held a median of 42 packets queued, about 50 ms on every packet of the call; BBR keeps one BDP in flight and the queue near empty at about the same goodput (Fig. 7, p. 64; the lab's model). The price: Startup overflows the buffer once, ProbeRTT costs 2%, and sharing with loss-based senders is the next chapter's question. Controlling the router, you could put fq_codel there instead.

Recap

BBR models the path with two numbers, BtlBw and RTprop, each kept by a windowed filter that matches the one way its samples can err. It paces at pacing_gain times BtlBw and caps inflight at twice the BDP, so on the spine it keeps about 34 segments in flight and little queued, where loss-based control fills the buffer. Four states schedule the measuring: Startup and Drain once, then ProbeBW's quarter-up, quarter-down cycle, with ProbeRTT every 10 s.
