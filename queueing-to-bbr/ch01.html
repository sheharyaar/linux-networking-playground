Chapter 1 · before you read

Congestion avoidance and control

~5 min before · pp. 158–173 (~1.5 h) · ~23 min after · CC
The 230.4 kbit/s microwave link between the routers csam and cartan, with its 50-packet queue (Fig. 7, p. 166): first one connection starts across it, then four share it.

The question this chapter answers

In October 1986 the link from Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory to UC Berkeley, 400 yards and three router hops long, fell from 32 kbit/s to 40 bit/s (p. 158). Nothing was broken. Every host was sending, every router was forwarding, and almost every packet on the wire was a copy of one that was already on its way. A drop by a factor of a thousand, with all the hardware working, is the failure this paper explains and fixes.

Picture the spine of this chapter: a 230.4 kbit/s link between two routers, csam and cartan, with room for 50 packets in its queue. Four hosts each want to push a megabyte across it. Nobody tells a sender how fast the link is, how full the queue is, or how many others are sending. A TCP sender sees only two things: acks coming back, and acks not coming back. The question is how a sender can find a good rate from those two signals alone, and why the 1986 TCP found a terrible one.

Jacobson's answer is a handful of rules, most of them three lines of code, that still run in your kernel. This chapter is about why each rule has the shape it has.

Words you will meet

congestion collapse
Throughput falls by orders of magnitude because the network is busy carrying retransmissions nobody needed. The 1986 event above.
conservation of packets
With a full window in flight, a new packet enters the network only when an old one leaves.
bottleneck
The slowest link on the path. Its packet time, written \(P_b\), is the smallest gap it can leave between two packets.
ack clock
Acknowledgements (acks) come back spaced like the bottleneck spaced the data, so a sender that sends only when an ack arrives sends at the bottleneck's rate. The paper says "self-clocking".
congestion window (cwnd)
A per-connection limit kept by the sender. It may have at most the smaller of cwnd and the receiver's advertised window unacknowledged.
slow start
Start cwnd at one packet and add one for every ack. The window doubles every round-trip time (RTT).
ssthresh
The slow-start threshold: the window size at which slow start hands over to congestion avoidance.
retransmit timeout (RTO)
How long the sender waits for an ack before it sends the packet again.
mean deviation
The average size of the error between a new RTT sample and the running average. A cheap stand-in for the standard deviation.
exponential backoff
Double the RTO each time the same packet has to be sent again.
congestion avoidance (AIMD)
The steady-state window policy: on congestion, multiply the window by a factor below one (multiplicative decrease); otherwise add a small constant each RTT (additive increase).
pipe size (bandwidth-delay product, BDP)
Bottleneck rate times round-trip time: the amount of data in flight that exactly fills the path, and the largest window that builds no queue.

Chapter 1 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. If a sender transmits only when an ack arrives, why does it end up sending at exactly the bottleneck's rate, and why can that same rule not start a connection? pp. 158–159

Answer

Because the bottleneck sets the spacing and nothing after it undoes it. Packets leave the slow link one \(P_b\) apart, the faster networks beyond keep that gap, and the acks carry it back (Fig. 1, p. 159). One packet per ack is one packet per \(P_b\). At the start there are no acks: data makes acks and acks release data (p. 159), so the 1986 TCP sent a whole window at LAN speed, the burst slow start replaces. Near miss: the clock does not measure the rate. It copies it, one ack at a time.

2. Why must the window shrink by multiplication but grow only by addition? pp. 163–165

Answer

Because a congested queue grows geometrically and a free network says nothing. The queue model \(L_i = N + \gamma L_{i-1}\) gives \(L_n = \gamma^n L_0\) under congestion (footnote 8, p. 164), so only a multiplicative cut keeps up. For growth there is no signal, so the sender must probe, and an overestimate is costly: it is "easy to drive the net into saturation but hard for the net to recover" (p. 164). Any multiplicative increase overshoots (p. 165), so the window grows one packet per RTT, 1/cwnd per ack (footnote 10).

3. Why does a retransmit timer set to twice the average RTT fail when the network is busy, and what does the paper add to fix it? pp. 160–162, 169–171

Answer

Because the RTT's spread grows with load as fast as its average. Both grow like \(1/(1-\rho)\), and RFC 793's \(\beta = 2\) copes only up to about 30% load (p. 161). Above that, delayed packets get resent, adding load exactly when the network is struggling. The fix estimates the spread as the mean deviation \(D\) and sets \(\text{rto} = A + 2D\) (Appendix A, pp. 169–171). A packet resent again waits twice as long each time: exponential backoff (p. 162).

The chapter on one screen

The paper starts from the 1986 collapse and one principle, conservation of packets: in equilibrium, a packet enters only when another leaves (p. 158). It can fail three ways: the connection never reaches equilibrium, a sender injects early, or the path cannot hold the equilibrium. Each main section fixes one.

Reaching equilibrium. Acks arrive spaced by the bottleneck, so they can clock the sender (Fig. 1, p. 159), but a new connection has none. Slow start opens cwnd from one packet, adding one per ack: the window doubles each RTT and reaches \(W\) in \(R\log_2 W\), with bursts that need \(W/2\) packets of buffer (Fig. 2, p. 160).

Staying in equilibrium. Early injection means a bad retransmit timer. RTT and its spread grow with load, so RFC 793's \(\text{rto} = 2R\) fails above about 30% load (p. 161). The fix measures the spread as a mean deviation and sets \(\text{rto} = A + 2D\) (Appendix A); repeated retries back off exponentially (p. 162).

Adapting to the path. Damage loss is well under 1%, so a timeout means congestion (p. 163). Congested queues grow geometrically, so windows shrink by multiplication; the network is silent about spare capacity, so they grow slowly, by addition (pp. 164–165). Appendix B combines both: slow start up to ssthresh, then 1/cwnd per ack; on a timeout, ssthresh is half the window and cwnd returns to one (pp. 171–172).

Two warnings close the paper: a window larger than the pipe leaves a queue that never drains (footnote 9, p. 164), and only routers, which see every flow, can make sharing fair (pp. 165–166). On the csam–cartan test, retransmissions fall from 4,000 of 11,000 packets to 89 of 8,281 (Figs 8–9).

The ack clock

A sender that transmits one packet per returning ack sends at the bottleneck's rate without knowing what that rate is. The bottleneck stretches packets out in time, and the stretch is carried back to the sender by the acks (Fig. 1, p. 159).

Put numbers on the spine. A 512-byte data packet plus 40 bytes of TCP and IP headers is 552 bytes, so the csam–cartan link needs \(552 \times 8 / 230{,}400 = 19.2\) ms to send one. That is \(P_b\). The four hosts sit on 10 Mbit/s Ethernets, where the same packet takes under half a millisecond, so a window sent back to back arrives at csam as a clump and leaves it one packet every 19.2 ms. The acks return 19.2 ms apart, and a sender that waits for them releases one packet every 19.2 ms, which is about 26 KB/s of payload. The paper measures 25 KB/s of usable data on this link (Fig. 8 caption, p. 167).

On the bench in the hands-on section, a 1514-byte frame takes 1.21 ms at 10 Mbit/s, so the sender settles to 825 packets per second.

The clock has two weak points, and the paper names both. It cannot start itself, which is the next learning. And it trusts the ack spacing. A receiver that acks only every few packets turns the clock into a burst generator: the Fig. 9 caption (p. 168) blames receivers that held their ack for 5–7 packets for three times the loss rate (1.8% against 0.5%). Modern Wi-Fi and receive-side batching bunch acks in the same way. Spacing packets with a timer instead of with acks is the fix later chapters build.

What slow start costs

Slow start reaches a window of \(W\) packets in \(R\log_2 W\), where \(R\) is the RTT. It never sends faster than twice the path's maximum rate, but each round needs up to \(W/2\) packets of buffer at the bottleneck (p. 160).

The cost comes from Fig. 2. Each ack releases two packets, one to replace the packet that left and one because the window grew. On a fast LAN those two arrive at the bottleneck together, and the bottleneck drains only one per \(P_b\). So every ack in a round adds one packet to the queue. A round that ends with window \(W\) was clocked out by \(W/2\) acks, so it leaves \(W/2\) packets waiting.

Derivation · the time and the buffer

Start at cwnd = 1. In each RTT every packet in flight is acked and every ack adds one, so the window doubles: after \(k\) rounds it is \(2^k\). It reaches \(W\) when \(k = \log_2 W\), which takes \(R\log_2 W\).

In the round that grows the window from \(W/2\) to \(W\), the \(W/2\) acks arrive one \(P_b\) apart, because the previous round left the bottleneck that way. Each releases two packets at LAN speed, and the bottleneck forwards one per \(P_b\). The queue therefore grows by one per ack and peaks at \(W/2\) at the end of the round. Sending two packets per \(P_b\) is also where "at most twice the path rate" comes from.

On the spine, each Fig. 7 connection has a 16 KB window, 32 packets of 512 bytes: 5 RTTs to open, and 16 of csam's 50 queue slots for the last burst. Three bursting at once would not fit; the paper started the transfers 3 s apart (p. 166).

The bench run shows the cost in full. The pipe is about 33 packets and the queue holds 50, so a window of about 83 packets fills both. Slow start doubled past 64, and the next round's burst needed more than 50 packets of queue. The loss signal then took one more full-queue RTT, about 100 ms, to reach the sender. By then cwnd had reached 166, and the first recovery had to resend 86 packets. The plot in the learning on halving shows this spike at \(t \approx 0.3\) s.

Since the paper. The initial window is 10 packets (RFC 6928; TCP_INIT_CWND is 10 in include/net/tcp.h:269). That saves \(\log_2 10 \approx 3.3\) rounds: reaching a 432-packet window (100 Mbit/s, 50 ms) takes 5.4 RTTs instead of 8.75. Linux's default, CUBIC, also leaves slow start early when it sees the RTT rise (HyStart, net/ipv4/tcp_cubic.c:55). Plain Reno, which the bench uses, has no such exit, which is why it overshoots.

A timer that measures spread

A retransmit timer must cover the spread of the RTT as well as its average. Under load the spread grows as fast as the average, so a fixed multiple of the average is wrong exactly when it matters (pp. 160–161).

The argument uses the load \(\rho\), the ratio of average arrival rate to average departure rate at the bottleneck. RTT and its variation both grow like \(1/(1-\rho)\), and RFC 793's \(\text{rto} = 2R\) copes only up to about 30% load (p. 161), a figure the paper asserts without derivation. The plot shows the same shape from a simple model.

Derivation · the three curves

Let the RTT be \(b + Q\), where \(b = 100\) ms is fixed and the queueing delay \(Q\) is exponential with mean \(m\); the next chapter shows when that holds. For an exponential, \(P(Q > x) = e^{-x/m}\) and the mean absolute deviation is \(E|Q - m| = 2m/e\).

The 2R timer is \(2(b+m)\), so it is late when \(Q > b + 2m\): \(P = e^{-2 - b/m}\). As load grows, \(m\) grows and \(b/m \to 0\), so \(P \to e^{-2} = 13.5\%\).

The A + kD timer is \(b + m + k\cdot 2m/e\), so it is late when \(Q > m(1 + 2k/e)\): \(P = e^{-(1+2k/e)}\). The load has cancelled out. For k = 2, \(e^{-2.47} = 8.5\%\); for k = 4, \(e^{-3.94} = 1.9\%\). In this model \(A + 2D\) is worse than 2R at low load, one reason the 1990 revision moved to 4D.

The estimator itself is two copies of the same idea: correct a prediction by a fraction of its error (Appendix A, pp. 167–171). With a new sample M, average A and mean deviation D:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Err} &= M - A, & A &\leftarrow A + \tfrac18\,\text{Err},\\ D &\leftarrow D + \tfrac14\,(|\text{Err}| - D), & \text{rto} &= A + 2D. \end{aligned} \]

Derivation · the shift code on p. 171

Keep scaled values \(SA = 8A\) and \(SD = 4D\) so everything stays an integer. Then M -= (SA >> 3) computes Err; SA += M is \(8A \leftarrow 8A + \text{Err}\), that is \(A \leftarrow A + \text{Err}/8\). M -= (SD >> 2); SD += M is \(4D \leftarrow 4D + (|\text{Err}| - D)\), that is \(D \leftarrow D + (|\text{Err}| - D)/4\). Finally rto = ((SA >> 2) + SD) >> 1 is \((2A + 4D)/2 = A + 2D\). Check: \(SA = 800, SD = 200\) gives \((200 + 200)/2 = 200 = 100 + 2\cdot 50\).

Linux runs this code almost line for line in tcp_rtt_estimator() (net/ipv4/tcp_input.c:1070, "The following amusing code comes from Jacobson's article in SIGCOMM '88"). Two things changed: the timer is \(A + 4D\) (Jacobson's 1990 revision, noted at line 1082; RFC 6298), and the deviation term is floored at 200 ms, so on a LAN the timer is about \(A + 200\) ms.

The paper's slip. Page 169 argues that the mean deviation is a more conservative (larger) estimate than the standard deviation \(\sigma\), and that for normal errors \(\text{mdev} = \sqrt{\pi/2}\,\sigma \approx 1.25\,\sigma\). Both are inverted. With the \(1/n\) factors that footnote 15 drops, Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\text{mdev} = \tfrac1n\sum_i |e_i| \le \sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum_i e_i^2} = \sigma\), and for normal errors \(\text{mdev} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sigma \approx 0.80\,\sigma\). So \(A + 2D \approx A + 1.6\sigma\), a tighter timer than it looks, and \(A + 4D \approx A + 3.2\sigma\).

The other half of the timer story is exponential backoff (p. 162): each further retry waits twice as long. The paper argues by analogy that a network is roughly a linear system, and linear systems are stabilised by exponential damping. The black-hole lab shows it live: 0.53, 1.06, 2.12, 4.24 s.

Loss as the signal

On the paths of 1988, packets were almost never lost to damage, so a timeout almost always meant a full queue somewhere. Loss is therefore a congestion signal that every existing network already delivers, with no new header bit and no router change (p. 163).

Footnote 6 (p. 163) bounds the claim: the scheme tolerates damage loss up to about one packet per window, 12–15% for an 8-packet window, where a 12% loss rate already costs 60% of throughput. The competing DEC scheme [JRC87] needed a congestion bit set by every router; Jacobson kept its end-node policy and swapped its signal for loss (footnote 7).

On the spine the signal works. Without congestion avoidance, the four connections in Fig. 8 retransmit 4,000 of 11,000 packets, and their shares are 8, 5, 5 and 0.5 KB/s, with 6 KB/s of the 25 KB/s link unaccounted for (p. 167). With it, Fig. 9 shows 89 retransmissions out of 8,281 packets, about 1%, and the whole link is accounted for (p. 168).

Where it matters in practice: the assumption "loss means a full queue" breaks on radio links that lose packets to noise, at policers that drop without queueing, and behind very deep buffers, where the loss arrives only after the queue has added seconds of delay. Each of these returns later. The controller at the end of this reading list stops treating loss as its main signal.

Since the paper. The header bit came back as Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN, RFC 3168): routers mark instead of dropping and receivers echo the mark. Loss detection stopped waiting for timeouts too: fast retransmit and recovery (RFC 5681), selective acks and time-based detection (RACK) find most losses within an RTT, which is how the bench's sawtooth recovers without falling to cwnd = 1.

Add slowly, cut by half

On congestion the window is multiplied by \(d < 1\); otherwise it grows by a constant \(u\) per RTT. Jacobson used \(d = 0.5\) and \(u = 1\) (pp. 164–165), implemented as cwnd += 1/cwnd per ack (footnote 10).

Derivation · why the cut must be a multiplication (footnote 8)

Measure load as the average queue length \(L_i\) over intervals of about one RTT. Uncongested, \(L_i = N\), a constant. Congested, part of each interval's traffic is left over for the next: \(L_i = N + \gamma L_{i-1}\). Once the left-over term dominates, \(L_i \approx \gamma L_{i-1}\), so \(L_n = \gamma^n L_0\), which grows without bound for \(\gamma > 1\). A sender controls load only through its window, so it must shrink the window at least geometrically: \(W_i = d\,W_{i-1}\) with \(d < 1\).

The bench plot is the rule in action. The pipe is about 33 packets and the queue holds 50, so the window grows until it reaches 33 + 50 = 83, the queue overflows, and the window is halved to 42 (ssthresh 42). It then climbs back by one packet per RTT. That takes 41 RTTs, and the RTT itself rises from 57 ms to 101 ms as the queue refills, so a cycle lasts about \(41 \times 78\ \text{ms} \approx 3.2\) s. The measured period is 3.4 s.

Derivation · why additive increase converges to fairness (Chiu and Jain)

Two flows with windows \(x_1, x_2\) share one bottleneck. Additive increase adds the same \(u\) to both, so the gap \(x_1 - x_2\) stays the same. Multiplicative decrease multiplies both by \(d\), so the gap becomes \(d(x_1 - x_2)\). Each congestion cycle shrinks the gap by a factor \(d\), and after \(k\) cycles it is \(d^k\) of what it was: the flows converge to equal shares. Multiplicative increase scales both windows by the same factor, which keeps the ratio \(x_1/x_2\) fixed, so an unfair split stays unfair forever. Additive decrease keeps the gap fixed. Only AIMD both converges and stays efficient (Chiu and Jain, 1989).

Derivation · packets per loss, and the loss rate a window implies (Appendix C)

A window that runs from \(W/2\) up to \(W\), gaining one packet per RTT, takes \(W/2\) RTTs and averages \(3W/4\) packets per RTT. One cycle therefore carries \(\tfrac{W}{2}\cdot\tfrac{3W}{4} = \tfrac{3W^2}{8}\) packets and ends with one loss, so the loss rate is \(p \approx 8/(3W^2)\). This is the paper's "O(w²) packets between drops" (p. 172). For the bench, W = 83 gives 2,583 packets per loss; the measured cycle is 3.4 s × 825 packets/s ≈ 2,800. Solving for the rate gives throughput \(\approx \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}}\sqrt{3/(2p)}\), a formula the next chapter derives properly from Mathis et al. (1997).

Since the paper. Linux defaults to CUBIC (RFC 9438): it cuts by 0.7 (beta = 717/1024, net/ipv4/tcp_cubic.c:50) and regrows along a cubic curve of time since the last loss. Reno still cites this paper: "SIGCOMM '88, p. 328" above tcp_reno_cong_avoid() (net/ipv4/tcp_cong.c:494) is page 172 of this reprint, Appendix B.

The queue a window leaves behind

Any window larger than the pipe size sits partly in a queue at the bottleneck, and that queue does not drain while the window stays the same, however carefully the sender clocks its packets (footnote 9, p. 164).

The paper's example: a pipe of 16 packets, 8 each way, and a window of 22. Six packets wait at the bottleneck, and each time the router forwards one, the returning ack lays a new one on the end. The paper calls it a "stable queue".

The bench shows it at modern scale. After each halving the window is 42 packets against a pipe of about 33, so about 9 packets stay queued, and the queue panel in Diagram 1.3 never falls below 8. Nine packets at 1.21 ms each is about 11 ms added to every RTT, all the time. That is why the RTT never drops below 57 ms on a 40 ms path while the transfer runs. At the top of each cycle the queue holds 50 packets, 61 ms.

Appendix C (p. 172) says halving clears the queue when half of the overflowing window fits in the pipe: the sender pauses, the queue empties, and it does not reform. That needs a buffer no larger than the pipe. On the bench the buffer (50) exceeds the pipe (33), so half of pipe plus buffer is still above the pipe, and Reno never empties its own queue. Footnote 9 adds that clearing a queue needs spare capacity, \(\rho < 1\), with clearing time growing like \((1-\rho)^{-2}\), and a sender aiming for 100% leaves none.

This learning is the hand-off to the next two chapters. The maths chapter asks how big a buffer should be; the queue-delay chapter attacks the queue that a large buffer lets a sender keep.

Real systems

Meet the system · Linux TCP, read through ss -ti

What it is. The TCP implementation in your kernel, which runs this paper's estimator, slow start and congestion avoidance in every connection, and ss, the socket-statistics tool from iproute2 that prints each connection's internal state. With -t it lists TCP sockets and with -i it adds the kernel's struct tcp_info for each one.

Why it exists. From outside you see throughput, never the window. The kernel exports each connection's state through getsockopt(TCP_INFO) and socket-diagnostics netlink, and ss formats it.

The parts you need.

  • reno or cubic at the start of the line: the congestion-control module for this socket, chosen per socket with TCP_CONGESTION or by the net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control sysctl.
  • rtt:A/D and rto: the estimator's average and mean deviation in milliseconds (tcpi_rtt = srtt_us >> 3 and tcpi_rttvar = mdev_us >> 2, net/ipv4/tcp.c:4297–4298), and the current timer.
  • cwnd and ssthresh: Appendix B's two state variables, in packets.
  • backoff: how many times the timer has doubled for the current packet (tcp.c:4255). The field appears only while it is non-zero.

Where this chapter lives inside it. The ack clock and slow start live in tcp_slow_start() and tcp_cong_avoid_ai() (net/ipv4/tcp_cong.c:456, 470), and their result is the cwnd field. The timer that measures spread is tcp_rtt_estimator() (tcp_input.c:1070), visible as rtt:A/D, with rto \(\approx\) \(A + \max(4D,\ 200\,\text{ms})\). Exponential backoff is icsk_rto << 1 in tcp_retransmit_timer() (tcp_timer.c:683–684), visible as backoff and a doubling rto. Add-slowly-cut-by-half is tcp_reno_cong_avoid() and tcp_reno_ssthresh(), which returns \(\max(\text{cwnd}/2,\ 2)\) (tcp_cong.c:496, 515–519), visible as ssthresh jumping to half of cwnd. Loss as the signal shows up as retrans:. The queue a window leaves behind is rtt minus minrtt.

Stable: the meaning of rtt, rto, cwnd, ssthresh and backoff. Drifting (as of October 2026, iproute2 7.2.0, kernel 7.2): the set of extra fields, the default congestion control (CUBIC), and the RTO floor, which is now tunable per namespace as net.ipv4.tcp_rto_min_us.

Common wrong turn. Computing the D update with the new A (125) instead of the Err from the old one. That gives |Err| = 175 instead of 200 and a smaller D. The paper's code computes Err once and uses it for both lines.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: csam's queue is full, 50 packets of 512 data bytes each. How long does the last packet wait before it reaches the 230.4 kbit/s link?

Answer

About 0.96 s. Each packet is 512 data bytes plus 40 bytes of TCP and IP headers, 552 bytes, so it takes \(552 \times 8 / 230{,}400 = 19.2\) ms on the link. Fifty of them ahead of you is \(50 \times 19.2\ \text{ms} = 0.96\) s, nearly a second added to the RTT by one router (Fig. 7, p. 166). Near miss: dividing by 230,400 bytes per second instead of bits gives 0.12 s. The link is 230.4 kilobits per second.

2. Predict the value: a 100 Mbit/s path with a 50 ms RTT, 1448-byte segments and an initial window of 10. How many RTTs does Reno's slow start need to fill the pipe, and how much bottleneck buffer does its last round need?

Answer

About 6 RTTs and about 216 packets (313 KB). The pipe is \(100 \times 10^6 \times 0.05 / 8 / 1448 \approx 432\) packets. Doubling from 10 needs \(\log_2(432/10) = 5.4\) rounds, so six rounds, about 300 ms, and the round that reaches 432 leaves \(W/2 = 216\) packets queued (p. 160). Near miss: \(\log_2 432 = 8.75\) rounds assumes the paper's start of one packet; since RFC 6928 it is 10.

3. Here is a symptom: ss -ti shows rtt:0.31/0.12 rto:201 for a connection to a machine on the same switch. A colleague says the estimator is broken, because \(A + 2D\) is 0.55 ms. What is going on?

Answer

Nothing is broken; Linux floors the timer. It uses \(A + 4D\) (Jacobson's 1990 revision, RFC 6298) with the deviation term floored at 200 ms (tcp_rto_min_us). Here \(4D = 0.48\) ms, so the floor wins: \(0.31 + 200 \approx 201\) ms. The estimator still runs (tcp_input.c:1070); on a LAN the floor dominates. Near miss: blaming delayed acks. They are one reason the floor exists, but the floor is a constant.

4. Here is a symptom: one bulk upload crosses a 10 Mbit/s bottleneck with a 50-packet drop-tail buffer, on a path whose empty RTT is 40 ms. While it runs, the sender's RTT never falls below about 50 ms, even just after a loss. No other traffic uses the link. Where do the 10 ms come from?

Answer

From a queue the window never drains. The pipe is about 33 packets (\(825\ \text{packets/s} \times 40\ \text{ms}\)). Reno peaks at pipe plus buffer, 83, and halves to 42, still nine more than the pipe. Those packets stay queued, \((8\text{–}9) \times 1.21\ \text{ms} \approx 10\) ms, for the whole transfer (footnote 9, p. 164), because the buffer is larger than the pipe. Near miss: a slow receiver. The goodput is 9.54 of the 9.56 Mbit/s the link can carry, so the receiver keeps up; the extra delay is all queue.

5. Choose and defend: your senders usually share a deep-buffered bottleneck with one other flow. On loss, should they halve the window (\(d = 0.5\), the paper) or multiply it by \(7/8\) (the DEC scheme the paper mentions, p. 172)?

Answer

Halve, for a deep buffer. Appendix C's argument fits: with one newcomer your fair share has just halved, and half the overflowing window is the largest one known to work (p. 172). Halving empties most of the queue; \(7/8\) leaves most of it in place. Fairness comes faster too: each cycle shrinks the gap between two flows by a factor \(d\), so \(7/8\) needs about five times as many cycles as \(0.5\). The cost: on a shallow buffer the halved window falls below the pipe and the link idles until it regrows. CUBIC's 0.7 sits between the two.

6. Here is a symptom: during a 9-second outage a transfer stalls. The link comes back, but the transfer stays silent for almost two more seconds, and both hosts are healthy. Explain the silence, and say how long it could have been.

Answer

Exponential backoff. The RTO had doubled several times (tcp_timer.c:683–684), and the next retransmission goes out only when the pending timer fires; nothing else tells the sender the path is back (p. 162). The silence can be anything up to the current RTO: about 4.2 s after four doublings from 0.27 s, 8.5 s after five, capped at 120 s. In the lab the gap was 0.43 s, because the fifth timer fired soon after the path returned. Near miss: slow start. Once an ack arrives it needs only a few RTTs.

Recap

A TCP sender finds its rate from acks and losses alone. Acks carry the bottleneck's spacing back; slow start opens the window at the cost of \(W/2\)-packet bursts; a timer built from the RTT's mean and mean deviation tells loss from delay. Loss reads as congestion: the window halves because congested queues grow geometrically, and grows one packet per RTT because the network is silent about spare room. A window above the pipe leaves a queue that never drains, and a buffer larger than the pipe lets Reno keep it forever.
