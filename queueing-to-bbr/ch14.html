The question this chapter answers

At 100 Gbit/s a full-size Ethernet frame lasts 123 ns: 1,500 bytes of packet plus 38 bytes of header, checksum, preamble and gap, 8.1 million frames a second (LWN §2, after Jesper Brouer's 2015 talk). One cache miss costs about that much. This is the congestion-avoidance chapter's packet time \(P_b\) of a bottleneck, now on the host's own link, and the host has to keep up with it.

Linux already batches. On the way out, TCP builds one skb for up to 45 segments, and the NIC or the stack cuts it into packets at the last moment. On the way in, the receive path merges the arriving segments of a flow back into one skb. The stack above pays its per-packet costs once per 64 KB, 5.2 µs at 100 Gbit/s. The small-queues chapter met this limit as the "64 KB GSO limit" at the top of the TSO autosizing formula. Why 64 KB? Because both IPv4 and IPv6 keep the packet's length in 16 bits (LWN §5).

BIG TCP lifts the limit to 185,000 bytes and beyond, inside the host only: the wire still carries 1,500-byte packets. This chapter is about how a packet can be longer than its own length field, which knobs set its size, what holds its bytes, and where the bigger skb pays. It is a side path. The main chain asks when each packet should leave; this chapter asks how many skbs the host must touch per second. It rejoins the chain at the far end of the capstone's upload, and it shows why it changes nothing on that upload's 10 Mbit/s path.

Words you will meet

packet budget
The time one packet lasts on the link at full rate. A host that keeps up must finish each packet within it, on average: 123 ns for a 1,538-byte frame at 100 Gbit/s.
GSO and GRO
Generic segmentation offload: TCP builds one large skb, and the NIC (TSO) or the stack just before the driver cuts it into MTU-size packets. Generic receive offload: the receive path merges consecutive packets of one flow into one skb before IP sees them.
BIG TCP
Linux's support for TCP GSO and GRO skbs larger than 64 KB: since v5.19 (2022) for IPv6, since v6.3 (2023) for IPv4. Off by default.
GSO max size
The knobs gso_max_size (IPv6) and gso_ipv4_max_size (IPv4): per-device limits on the largest skb the stack builds for that device. The default is 65,536; the driver's tso_max_size caps them.
GRO max size
The knobs gro_max_size (IPv6) and gro_ipv4_max_size (IPv4): per-device limits on the largest skb GRO may build from packets received on that device. The default is 65,536.
hop-by-hop header (HBH)
The IPv6 hop-by-hop options header: an extension header right after the IPv6 header, carrying options every node on the path must read.
jumbogram
An IPv6 packet longer than 65,535 bytes (RFC 2675): the 16-bit payload length is 0, and a hop-by-hop option carries the real length in 32 bits.
MAX_SKB_FRAGS
An skb keeps most of its payload in page fragments, entries of (page, offset, length) in its shared info. MAX_SKB_FRAGS is the most it may have: 17 by default, and a build option from 17 to 45 since v6.4.

Chapter 14 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. Both IP versions keep the packet length in 16 bits. How does a 185,000-byte TCP packet tell the stack its length: in IPv6 as the 2022 article describes, in IPv4 from 2023, and in IPv6 today? LWN §5–§6; Isovalent §2–§3

Answer

Three ways. In 2022 IPv6 set the payload length to 0 and added an 8-byte hop-by-hop header whose jumbo option held the length in 32 bits, an RFC 2675 jumbogram (LWN §6, Isovalent §2). IPv4 has no such option, so from v6.3 it writes tot_len 0 and reads skb->len (Isovalent §3; iph_totlen(), include/linux/ip.h:39). Since v7.0 IPv6 does the same, with no extra header (merge 1fdad81d8803). Near miss: none of this reaches the wire. GSO writes a real length into every segment.

2. The wire still carries 1,500-byte packets. Where does the 185,000-byte packet exist, and what has to change on the network for it? Isovalent §4; LWN §7, §10

Answer

Only inside the two hosts: from TCP down to the driver, where TSO or GSO cuts it into MTU-size packets, and from GRO up to TCP on the far side (Isovalent §4). Switches, routers and MTUs stay as they are. Each host's devices must allow the size (the driver's tso_max_size) and be told it (the four knobs), and any code in the host that reads an IP length must cope. Near miss: jumbo frames, which change the wire and every hop on it (Isovalent §4).

3. Why did the limit of 17 fragments stand in the way, and why could the patch set not simply raise it? LWN §7–§9

Answer

Because an skb holds most of its payload in page fragments, at most MAX_SKB_FRAGS of them, and 17 single 4 KB pages hold only about 64 KB (LWN §7–§8). Zero-copy sends and many receive paths fill an skb one page at a time. But the constant sizes a structure every driver sees, and Alexander Duyck warned that drivers assume 17 and could lock up the hardware (§8). So it became a build option (§9), CONFIG_MAX_SKB_FRAGS from 17 to 45 in v6.4. Arch builds 17.

The chapter on one screen

The LWN article starts from the budget: at 100 Gbit/s, 1,538-byte packets arrive eight million times a second, about 120 ns each (§2), and the costs are per packet: the trip through the stack, interrupts, the large sk_buff (§3–§4). GSO and GRO already move 64 KB at a time, but both IP versions store the length in 16 bits (§5). For IPv6, RFC 2675's jumbograms are a way out: payload length 0, and the real length in a 32-bit hop-by-hop option (§6).

The patch set builds such packets inside the host (§7), and two problems follow. The limit of 17 page fragments fits only 64 KB of single pages; the posting raised it to 45, driver authors objected, and a build option was proposed (§8–§9). And the extra header sits where software expects TCP, so tcpdump and BPF programs misread it, and the feature is off by default (§11). Dumazet's 185,000-byte setting raised transactions per second by nearly half (§12).

The Isovalent post adds IPv4, from v6.3: a total length of 0, with the real length taken from the skb (§3). Nothing changes on the wire, unlike jumbo frames (§4). Cilium 1.13 sets 196,608 bytes on node and pod devices, given kernel 5.19 (6.3 for IPv4), BPF host routing, kube-proxy replacement, BPF masquerading, and no tunnels or encryption (§5).

Since then, v7.0 removed the hop-by-hop header, so IPv6 does what IPv4 does (merge 1fdad81d8803, February 2026), and the fragment limit became a build option in v6.4. Arch ships 17.

A budget per packet, spent per skb

The host's cost is mostly a cost per packet it handles: one trip through the stack, interrupts, and one large sk_buff to allocate, fill and free (LWN §4). So the time one packet lasts at line rate is a budget, and GSO and GRO change what the stack spends it on, from a frame to an skb.

The arithmetic is the congestion-avoidance chapter's \(P_b\) again. A 1,538-byte frame at 100 Gbit/s lasts \(1{,}538 \times 8 / 10^{11} = 123\) ns, 8.13 million a second (LWN §2 rounds to "about 120ns"). With 64 KB skbs the stack above GSO sees 45 frames as one skb, \(65{,}536 \times 8 / 10^{11} = 5.2\) µs apart. At Dumazet's 185,000 bytes it is 14.8 µs: the same bytes in a third of the skbs.

The article's slip. Isovalent §1 writes "eight-million packets per second (assuming a MTU of 1,538 bytes)". The MTU is 1,500; 1,538 is the frame on the wire, with Ethernet header, checksum, preamble and gap.

On the veth pair from the hands-on section, one IPv6 bulk flow used 0.218 CPU-seconds per GB with 64,260-byte skbs and 0.155 with 184,212-byte skbs, both ends' CPUs counted, and its throughput rose from 55.8 to 69.1 Gbit/s. That is 14.0 and 28.6 µs per skb, which fits a two-term model: about 6.2 µs fixed per skb plus 0.12 ns per byte.

Derivation · the two-term model

Let one skb with \(s\) bytes of payload cost \(F + b\,s\): \(F\) for everything done once per skb, \(b\) for everything done per byte. Then CPU per byte is \(c(s) = F/s + b\), and CPU-seconds per GB is the same number as nanoseconds per byte.

The IPv6 medians give \(0.2175 \times 64{,}260 = 13{,}976\) ns and \(0.1552 \times 184{,}212 = 28{,}590\) ns per skb. Subtracting, \(b = 14{,}614 / 119{,}952 = 0.122\) ns per byte, and \(F = 13{,}976 - 0.122 \times 64{,}260 = 6{,}150\) ns. IPv4 gives \(F = 6.6\) µs and \(b = 0.120\). At the ceiling, \(\lfloor (524{,}280 - 321)/1{,}428 \rfloor \times 1{,}428 = 522{,}648\) bytes, so \(c = 6{,}150/522{,}648 + 0.122 = 0.134\). A two-point fit says nothing about skbs much smaller than 64 KB, where acks and other per-packet work change shape.

The model shows where the gain comes from and where it ends. The per-skb part fell from 0.096 to 0.033 CPU-seconds per GB; the per-byte part, mostly the copies into and out of the sockets, stayed at 0.12. On a real NIC the per-frame costs stay too: interrupts, DMA descriptors, and GRO itself.

Sixteen bits of length, and what replaced them

IPv4's total length and IPv6's payload length are 16-bit fields, so no IP header can state more than 65,535 bytes (LWN §5). BIG TCP must state more, and only inside the host.

For IPv6 the 2022 code used RFC 2675: payload length 0, next header 0, and an 8-byte hop-by-hop header whose jumbo option carries the length in 32 bits (LWN §6, Isovalent §2). ip6_xmit() and GRO inserted it, and drivers stripped it before the wire. It sat where software expects the TCP header, so tcpdump and BPF programs misread such packets, and the feature stayed off by default (LWN §11). IPv4 has no option to borrow. From v6.3 it writes tot_len 0, and iph_totlen() returns skb->len minus the network offset for a TCP GSO skb (include/linux/ip.h:39–58; Isovalent §3). Xin Long's cover letter says why that is safe: such a packet is built and used on one host, with no padding (983f507c3043).

Since the articles. v7.0 removed the hop-by-hop header (February 2026, merge 1fdad81d8803). Alice Mikityanska of Isovalent did it as part one of "BIG TCP for UDP tunnels": in a tunnel, drivers would have to find and strip one or two such headers, and IPv4 had shown none was needed. IPv6 now writes 0 above 65,535 (ipv6_set_payload_len(), include/linux/ipv6.h:146–149; ip6_output.c:334–335, ip6_offload.c:344), and ipv6_payload_len() reads the skb (lines 130–137). The parser for real jumbograms stays (exthdrs.c:993). Cilium 1.20's tuning guide still describes the hop-by-hop header.

On the bench at 185,000, 19,963 of the first 20,000 data skbs at the receiver had a length field of 0, in both families, and none carried a hop-by-hop header. tcpdump 4.99.6 copes with one family and not the other (real output, 2 October 2026):

IP (tos 0x0, ttl 64, id 47792, offset 0, flags [DF], proto TCP (6), length 183948 [was 0, presumed TSO])
    10.0.14.1.49486 > 10.0.14.2.5014: Flags [P.], seq 14367072:14550968, ack 1, win 63, [...], length 183896
IP6 (flowlabel 0x5654f, hlim 64, next-header TCP (6), payload length 0) fd14::1 > fd14::2: [|tcp]

So LWN §11's trap moved, from a header where TCP was expected to a length field of 0. A BPF program that parses by tot_len or payload_len must use the skb's length instead, as the kernel's helpers do.

Four knobs, read when a socket connects

Each device has four limits: the GSO max size for skbs the stack builds for it (gso_max_size for IPv6, gso_ipv4_max_size for IPv4) and the GRO max size for skbs GRO builds from what it receives (gro_max_size, gro_ipv4_max_size). All four default to 65,536 (net/core/dev.c:12084–12089); the merge message raises them with ip link set dev eth0 gro_max_size 185000 gso_max_size 185000 (7fa2e481ff2f). LWN §7 ties jumbo packets to a high MTU; the merged code needs none, and the bench ran at 1,500.

Two ceilings apply: the driver's tso_max_size for the GSO knobs, and GRO_MAX_SIZE, \(8 \times 65{,}535 = 524{,}280\), for the GRO knobs (rtnetlink.c:2741–2768). veth, loopback, netkit and WireGuard raise tso_max_size to 524,280 (veth.c:1770); wlan0 or a dummy device answers Error: too big gso_max_size. All four are settable without sudo in a user namespace that owns the network namespace, because rtnetlink checks CAP_NET_ADMIN against the namespace's owner (rtnetlink.c:7003). One trap: a gso_max_size of 65,536 or less also sets the IPv4 knob (net/core/dev.h:205–212), so set IPv6 first.

TCP reads the knob when the socket gets its route: sk_setup_caps() stores it minus MAX_TCP_HEADER + 1, 321 bytes on this kernel, as sk_gso_max_size (net/core/sock.c:2618–2634, 2659). That is the 64 KB cap in the small-queues chapter's autosizing formula (tcp_output.c:2265–2268), and on a path under 512 µs the RTT bonus fills it. Predict the bench: IPv6's MSS is 1,428, so \(\lfloor (185{,}000 - 321)/1{,}428 \rfloor = 129\) segments, 184,212 bytes of payload, and with 86 bytes of headers a 184,298-byte frame. IPv4 gives 127 segments and 183,962. The captures matched to the byte, as did 64,346 and 65,226 at the default.

A running connection keeps its old size: raising the IPv4 knobs one second into a flow left it at 65,226-byte skbs, and the next connection got 183,962. That is half of why pods must restart after BIG TCP is switched on; the other half, per Cilium's docs, is that Cilium cannot reach into a running pod.

GRO checks its own limit, per family, on every merge (netif_get_gro_max_size(), netdevice.h:5522–5528; gro.c:115), and above 64 KB it merges only plain TCP. Anything encapsulated stops at 64 KB (gro.c:119–123).

Where 185,000 bytes sit: page fragments

An skb keeps its headers in a small linear area and most of its payload in page fragments, at most MAX_SKB_FRAGS of them. Seventeen fragments of one 4 KB page each hold \(17 \times 4{,}096 = 69{,}632\) bytes, just enough for 64 KB, so the old limit and the old packet size fit each other (LWN §7–§8).

The first posting raised the limit to 45 (§8); Alexander Duyck objected that drivers assume 17, and Dumazet proposed a build option (§9). The merged series dropped the change ("this belongs to a different series", 7fa2e481ff2f), and the option came in v6.4: CONFIG_MAX_SKB_FRAGS, 17 to 45, default 17 (net/Kconfig:293–303, include/linux/skbuff.h:350–354). Arch's 7.2.6 kernel builds 17, as LWN §9 feared a distribution would. Dumazet's 185,000 fits 45: his test used a 4K MTU, one page per received packet, and \(45 \times 4{,}096 = 184{,}320\).

So how did the bench build 184 KB skbs with 17? It depends on who fills the fragments.

  • TCP's copy path copies user data into 32 KB chunks of a per-socket page (SKB_FRAG_PAGE_ORDER, include/net/sock.h:3091; tcp.c:1284–1291). 185,000 bytes need six fragments.
  • A zero-copy send (MSG_ZEROCOPY) pins the user's own 4 KB pages as fragments and stops at the limit (net/core/datagram.c:650). With all four knobs at 185,000 the bench's largest zero-copy skbs were 69,698 bytes (IPv4) and 69,718 (IPv6): exactly 17 pages of payload, plus headers.
  • GRO on a 1,500-byte link adds one fragment per packet. After 17 it chains further packets on the skb's frag_list, a linked list of skbs (gro.c:135, 167, 189–213). The stack above still sees one skb, but a chain costs more to walk and split, which is what the Kconfig help means by "help GRO efficiency".
cd labs/ch14-big-tcp                        # from the dossier folder

python3 skblen.py zc.pcap                   # max 69698 B: 17 pages, whatever the knobs say

Where it matters: on a stock distribution kernel, BIG TCP works for ordinary send() calls and stops near 68 KB for a zero-copy sender.

Where BIG TCP pays, and where it changes nothing

BIG TCP removes per-skb work only where skbs can grow: the sender must have 185,000 bytes ready, and the far side must receive packets close enough together for GRO to merge them. GRO merges only what one NAPI poll, the driver's batch-receive loop, finds waiting. It flushes when the poll completes (net/core/dev.c:6817) and at each segment carrying PSH, which ends every segmented skb (tcp_offload.c:209, 357).

It pays on fast hops. Dumazet's 100 Gbit/s NICs with a 4K MTU gave +45% TCP_RR transactions (median of ten runs, 7fa2e481ff2f; LWN §12 says "nearly 50%"), Xin Long's IPv4 run at 25 Gbit/s with a 1.5K MTU +8% (983f507c3043). On the veth pair, the hop two pods on one Cilium node share, one flow went from 55 to 69 Gbit/s with 30% less CPU per byte, and with NAPI GRO on the receiver GRO handed up 184,298-byte skbs.

It changes nothing on the capstone's 10 Mbit/s path. With a 10 Mbit/s tbf on that GRO path, packets arrived 1.21 ms apart, each poll found one, and GRO handed up single 1,514-byte frames: 4,858 of the first 5,000 skbs. The knobs said 185,000; the arrival rate said one. A far end gets big GRO skbs only when the upload arrives at multi-gigabit rate, pod to pod on one node or on a 25–100 Gbit/s fabric. Nor does it help where the driver refuses: Wi-Fi's tso_max_size is 65,536.

Nor through a tunnel. GRO stops encapsulated packets at 64 KB (gro.c:119–123), and VXLAN copies tso_max_size from its lower device (vxlan_core.c:3924), so over a veth it accepts 185,000 that it cannot carry. Cilium therefore refuses BIG TCP in tunnel mode unless its probe finds a Geneve tso_max_size above 64 KB, which v7.2's Geneve never shows (bigtcp.go:254–261, probes.go:670–681).

The article's slip. Isovalent §5.2 reports the IPv4 test as "between 5,000 and 6,000 packets per seconds" without BIG TCP and "between 10,000 and 11,000" with it, "a whopping 50% increase". netperf's TCP_RR throughput counts transactions per second, each an 80,000-byte request and an 80,000-byte response, and the post's own output reads 7,102.21 and 10,304.92: +45%.

Real systems

Meet the system · GSO and GRO size knobs and BIG TCP, read through ip -d link show

What it is. Four per-device limits in the kernel, two on what TCP may build for a device and two on what GRO may build from it, under a ceiling the driver sets. ip -d link show DEV prints them, ip link set DEV gso_max_size N sets them, and Cilium sets them when BIG TCP is on.

Why it exists. IP's length field capped GSO and GRO at 64 KB. In 2022 Eric Dumazet and Coco Li of Google made the cap a per-device setting for hosts with 100 Gbit/s NICs (v5.19); in 2023 Xin Long gave IPv4 its own knobs (v6.3).

The parts you need.

  • gso_max_size, gso_ipv4_max_size: what TCP may build for this device, copied into each socket when it gets its route (sock.c:2618–2659).
  • gro_max_size, gro_ipv4_max_size: what GRO may build from this device's packets, checked on every merge (gro.c:115).
  • tso_max_size: the driver's ceiling for the GSO knobs, 524,280 on veth and 65,536 on a driver that never raised it. GRO's ceiling is GRO_MAX_SIZE, also 524,280.
  • Cilium's bigtcp cell, flags enable-ipv6-big-tcp and enable-ipv4-big-tcp: it writes the knobs on the node's native devices, on both ends of every pod's veth pair, and on its tunnel devices.

Where this chapter lives inside it. The packet budget is the knob: one skb covers 45 frames at 65,536 and 129 at 185,000 with an MSS of 1,428. The length field follows from it: above 65,535, tot_len or payload_len reads 0 (ip.h:55–58, ipv6.h:146–149). The four knobs and tso_max_size are the line ip -d prints; sk_gso_max_size is the knob minus 321, taken at connect. Page fragments sit outside the knobs: a zero-copy sender stops at 17 pages whatever they say. Where it pays starts at tso_max_size: at 65,536 (wlan0, Geneve) BIG TCP cannot start. In Cilium (fork v1.19.6-vpc), bigtcp.go:27 caps the value at 196,608; validateConfig() refuses DSR with IPIP, tunnel mode without the probe, IPsec and legacy host routing (lines 251–268); startBIGTCP() needs kernel 6.3 for IPv4 and 5.19 for IPv6 (305–312), takes the smallest tso_max_size of the native devices (337–356), sets IPv6 before IPv4 (314–318, 392–450), and with BIG TCP off lowers anything above 64 KB (407–434, 451–478). connector/veth.go:123–165 writes all four knobs on both ends of each new pod veth, and loader/netlink.go:333 on the tunnel devices.

Hands-on · The budget at three speeds, and the pages a 185 KB skb needs

~25 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil

Task. For 10, 100 and 400 Gbit/s and units of 1,500, 9,000, 65,536 and 185,000 bytes, compute units per second and time per unit; redo 1,500 as the 1,538-byte frame it is on the wire. Then count the fragments 185,000 bytes of payload need in 4 KB pages and in TCP's 32 KB chunks, and what 17 and 45 pages hold. This is the packet-budget and page-fragments learnings on paper.

Done when. You have a table of 15 cells with both numbers, you can say which cells leave the stack less than 1 µs, and you have the four fragment numbers.

Hint 1 · direction

The link counts bits; the sizes are bytes. Time per unit is size times 8 over the rate, and units per second is its inverse.

Hint 2 · mechanism

100 Gbit/s is \(1.25 \times 10^{10}\) bytes per second. For fragments, divide and round up; a buffer that does not start on a page boundary needs one page more.

Hint 3 · nearly there

185,000 bytes at 400 Gbit/s: \(185{,}000 \times 8 / (4 \times 10^{11}) = 3.7\) µs, so 270,270 per second. Pages: \(185{,}000 / 4{,}096 = 45.2\).

Solution 
unit          10 Gbit/s             100 Gbit/s            400 Gbit/s
1,500 B       833 K/s    1.20 us    8.33 M/s   120 ns     33.3 M/s   30.0 ns
1,538 B wire  813 K/s    1.23 us    8.13 M/s   123 ns     32.5 M/s   30.8 ns
9,000 B       139 K/s    7.20 us    1.39 M/s   720 ns     5.56 M/s   180 ns
65,536 B      19.1 K/s   52.4 us    191 K/s    5.24 us    763 K/s    1.31 us
185,000 B     6.76 K/s   148 us     67.6 K/s   14.8 us    270 K/s    3.70 us

185,000 B in 4 KB pages: 45.2, so 46 (47 if unaligned)    in 32 KB chunks: 5.6, so 6
17 pages: 69,632 B (about 64 KB)                         45 pages: 184,320 B (about 185,000)

Expected output. Six cells fall below 1 µs: every frame-sized unit at 100 and 400 Gbit/s. Only skb-sized units leave the stack microseconds. At 400 Gbit/s even 64 KB skbs arrive every 1.31 µs, which is why Isovalent expected the 192 KB value to rise toward 512 KB (§5.1).

What this shows. The budget per frame shrinks with the rate and cannot be helped from software; the budget per skb is what BIG TCP widens, threefold at 185,000 (the packet-budget learning, LWN §2–§4). 46 single pages exceed 17, so any path that fills an skb page by page cannot build it on Arch's kernel, while TCP's copy path needs only 6 chunks (the page-fragments learning, LWN §7–§8).

Common wrong turn. Treating the 185,000-byte skb as one packet on the wire. The wire still carries 128 frames of 1,538 bytes, so the NIC's budget per frame stays 123 ns at 100 Gbit/s. Only the stack above GSO and GRO gets 14.8 µs.

Hands-on · Raise the knobs rootless, and measure what the far end receives

~40 min · measure · no sudo, needs Python 3 and tcpdump

Task. Predict the frame length of a data skb at the receiver for IPv6 (MSS 1,428) and IPv4 (MSS 1,448), at 65,536 and at 185,000, with MAX_TCP_HEADER 320. Capture and compare, then run the five-repetition matrix and compare throughput and CPU per GB. This is the size-knobs and packet-budget learnings.

cd labs/ch14-big-tcp                        # from the dossier folder
./bigpair.sh -- ./capture.sh .              # four 2 s captures and their summaries
./bigpair.sh -- ./matrix.sh 5 8 m.jsonl     # 20 flows of 8 s, about 3.5 min
python3 summarise.py m.jsonl

Done when. You have four predicted frame lengths next to four measured ones, and the median Gbit/s and CPU-seconds per GB for the four cases.

Hint 1 · direction

The socket stores the knob minus MAX_TCP_HEADER + 1, and on a path this short TSO autosizing fills that whole size.

Hint 2 · mechanism

Segments \(= \lfloor (\text{knob} - 321)/\text{MSS} \rfloor\). Frame \(=\) segments \(\times\) MSS plus 14 bytes of Ethernet, 40 of IPv6 or 20 of IPv4, and 32 of TCP with timestamps.

Hint 3 · nearly there

IPv4 at 65,536: \(\lfloor 65{,}215/1{,}448 \rfloor = 45\), and \(45 \times 1{,}448 + 66 = 65{,}226\). matrix.sh pins the sender to CPU 5 and the sink to CPU 6 and reads /proc/stat, because softirq time is not in either process's own CPU time on this kernel.

Solution

Predictions: IPv6 \(45 \times 1{,}428 + 86 = 64{,}346\) and \(129 \times 1{,}428 + 86 = 184{,}298\); IPv4 \(45 \times 1{,}448 + 66 = 65{,}226\) and \(127 \times 1{,}448 + 66 = 183{,}962\).

What this shows. The knobs decide the skb exactly, through sk_gso_max_size (the size-knobs learning, Isovalent §5; sock.c:2618), and a third as many skbs cost only 30% less CPU per byte, because the copies are paid per byte whatever the skb size (the packet-budget learning, LWN §4).

Common wrong turn. Setting only gso_max_size and gro_max_size and expecting IPv4 to change: IPv4 has its own two knobs. The reverse order also fails quietly: set the IPv4 knobs, then lower gso_max_size to 65,536 or less, and the IPv4 knob follows it down (dev.h:205–212).

Hands-on · Make GRO build the big skb, then starve it

~30 min · break a real one · no sudo, needs Python 3 and tcpdump

Task. Make the receiver's skbs come from GRO: --gro-path turns TSO off on s0, so the sender segments in software, and GRO on for c0. With all knobs at 185,000, predict and measure the skb size GRO hands up at full speed and behind a 10 Mbit/s tbf, then say what it means for the capstone's upload. This is the learning on where BIG TCP pays.

cd labs/ch14-big-tcp                        # from the dossier folder
./bigpair.sh --gro-path --size 185000 -- ./grocap.sh 3 gro-185k
./bigpair.sh --gro-path --size 185000 --rate 10mbit -- ./grocap.sh 8 gro-185k-10mbit

Done when. Both runs are summarised, and you can name the condition GRO needs to build a 184 KB skb.

Hint 1 · direction

GRO can merge only packets that one NAPI poll finds waiting.

Hint 2 · mechanism

At 10 Mbit/s a 1,514-byte frame lasts 1.21 ms. How many frames arrive within one poll?

Hint 3 · nearly there

tcpdump -n -r gro-185k-10mbit-v4.pcap | head shows the gaps. A poll that completes flushes GRO (dev.c:6817), and so does each segment with PSH set (tcp_offload.c:357).

Solution

Expected output (the author's runs, 2 October 2026, single runs):

flow: v6 "gbps": 22.967
gro-185k-v6.pcap: IPv6 5000 data skbs, max 184298 B, median 184298 B, above 65,535: 4647 (92.9%), IP length field 0: 4647, hop-by-hop first: 0
flow: v4 "gbps": 22.874
gro-185k-v4.pcap: IPv4 5000 data skbs, max 183962 B, median 183962 B, above 65,535: 4390 (87.8%), IP length field 0: 4390, hop-by-hop first: 0
flow: v6 "gbps": 0.009
gro-185k-10mbit-v6.pcap: IPv6 5000 data skbs, max 14366 B, median 1514 B, above 65,535: 0 (0.0%), IP length field 0: 0, hop-by-hop first: 0
flow: v4 "gbps": 0.01
gro-185k-10mbit-v4.pcap: IPv4 5000 data skbs, max 14546 B, median 1514 B, above 65,535: 0 (0.0%), IP length field 0: 0, hop-by-hop first: 0

In the slow IPv4 run, 4,858 of 5,000 skbs were single frames, 1.21 ms apart; the largest, 14,546 bytes, was ten frames that left together after an idle gap.

What this shows. GRO builds the big skb only from packets that arrive back to back. At full speed it rebuilt the sender's 129-segment skbs, stopping at each PSH. At 10 Mbit/s each poll found one frame. The knobs are a ceiling; the arrival rate decides (the learning on where BIG TCP pays; Isovalent §4 on GRO's part). The capstone upload's far end therefore gets one or two frames per skb, and BIG TCP changes nothing for it.

Common wrong turn. Blaming tbf. It did cut the sender's full-size skbs into frames (sch_tbf.c:258–261), but any 10 Mbit/s bottleneck spaces frames 1.21 ms apart, and a sender at that rate would build two-segment skbs anyway (the small-queues chapter's autosizing at 10 Mbit/s).

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: Cilium writes 196,608 on a pod's veth. For IPv6 with an MTU of 1,500 (MSS 1,428) on this kernel, how many segments does one full skb carry, and what frame length does the capture record?

Answer

137 segments and a 195,722-byte frame. The socket stores the knob minus MAX_TCP_HEADER + 1 (sock.c:2633): \(196{,}608 - 321 = 196{,}287\), and \(\lfloor 196{,}287/1{,}428 \rfloor = 137\), so 195,636 bytes of payload plus 86 of headers, with the payload length field 0. The value is bigtcp.go:27 (Isovalent §5.1). Near miss: a 196,608-byte frame; the reserve and whole segments keep the skb below the knob.

2. Predict the value: with the bench's model, about 6.15 µs per skb plus 0.122 ns per byte, what CPU per GB does 196,608 give against 185,000, and what does that say about raising the knob further?

Answer

0.153 against 0.155 CPU-seconds per GB, about 1% better: \(6{,}150/195{,}636 + 0.122 = 0.153\) and \(6{,}150/184{,}212 + 0.122 = 0.155\). The per-skb share is already a fifth of the total, and the copies cost 0.122 at any size, so even the 524,280 ceiling gives 0.134 (the packet-budget learning, LWN §4). Near miss: expecting the cost to fall with the skb count; only the per-skb part does.

3. Here is a symptom: after ip link set dev eth0 gso_max_size 185000 gro_max_size 185000 on two test hosts, a new IPv6 transfer shows 184 KB skbs in tcpdump, but an IPv4 transfer started afterwards still shows 65,226-byte skbs. What was missed?

Answer

The IPv4 knobs. gso_max_size and gro_max_size govern IPv6; IPv4 reads gso_ipv4_max_size and gro_ipv4_max_size, which follow the IPv6 values only at 65,536 or below (net/core/dev.h:205–212; sock.c:2628–2629). IPv4 opts in separately because a tot_len of 0 can confuse IPv4 tools (Isovalent §3), hence Cilium's two flags. Near miss: the socket's cached size, which explains old connections only.

4. Here is a symptom: on kernel 7.2, tcpdump -v on a pod's veth shows IP6 (… payload length 0) … [|tcp] for most data packets of a bulk flow, and normal lines for the small ones. A colleague says the capture is truncated. Is it?

Answer

No. These are BIG TCP skbs: since v7.0 IPv6 writes payload length 0 above 65,535, with no hop-by-hop header (ipv6.h:146–149, merge 1fdad81d8803). tcpdump 4.99.6 takes the 0 literally, finds no room for TCP and prints [|tcp]; the pcap record still holds the length, 184,298 bytes on the bench. IPv4 prints [was 0, presumed TSO]. Near miss: the snap length; a full capture prints the same. LWN §11's trap moved.

5. Here is a symptom: with enable-ipv6-big-tcp: true on a cluster in VXLAN tunnel mode, cilium-agent stops at start with "BIG TCP in tunneling mode requires pending kernel support". A teammate wants to set gso_max_size 196608 on cilium_vxlan by hand, since ip link accepts it. What happens?

Answer

Drops, most likely. validateConfig() (bigtcp.go:254–257) fired because its probe found Geneve's tso_max_size at 65,536: this kernel has no BIG TCP for UDP tunnels. VXLAN accepts 196,608 only because it copies tso_max_size from its lower device (vxlan_core.c:3924), and GRO will not merge encapsulated packets past 64 KB (gro.c:119–123). Cilium's probe comment says this "leads to packet drops instead" (probes.go:670–681). Near miss: reading an accepted knob as support.

6. Choose and defend: you own the switches of a 100 Gbit/s cluster and want fewer CPU cycles per byte. Jumbo frames (MTU 9,000) or BIG TCP?

Answer

BIG TCP first: no fabric change, per node and per pod, and it comes off by restarting pods (Isovalent §4–§5). But it cuts only costs above GSO and GRO. Jumbo frames cut the per-frame work in the NIC, the driver and GRO, at the price of every switch, host and path agreeing on the MTU (Isovalent §4). They combine: Dumazet's 185,000 ran on a 4K MTU (7fa2e481ff2f). Add jumbo frames when per-frame costs show in profiles.

Recap

A host's cost is mostly per packet, so the 123 ns a frame lasts at 100 Gbit/s is its budget, and GSO and GRO let the stack spend it once per skb. IP's 16-bit length capped skbs at 64 KB; BIG TCP writes 0 and takes the length from the skb, in IPv4 since v6.3 and in IPv6 since v7.0. Four per-device knobs set the size, read by TCP at connect and by GRO on every merge. Arch's 17 fragments cap zero-copy senders near 68 KB. The gain needs fast, back-to-back packets: 30% less CPU per byte on a veth pair, nothing at 10 Mbit/s.
