How to use it

How each chapter works. Every chapter page has two halves, with the reading between them.

  1. Before you read (about 10 minutes): the question the chapter answers, the words you will meet, one diagram, and a reading guide that says which sections to read closely and which to skim.
  2. Pre-read quiz (about 5 minutes): paste the prompt from the card at the end of that half into an agent. It finds what you already know and tells you where to slow down.
  3. The reading. Read the paper, article or man page with the three carried questions in mind.
  4. After you read (about 20 minutes): your three questions answered, the chapter on one screen, the key ideas worked through with numbers, real systems you can run on your laptop, and two or three exercises.
  5. Lock-in quiz. Paste the second prompt. Whatever it finds loose goes back into the page as a dated revision card.

Words. Every bold word is defined where it first appears and never used before that. Words from earlier chapters link to the glossary on this page.

Maths. Each result is stated in words and then used on real numbers in the page. The derivation sits behind a purple toggle labelled Derivation. Open it when you want the proof; the page reads without it.

Diagrams. Every diagram has a zoom bar in its top right corner: minus, fit, plus, and a full-screen view where the mouse wheel zooms, dragging pans and Esc closes.

Check yourself. Every question has its answer behind a toggle. Answer out loud first, in full sentences, and only then open it. A question you can half-answer is the one worth re-reading for. Exercises climb through hints before the solution; open only as many as you need.

Getting quizzed properly. The quiz cards are written for an agent running the Alvar probe and teach skills. It asks through a real question tool, scores your reasoning rather than your letter, and writes what it found to .alvar/maps/. Without the skills, paste the same prompt to any agent along with BOOK_GUIDED_BLOG_STYLE_LEARNING.md.

The reading list. The papers live in ~/Documents/Books/networking/routing-papers-bbr/, the fetched web pages in its web/ folder, and the added readings in its extra/ folder. Page references are printed page numbers. The pacing paper has none, so its references are PDF pages. Man pages, LWN articles and slides are cited by section, message or slide number.

The lab bench

Most labs run without sudo. unshare -Urn starts a shell in a new user namespace that owns a new network namespace, and inside it you may create veth pairs, move them between namespaces and attach qdiscs. The helpers in labs/common/ build every topology from nothing, so no lab depends on another one.

Labs that need your sudo say so in their header line: loading tcp_bbr once (sudo modprobe tcp_bbr), bpftrace, and loading tc BPF programs. Optional tools you may want to install yourself: iperf3, ipvsadm. Every lab works without them.

Commands in the pages are written so they paste into fish as well as bash.

Glossary

Every term, in the order the chapters introduce it. Each links to the chapter that defines it.

Congestion avoidance and control

congestion collapse
Throughput falls by orders of magnitude because the network is busy carrying retransmissions nobody needed. The 1986 event above. in the chapter
conservation of packets
With a full window in flight, a new packet enters the network only when an old one leaves. in the chapter
bottleneck
The slowest link on the path. Its packet time, written \(P_b\), is the smallest gap it can leave between two packets. in the chapter
ack clock
Acknowledgements (acks) come back spaced like the bottleneck spaced the data, so a sender that sends only when an ack arrives sends at the bottleneck's rate. The paper says "self-clocking". in the chapter
congestion window (cwnd)
A per-connection limit kept by the sender. It may have at most the smaller of cwnd and the receiver's advertised window unacknowledged. in the chapter
slow start
Start cwnd at one packet and add one for every ack. The window doubles every round-trip time (RTT). in the chapter
ssthresh
The slow-start threshold: the window size at which slow start hands over to congestion avoidance. in the chapter
retransmit timeout (RTO)
How long the sender waits for an ack before it sends the packet again. in the chapter
mean deviation
The average size of the error between a new RTT sample and the running average. A cheap stand-in for the standard deviation. in the chapter
exponential backoff
Double the RTO each time the same packet has to be sent again. in the chapter
congestion avoidance (AIMD)
The steady-state window policy: on congestion, multiply the window by a factor below one (multiplicative decrease); otherwise add a small constant each RTT (additive increase). in the chapter
pipe size (bandwidth-delay product, BDP)
Bottleneck rate times round-trip time: the amount of data in flight that exactly fills the path, and the largest window that builds no queue. in the chapter

Queues in numbers

utilisation (utilization) \(\rho\)
The fraction of time the link is busy: arrival rate \(\lambda\) times the average service time \(\bar X\) (the time to send one packet), so \(\rho = \lambda \bar X = \lambda/\mu\), where \(\mu\) is the service rate. The congestion-avoidance chapter called it load. At \(\rho \ge 1\) the queue grows without limit. in the chapter
Little's law
The average number in any system equals the arrival rate times the average time spent inside: \(N = \lambda T\). It holds for any service order and any traffic that settles into a steady state. in the chapter
Poisson arrivals
Arrivals that are independent of each other, with exponentially distributed gaps of mean \(1/\lambda\). The usual model for the sum of many small, independent senders. in the chapter
M/M/1 queue
One server, Poisson arrivals, exponentially distributed service times. Kendall's letters name arrivals / service / servers: M is memoryless (exponential), D deterministic, G general. in the chapter
M/D/1 queue
Poisson arrivals into one server with a fixed service time: a link sending same-size packets. in the chapter
Pollaczek–Khinchine (P-K) formula
The average wait before service in an M/G/1 queue, whatever the service-time distribution: \(W = \lambda \overline{X^2} / (2(1-\rho))\). \(\overline{X^2}\) is the second moment, the mean of the squared service time, which grows with the spread of service times. in the chapter
Kleinrock's power
Throughput divided by delay. One number that rises when throughput rises and falls when delay rises. in the chapter
operating point
The throughput and delay a system settles at. Kleinrock's optimal operating point is where power peaks; for one window-limited flow through one bottleneck it is a window of exactly one pipe size, with the link full and nothing queued. in the chapter
Mathis formula
The rate of a loss-driven AIMD flow: \(\text{rate} \approx \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}} \cdot \frac{C}{\sqrt{p}}\), with \(C = \sqrt{3/2} \approx 1.22\) and \(p\) the loss rate (congestion signals per packet). in the chapter
buffer-sizing rule
One long flow needs one BDP of buffer at the bottleneck to keep the link busy after each halving; \(n\) long flows whose sawtooths are out of step need about \(\text{BDP}/\sqrt{n}\). in the chapter
Jain's fairness index
For \(n\) flows with rates \(x_i\): \(J = (\sum x_i)^2 / (n \sum x_i^2)\). It is 1 when all rates are equal and \(1/n\) when one flow gets everything. in the chapter

Controlling queue delay

bufferbloat
Buffers so large that they stay full and add delay without adding throughput (pp. 42, 44). in the chapter
active queue management (AQM)
A router that decides to drop packets before its buffer is full, instead of only when it overflows (p. 42). in the chapter
RED (random early detection)
The AQM the Internet Research Task Force recommended in 1998: drop with a probability that rises with the average queue length. It has four settings and no good rule for choosing them (p. 42). in the chapter
standing queue
The part of a queue that stays longer than a round trip. With one flow it is the window minus the pipe size (p. 44). in the chapter
good queue and bad queue
Good queue absorbs a burst and drains within about one round trip; bad queue stays for several round trips and only adds delay (p. 45). in the chapter
sojourn time
How long a packet spent in the queue: the time it leaves minus the time it arrived (p. 46). in the chapter
target
The standing-queue delay CoDel accepts: 5 ms (pp. 46–47). in the chapter
interval
How long the queue delay must stay above target before CoDel acts, about a worst-case round trip: 100 ms (pp. 46–47). in the chapter
control law
The rule for the next drop once dropping has started: \(\text{interval}/\sqrt{\text{count}}\) after the last one, where count is the number of drops so far (p. 46). in the chapter
CoDel
Controlled Delay, this paper's AQM: drop when the smallest sojourn time over an interval stays above target (p. 46). in the chapter
flow queueing (fq_codel)
(after reading) Give each flow its own small queue, run CoDel on each, and serve the queues in turn. It is not in the paper; it is Linux's fq_codel and RFC 8290. in the chapter

Shapers and fair schedulers

token bucket
A counter of permission to send. It fills at rate \(r\) up to a depth \(b\), the burst; a packet may leave only by spending tokens equal to its size. in the chapter
arrival curve
An upper bound \(\alpha(t)\) on the bytes a flow sends in any window of length \(t\). A token bucket enforces \(\alpha(t) = b + rt\). in the chapter
shaper
A token bucket with a queue in front of it. A packet without enough tokens waits until it has them. in the chapter
policer
A token bucket with no queue. A packet without enough tokens is dropped, or re-marked. in the chapter
work-conserving
A scheduler is work-conserving if the link is never idle while a packet is waiting. A shaper is not, by design. in the chapter
HTB class (rate, ceil)
One node of a hierarchical token bucket tree. Its rate is a guaranteed share with its own bucket. Its ceil is a second bucket that caps it. Between the two it may borrow what its parent is not using. in the chapter
quantum
The bytes a round-robin scheduler grants one queue per turn. in the chapter
max-min fairness
No flow can get more without taking from a flow that has the same or less. On one link: equal shares, except that a flow wanting less gets what it wants, and the rest is shared again. in the chapter
generalized processor sharing (GPS)
The ideal fluid scheduler: every backlogged flow is served at once, bit by bit, in proportion to its weight. Weighted fair queueing (WFQ) sends packets in the order they would finish under GPS. in the chapter
deficit round robin (DRR)
Round robin over queues in which each queue gets a quantum of bytes per round and carries what it could not use in a deficit counter. in the chapter

Small queues on the host: TSQ, TSO autosizing, and fq

TCP small queues (TSQ)
A per-socket limit on the bytes of that socket's packets that sit below TCP, in qdiscs and the device, and have not been freed yet. Over the limit, TCP stops sending until a packet is freed. in the chapter
TSO autosizing
TCP sizes each skb it hands down to about 1 ms of data at the socket's pacing rate, at least two segments and at most the 64 KB GSO limit. TSO is TCP segmentation offload: the device, or the stack just before it, cuts the skb into packets. in the chapter
pacing
Spreading a window's packets evenly over the round trip at a set rate, instead of sending each burst as fast as the acks or the application allow. in the chapter
pacing rate
The rate Linux TCP computes for every socket, sk_pacing_rate: a ratio times \(\text{cwnd} \times \text{MSS} / \text{srtt}\), where cwnd is the congestion window, MSS the maximum segment size and srtt the smoothed RTT. The ratio is 200% until cwnd reaches half of ssthresh, then 120%. Computing it does not enforce it. in the chapter
fq flow (sch_fq's per-flow state)
fq, the fair-queue qdisc sch_fq, keeps one record per socket: a queue of its packets, a byte credit, and the earliest time its next packet may leave. in the chapter
new and old lists
fq's two round-robin lists of active flows. A flow that wakes up joins the new list, which is served first; when its credit runs out it moves to the old list. in the chapter
throttled tree
The red-black tree in fq that holds flows whose next packet may not leave yet, ordered by that time. One timer is armed for the earliest one. in the chapter
byte queue limits (BQL)
A limit on the bytes queued inside one transmit ring of a network device. The driver reports bytes sent and bytes completed, and the limit adapts to what the device finishes per interval. in the chapter

TCP pacing

slow-start burst
In slow start each ack releases two packets, so a round leaves the sender at twice the bottleneck's rate, and a round that ends with window \(W\) leaves \(W/2\) packets queued (PDF p. 2). in the chapter
ack compression
Acks that queue behind data on the return path lose their spacing and arrive in a clump, so the ack clock releases a burst (PDF p. 3). in the chapter
paced Reno
The paper's sender: Reno plus a timer that sends the next packet every \(\text{RTT}/\text{window}\), for the whole life of the connection. The window decides how much to send and the timer decides when (PDF p. 3). in the chapter
late congestion signal
Paced traffic builds no queue until the link is full, so a loss-driven sender hears of congestion only after it has overloaded the link (PDF pp. 5–6). in the chapter
mixing of flows
Paced flows spread their packets over the whole round trip, so in a shared FIFO each flow's packets sit between everyone else's (PDF p. 6). in the chapter
congestion epoch
One overflow of the bottleneck buffer, plus the round trip in which the senders find out. Flows that lose a packet in it lose in the same epoch. in the chapter
synchronized drops
Most flows lose packets in the same congestion epoch and cut their windows together, so the link swings from overloaded to underused (PDF p. 1). in the chapter
desynchronization
Flows lose in different epochs, so their sawtooths are out of step and the sum of their windows swings less. The paper writes de-synchronization (PDF p. 6). in the chapter

Carousel

traffic shaping
Making packets leave at a set rate. The paper names two kinds: pacing, gaps between the packets of one connection, and rate limiting, one rate on a flow aggregate (p. 404). in the chapter
flow aggregate
A group of connections held to one rate together, such as every flow from one VM to one destination. in the chapter
release time (earliest release time, ERT)
The absolute time before which a packet may not leave. Each shaping policy computes one, and the packet keeps the latest. in the chapter
timing wheel
A circular array of slots, each covering one fixed slice of time. A packet goes into the slot that covers its release time, and a poller empties each slot when the clock passes it. in the chapter
slot granularity
The slice of time one slot covers, written \(g\). Packets in the same slot leave together, first in, first out. in the chapter
horizon
How far past now the wheel reaches: the number of slots times \(g\). A release time beyond it is either clamped into the last slot or dropped. in the chapter
calendar queue
A wheel that keeps every slot sorted and resizes its slots to fit the times inside: exact order, \(O(1)\) on average, \(O(n)\) when the times bunch up. in the chapter
deferred completion
The shaper reports a packet as done only when it leaves for the wire, so the sender cannot park more than a couple of packets per flow in the shaper. in the chapter
backpressure
A signal from a queue that slows the sender before the queue overflows. A drop is the coarse form; a withheld completion is the fine one. in the chapter
head-of-line blocking (HoL)
A packet that has to wait holds up unrelated packets queued behind it. in the chapter
Carousel
The paper's shaper: release times on every packet, one timing wheel per core, and deferred completions, built into a software NIC. in the chapter

Teaching NICs about time

AFAP (as fast as possible)
Jacobson's name for the contract between a protocol and its device queue: the sender says what to send and how much, and the queue's drain rate decides how fast (slide 7). His footnote calls it work-conserving. The opposite is AFAN, "as fast as necessary". in the chapter
earliest departure time (EDT)
A time written into each packet before which it must not leave. A qdisc or a network card holds the packet until then; it may leave later. in the chapter
skb->tstamp
The 64-bit field of an skb that carries the departure time on the way out (on the way in, it holds the arrival time). TCP reads the same bits under the name skb_mstamp_ns. in the chapter
CLOCK_TAI
International Atomic Time: a clock with no leap seconds, 37 s ahead of UTC once the system has been told the offset. Network cards that keep time with PTP, the Precision Time Protocol, count TAI. in the chapter
SO_TXTIME
A socket option, since Linux 4.19, that lets a socket attach a departure time to each packet it sends, in a control message called SCM_TXTIME, on a clock the socket names. in the chapter
ETF (earliest txtime first) qdisc
A qdisc that sorts packets by departure time, releases each one a fixed delta before its time, and drops any packet whose time has passed. It is made for cards that can launch a packet at a given instant. in the chapter

Eiffel

Eiffel
The paper's software scheduler: the queues below plus an extended PIFO way of writing policies, built as a Linux qdisc and as a module of the BESS software switch. in the chapter
rank
The number a scheduling policy gives a packet; the scheduler sends the smallest rank first. A send time, a deadline, a finish number or a strict priority can each be a rank. in the chapter
integer priority queue
A priority queue whose keys are integers in a known range, so it can find an element by indexing an array instead of comparing keys. in the chapter
bucketed queue
An integer priority queue over the ranks 0 to \(C\), split into \(N\) buckets of \(C/N\) ranks each. Packets in one bucket count as equal and leave first-in first-out. in the chapter
find first set (FFS)
A CPU instruction that returns the index of the lowest set bit of a machine word in a few cycles. With one bit per bucket, it names the first non-empty bucket. in the chapter
hierarchical FFS queue
A tree of bitmap words over the buckets. Each bit says whether the word below it has any bit set. Finding the first non-empty bucket costs one word and one FFS per level: \(\lceil \log_w N \rceil\) levels for \(N\) buckets and \(w\)-bit words. in the chapter
cFFS (circular hierarchical FFS queue)
Two hierarchical FFS queues: a primary for the current range of ranks and a secondary for the range after it. When the primary empties, their pointers swap, so the range moves forward without rebuilding a bitmap. in the chapter
gradient queue
Eiffel's arithmetic stand-in for FFS: each non-empty bucket adds a weighted parabola to a running sum, and the sum's lowest point falls on the highest non-empty bucket. Its approximate form answers in one step and can miss. in the chapter
PIFO (push-in first-out)
A queue that inserts each element where its rank puts it and always removes from the head. Sivaraman et al. (2016) build programmable hardware schedulers from trees of PIFOs. in the chapter
single shaper
Eiffel's way to enforce every rate limit in a scheduling hierarchy with one queue ordered by time: each limit becomes a send time on the packet. in the chapter

BBR

BBR
Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time: a congestion control that sets its rate from a measured model of the path instead of from losses (p. 60). In Linux, the tcp_bbr module. in the chapter
BtlBw (bottleneck bandwidth)
The rate of the path's slowest link, the pipe's "minimum diameter" (p. 58). BBR's estimate is the largest recent delivery rate. in the chapter
RTprop (round-trip propagation time)
The round-trip time with no queue anywhere, the pipe's "length" (p. 58). BBR's estimate is the smallest recent RTT. in the chapter
inflight
Data sent but not yet acknowledged (p. 58). The BDP is \(\text{BtlBw} \times \text{RTprop}\), the inflight that exactly fills the pipe. in the chapter
rate balance and full pipe
The paper's two conditions for the best operating point: packets reach the bottleneck at exactly BtlBw, and inflight equals one BDP. Both must hold at once (p. 60). in the chapter
delivery rate
Data delivered divided by the time it took, measured over the interval from a packet's send to its ack: \(\Delta\text{delivered}/\Delta t\) (p. 61). in the chapter
app-limited (application-limited)
The application had nothing to send, so the network was not the limit. Delivery-rate samples taken then are marked (p. 61). in the chapter
windowed filter (windowed max, windowed min)
The largest, or smallest, sample seen within a recent window of time or of round trips. Older samples fall out of the window. in the chapter
pacing gain (pacing_gain)
The multiplier on BtlBw that sets the pacing rate: \(\text{pacing rate} = \text{pacing\_gain} \times \text{BtlBw}\). BBR's primary control (pp. 61–62). in the chapter
cwnd gain (cwnd_gain)
The multiplier on the BDP that caps inflight: \(\text{cwnd} = \text{cwnd\_gain} \times \text{BDP}\). BBR's secondary control (p. 61). in the chapter
BBR states (Startup, Drain, ProbeBW, ProbeRTT)
Four modes, each a table of fixed gains with an exit rule (p. 62): find the rate, empty the queue that finding it built, cycle the gain around the rate, and briefly cut inflight to re-measure RTprop (pp. 62–64). in the chapter

Where BBR loses

shallow and deep buffer
A bottleneck buffer smaller, or larger, than one BDP. Cao et al. test 100 KB as shallow and 10 MB as deep, because 10 MB is larger than most BDPs in their grid (p. 133). in the chapter
goodput gain (GpGain)
Cao et al.'s score: BBR's goodput minus CUBIC's, as a percentage of CUBIC's (Eq. 1, p. 133). Positive means BBR got more. It is not symmetric: \(-34\%\) means CUBIC got \(1/0.66 \approx 1.5\) times BBR's goodput. in the chapter
loss cliff (cliff point)
The random loss rate above which BBR's goodput collapses instead of shrinking slowly. Cao et al. predict it at \(p = 1 - 1/g\), where \(g\) is the largest pacing gain (Eq. 3, p. 134). in the chapter
policer mode
BBRv1's long-term bandwidth mode. After two sampling intervals with steady delivery and heavy loss, it paces at their average rate with a pacing gain of 1 for 48 round trips (net/ipv4/tcp_bbr.c:184–194). Cao et al. call the trigger "a signal of policing" (p. 134). in the chapter
inflight cap (in-flight cap)
The most data BBRv1 lets be in flight: cwnd gain times BtlBw times RTprop, so 2 BDPs as BBR estimates them (Ware et al. Eq. 1, p. 140). A sender at its cap is window-limited: it sends only when acks free room. in the chapter
probe time
Ware et al.'s name for the time BBR spends in ProbeRTT, waiting for the queue to drain and then holding 4 packets in flight (Eq. 11, p. 142). in the chapter
BBRv3 (after reading)
The current version of BBR, specified in the IETF draft draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-06 (July 2026) and shipped by Google outside the mainline kernel. It reacts to loss rates above 2% and keeps two limits on inflight, one long-term and one short-term. Linux 7.2 still runs BBRv1. in the chapter

Pods with a speed limit

tenant
A customer of a shared cluster, owning some of its VMs or pods. The shapers chapter said "customers" for the same thing. EyeQ promises bandwidth per tenant endpoint; TCP knows nothing about tenants (p. 298). in the chapter
hose model
A guarantee written per endpoint: a rate in and a rate out, whoever the endpoint talks to, as if all of a tenant's endpoints hung on one private switch (p. 299, footnote 1). Its rival, the "pipe" model, needs a rate for every pair of endpoints (p. 308). in the chapter
admissible traffic
Traffic whose senders never send one receiver, in total, more than the bottleneck on the way can carry (p. 300). TCP keeps traffic admissible by backing off; EyeQ makes every transport keep it. in the chapter
EyeQ
The paper's system: on every server a sender module (SEM) of rate limiters and a receiver module (REM) of rate meters, joined by small feedback packets, so that each tenant endpoint gets a minimum and a maximum rate (pp. 298, 301). in the chapter
RCP (rate control protocol)
A congestion control in which a meter computes one explicit rate and tells the senders to use it, so they need not probe by losing packets (refs [27, 29]). EyeQ runs a variant per receiving endpoint (p. 302). in the chapter
mono delivery time (skb->tstamp_type)
A mark on an skb saying that skb->tstamp holds a departure time on CLOCK_MONOTONIC, so the kernel may keep it when it forwards the packet. One bit in Linux 5.18 (2022); since 6.11 a two-bit field that also names realtime and TAI. in the chapter
EDT rate limiter
A rate limit enforced by moving each packet's departure time: a packet may leave no earlier than its length over the rate after the previous packet of the same group. Cilium's runs in BPF at the host's network card (bpf/lib/edt.h). in the chapter
Bandwidth Manager
Cilium's feature that holds each pod to its kubernetes.io/egress-bandwidth with the EDT rate limiter, puts fq on the host's network cards, and can make BBR the pods' congestion control. It is off by default. in the chapter
BBR for pods
The Bandwidth Manager's option (bandwidthManager.bbr=true) that makes BBR the default congestion control for new pods. It needs Linux 5.18, so the departure time crosses the veth, and BPF host routing, so the packet keeps its socket. in the chapter

Maglev (side path)

5-tuple
Source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port and protocol number: the five header fields that name one connection (p. 4, footnote 1). in the chapter
VIP (virtual IP address)
An address that names a service rather than a machine. No single interface owns it; load balancers announce it to the router and many backends serve it (p. 2). in the chapter
ECMP (equal-cost multipath)
A router with several equally good next hops for one address hashes each packet's 5-tuple to pick one, so a connection stays on one next hop while the set does not change (p. 2). in the chapter
GRE (generic routing encapsulation)
Wrapping a packet in an outer IP header addressed to the chosen backend. The backend unwraps it and sees the original packet, still addressed to the VIP (p. 2). in the chapter
DSR (direct server return)
The backend sends its reply from the VIP straight to the router, so replies skip the load balancer (p. 3). in the chapter
connection tracking table
A fixed-size hash table on each Maglev, one per packet thread, that remembers which backend was chosen for each 5-tuple (pp. 4–5). in the chapter
consistent hashing
A rule that maps keys to backends so that each backend gets almost the same share (load balancing) and few keys change backend when the set of backends changes (p. 5). in the chapter
Maglev lookup table
The paper's consistent hash: an array of \(M\) slots per VIP, each naming a backend. A packet's 5-tuple hash modulo \(M\) picks its slot. \(M\) is prime (p. 6). in the chapter
offset and skip (preference list)
Two numbers hashed from a backend's name. The backend's preference list is the slots \(\text{offset},\ \text{offset} + \text{skip},\ \text{offset} + 2\,\text{skip}, \dots\), each taken modulo \(M\) (p. 6). in the chapter
disruption (minimal disruption)
The share of table slots that change backend when the set of backends changes. The slots that matter are those of backends that did not leave (pp. 5, 11). in the chapter
overprovision factor
The busiest backend's load divided by the average load. A pool has to be sized for its busiest backend (p. 9). in the chapter

BIG TCP (side path)

packet budget
The time one packet lasts on the link at full rate. A host that keeps up must finish each packet within it, on average: 123 ns for a 1,538-byte frame at 100 Gbit/s. in the chapter
GSO and GRO
Generic segmentation offload: TCP builds one large skb, and the NIC (TSO) or the stack just before the driver cuts it into MTU-size packets. Generic receive offload: the receive path merges consecutive packets of one flow into one skb before IP sees them. in the chapter
BIG TCP
Linux's support for TCP GSO and GRO skbs larger than 64 KB: since v5.19 (2022) for IPv6, since v6.3 (2023) for IPv4. Off by default. in the chapter
GSO max size
The knobs gso_max_size (IPv6) and gso_ipv4_max_size (IPv4): per-device limits on the largest skb the stack builds for that device. The default is 65,536; the driver's tso_max_size caps them. in the chapter
GRO max size
The knobs gro_max_size (IPv6) and gro_ipv4_max_size (IPv4): per-device limits on the largest skb GRO may build from packets received on that device. The default is 65,536. in the chapter
hop-by-hop header (HBH)
The IPv6 hop-by-hop options header: an extension header right after the IPv6 header, carrying options every node on the path must read. in the chapter
jumbogram
An IPv6 packet longer than 65,535 bytes (RFC 2675): the 16-bit payload length is 0, and a hop-by-hop option carries the real length in 32 bits. in the chapter
MAX_SKB_FRAGS
An skb keeps most of its payload in page fragments, entries of (page, offset, length) in its shared info. MAX_SKB_FRAGS is the most it may have: 17 by default, and a build option from 17 to 45 since v6.4. in the chapter
