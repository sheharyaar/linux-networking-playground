Chapter 2 · before you read

Queues in numbers

~7 min before · Bertsekas & Gallager pp. 152–171, 186–190; Mathis et al. pp. 67–71; Appenzeller et al. pp. 281–286 (~2.5 h) · ~22 min after · MATH
One 10 Mbit/s bottleneck with a 40 ms round trip, the bench's defaults: a pipe of 50 KB, about 33 full-size packets of 1.21 ms each. First random packets arrive at it, then one Reno flow fills it, then many flows share it.

The question this chapter answers

The congestion-avoidance chapter left its results in packets. On the bench, one Reno flow kept between 8 and 50 packets in the router's queue, and its RTT swung between 51 and 101 ms on a 40 ms path. Jacobson asserted that delay and its spread grow like \(1/(1-\rho)\) as the load \(\rho\) rises (his p. 161) and gave no derivation. Each of those is a number somebody has to compute before they size a buffer or trust a latency graph. This chapter is where the computing happens.

The spine is the bench's bottleneck: 10 Mbit/s and a 40 ms round trip. A 1514-byte frame takes \(1514 \times 8 / 10^7 = 1.21\) ms to send, so the link drains 825 packets per second, and the pipe holds \(10^7 \times 0.04 / 8 = 50{,}000\) bytes, about 33 packets. Four questions follow it through the page. If random traffic keeps the link 90% busy, how long does a packet wait? Where should one sender's window sit? What loss rate lets a Reno flow fill the link? And how big should the 50-packet buffer be for one flow, or for 400?

The readings are one textbook chapter and two papers. Bertsekas and Gallager supply the queueing maths: how backlog, rate and delay relate, and how delay grows with load. Mathis and colleagues apply it to TCP's sawtooth and get a rate for a given loss rate. Appenzeller and colleagues apply it to the buffer. Every result below is stated in words and used on the spine's numbers; the derivations sit in purple toggles.

Words you will meet

utilisation (utilization) \(\rho\)
The fraction of time the link is busy: arrival rate \(\lambda\) times the average service time \(\bar X\) (the time to send one packet), so \(\rho = \lambda \bar X = \lambda/\mu\), where \(\mu\) is the service rate. The congestion-avoidance chapter called it load. At \(\rho \ge 1\) the queue grows without limit.
Little's law
The average number in any system equals the arrival rate times the average time spent inside: \(N = \lambda T\). It holds for any service order and any traffic that settles into a steady state.
Poisson arrivals
Arrivals that are independent of each other, with exponentially distributed gaps of mean \(1/\lambda\). The usual model for the sum of many small, independent senders.
M/M/1 queue
One server, Poisson arrivals, exponentially distributed service times. Kendall's letters name arrivals / service / servers: M is memoryless (exponential), D deterministic, G general.
M/D/1 queue
Poisson arrivals into one server with a fixed service time: a link sending same-size packets.
Pollaczek–Khinchine (P-K) formula
The average wait before service in an M/G/1 queue, whatever the service-time distribution: \(W = \lambda \overline{X^2} / (2(1-\rho))\). \(\overline{X^2}\) is the second moment, the mean of the squared service time, which grows with the spread of service times.
Kleinrock's power
Throughput divided by delay. One number that rises when throughput rises and falls when delay rises.
operating point
The throughput and delay a system settles at. Kleinrock's optimal operating point is where power peaks; for one window-limited flow through one bottleneck it is a window of exactly one pipe size, with the link full and nothing queued.
Mathis formula
The rate of a loss-driven AIMD flow: \(\text{rate} \approx \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}} \cdot \frac{C}{\sqrt{p}}\), with \(C = \sqrt{3/2} \approx 1.22\) and \(p\) the loss rate (congestion signals per packet).
buffer-sizing rule
One long flow needs one BDP of buffer at the bottleneck to keep the link busy after each halving; \(n\) long flows whose sawtooths are out of step need about \(\text{BDP}/\sqrt{n}\).
Jain's fairness index
For \(n\) flows with rates \(x_i\): \(J = (\sum x_i)^2 / (n \sum x_i^2)\). It is 1 when all rates are equal and \(1/n\) when one flow gets everything.

Chapter 2 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. Why does dividing a queue's average backlog by its throughput give its average delay, whatever order the queue serves packets in? pp. 152–156

Answer

Because both count the same area. The gap between the arrival and departure staircases is the number inside, so the area between them is \(\int N\,dt\); summed packet by packet, it is \(\sum T_i\) (Fig. 3.1, p. 155). Divide by the elapsed time: \(N = \lambda T\) (p. 154). Reordering service only swaps whose strip is whose (Fig. 3.2, p. 156). Near miss: it needs neither Poisson arrivals nor first-in first-out service, only a steady state.

2. Why does the wait grow without limit as a link approaches 100% busy, and why does a link sending same-size packets wait only half as long as the M/M/1 model says? pp. 168–169, 186–189

Answer

Because a busy link has no spare time to work off random clumps. A new packet waits for the rest of the packet on the wire, \(R\), plus the queue ahead, so \(W = R + \rho W\) and \(W = R/(1-\rho)\) (Eq. 3.48, p. 188). \(R\) grows with the second moment of service (p. 189), and fixed service has half that of exponential, so M/D/1 waits exactly half (Eq. 3.45, p. 187). Near miss: the total time \(T\) is not halved.

3. Two square roots appear in the TCP papers. Where does each one come from? Mathis p. 68; Appenzeller pp. 284–286

Answer

The first is the sawtooth's area: a tooth from \(W/2\) to \(W\) carries \(3W^2/8\) packets and ends in one loss, so \(W \propto 1/\sqrt p\) (Mathis p. 68). The second is adding sawtooths: \(n\) windows out of step sum to a bell curve around one BDP whose relative width, and so the buffer that covers it, shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\) (Appenzeller pp. 285–286). Near miss: flows in step add into one big sawtooth and need a full BDP (p. 284).

The chapter on one screen

Little's theorem, \(N = \lambda T\), is an accounting identity with no assumption about arrivals or service order (pp. 152–157). On a link it makes \(\rho = \lambda \bar X\) the busy fraction (p. 157); on a window it says a larger window on a congested path only adds delay (Example 3.4, p. 158).

With Poisson arrivals, M/M/1 keeps each packet \(T = 1/(\mu-\lambda)\) (p. 169), without limit as \(\rho \to 1\). The P-K formula, \(W = \lambda \overline{X^2}/(2(1-\rho))\), covers any service time (p. 186): same-size packets wait half as long as exponential ones (p. 187).

Mathis et al. count the sawtooth: \(\text{rate} = \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}} \cdot \frac{C}{\sqrt p}\), with the RTT the flow sees (pp. 68–71). Appenzeller et al. size the buffer that keeps the link busy: one BDP for one flow, \(\text{BDP}/\sqrt n\) for many out of step (pp. 284–286). Kleinrock's power, throughput over delay, joins the pieces: for one window-limited flow it peaks where the pipe is exactly full.

Backlog is rate times delay

The average number of packets in any part of a network equals the rate through it times the average time each spends there, \(N = \lambda T\) (p. 153). It is an accounting identity, true for any service order and any traffic that settles into a steady state (p. 154), and for any box you draw: queue, link or whole path.

Derivation · the area counted twice (Fig. 3.1, p. 155)

Let \(\alpha(t)\) count arrivals and \(\beta(t)\) departures up to time \(t\). The number inside is \(N(t) = \alpha(t) - \beta(t)\), so the area between the two staircases up to a moment when the system is empty is \(\int_0^t N(\tau)\,d\tau\). Each packet \(i\) contributes a horizontal strip as long as its time inside, so the same area is \(\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha(t)} T_i\). Divide by \(t\):

\[ \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t N(\tau)\,d\tau = \frac{\alpha(t)}{t} \cdot \frac{1}{\alpha(t)} \sum_{i=1}^{\alpha(t)} T_i, \quad\text{that is}\quad N_t = \lambda_t T_t. \]

Let \(t \to \infty\). If the system is not empty at \(t\), the area sits between the sums over departed and over arrived packets, and both bounds converge to \(\lambda T\) when departures keep up with arrivals (p. 154). Reordering service only swaps whose strip is whose (Fig. 3.2, p. 156).

The previous chapter's Reno run checks it with two instruments that never talk to each other. The router's qwatch.py saw 31.5 packets queued on average at 825.7 packets per second, so Little predicts a 38.2 ms wait; the sender's mean rtt minus minrtt was 37.6 ms. Around the whole path, a mean cwnd of 65.2 packets at a mean RTT of 78.9 ms gives \(825.7 \times 0.0789 = 65.1\): the window is the path's \(N\).

That last check is Example 3.4 (p. 158): a window of \(W\) packets obeys \(W \ge \lambda T\). Once the bottleneck fixes \(\lambda\), a larger \(W\) only raises \(T\): on the bench, windows of 40 and 80 both get 825 packets per second, at 48 and 97 ms. It is the previous chapter's pipe size argument as an identity.

Where it matters: every backlog counter is a delay meter. A packet arriving at a first-in first-out queue that drains at a fixed rate waits the backlog divided by the rate: backlog 75700b 50p in tc -s on a 10 Mbit/s bottleneck is \(75{,}700 \times 8 / 10^7 = 61\) ms.

Delay as the link fills

With random arrivals the wait grows like \(\rho/(1-\rho)\): slowly, then without limit as the link approaches 100% busy. For M/M/1, \(T = 1/(\mu - \lambda)\) and the wait before service is \(W = \rho/(\mu-\lambda)\) (p. 169). The P-K formula (p. 186) shows that the spread of service times sets the constant. With \(c^2\), the service time's variance divided by its squared mean:

\[ W = \frac{1 + c^2}{2} \cdot \frac{\rho}{1-\rho} \cdot \bar X. \]

Exponential service has \(c^2 = 1\) (M/M/1); same-size packets have \(c^2 = 0\) (M/D/1) and wait exactly half as long (Eq. 3.45, p. 187).

Derivation · M/M/1 by balance, then P-K by the leftover service

M/M/1 (pp. 166–169). In steady state the system moves from \(n\) to \(n+1\) packets as often as from \(n+1\) to \(n\): \(p_n \lambda = p_{n+1} \mu\), so \(p_n = \rho^n p_0\). The probabilities sum to 1, which gives \(p_0 = 1 - \rho\) and \(p_n = \rho^n(1-\rho)\). Then \(N = \sum_n n p_n = \rho/(1-\rho)\), Little gives \(T = N/\lambda = 1/(\mu-\lambda)\), and \(W = T - 1/\mu = \rho/(\mu - \lambda)\).

P-K (pp. 187–189). An arriving packet waits for the leftover service \(R\) of the packet on the wire, then for the \(N_Q\) packets queued ahead, each taking \(1/\mu\) on average. Poisson arrivals see time averages (p. 172), so \(W = R + N_Q/\mu\). Little on the queue gives \(N_Q = \lambda W\), hence \(W = R + \rho W\) and \(W = R/(1-\rho)\). The leftover service, averaged over time, is a train of triangles of height \(X_i\) and area \(X_i^2/2\), one per packet (Fig. 3.16, p. 189), so \(R = \lambda \overline{X^2}/2\). With \(\overline{X^2} = \bar X^2(1 + c^2)\) and \(\lambda \bar X = \rho\), \(W = \frac{1+c^2}{2}\cdot\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\bar X\).

On the spine \(\bar X = 1.211\) ms and \(\mu = 825.6\) packets per second. At \(\rho = 0.5\) the M/D/1 wait is 0.61 ms, half a packet time. At \(\rho = 0.9\) it is 5.45 ms, where M/M/1 says 10.9 ms; at 0.95 it doubles again to 11.5 ms. Each halving of the spare capacity \(1-\rho\) doubles the wait.

This is the \((1-\rho)^{-1}\) Jacobson asserted (his p. 161). M/M/1's time in the system is exponential (p. 169), so its spread grows as fast as its mean, which is why a timer at a fixed multiple of the average fails under load.

Where it matters: "only 90% busy" is no comfort. Same-size packets at 90% wait 4.5 packet times on average, and the bench's 95th percentile was 14 packet times, 17.5 ms. Bursts cost more still (Problem 3.43, below).

Where one sender should sit

Example 3.7 (pp. 160–162) draws a window-limited flow's two regimes. Below a window of one pipe, throughput grows with the window and delay stays at the empty-path RTT; above it, throughput is stuck at the link rate and delay grows with the window. The crossover, \(N^* = 1 + R/P\) (p. 161), is the packets that fit in one round trip: 34 on the bench.

Kleinrock's power, throughput divided by delay, rises like the window below the pipe and falls like one over the window above it. Its peak is the operating point: the link full and nothing queued. With random arrivals the best point drops to one packet in the system on average, \(\rho = 1/2\) for M/M/1 (Kleinrock p. 43.1.2); a sender that controls its own timing can approach the deterministic one.

Derivation · power for a window, and for M/M/1

Let the empty-path RTT be \(\text{RTT}_0\), the link rate \(C\) packets per second, and the pipe \(P = C \cdot \text{RTT}_0\). With window \(W \le P\): throughput \(W/\text{RTT}_0\), delay \(\text{RTT}_0\), power \(W/\text{RTT}_0^2\), rising. With \(W \ge P\): throughput \(C\), delay \(W/C\) by Little, power \(C^2/W\), falling. Both equal \(C/\text{RTT}_0\) at \(W = P\). Dividing by that peak gives the normalised power in Diagram 2.3: \(W/P\) below the pipe and \(P/W\) above it.

For M/M/1 the power is \(\lambda/T = \lambda(\mu - \lambda)\), largest at \(\lambda = \mu/2\), where \(N = \rho/(1-\rho) = 1\).

The bench measures it. With one Reno flow's window held fixed (the lab below shows how), throughput climbs about 0.27 Mbit/s per packet of window to 9.56 Mbit/s at 36 while the RTT stays near 43 ms. From 40 up, throughput is flat and the RTT grows 1.21 ms per packet: 48.5 ms at 40, 72.7 ms at 60.

Where it matters: an AIMD sender cannot sit there. Its only signal, loss, comes after the buffer fills, so it runs past the operating point by design. The rest of this reading list works back toward that peak: keep the queue short, space the packets in time, and finally model the pipe.

Rate from loss rate

A loss-driven AIMD flow that loses a fraction \(p\) of its packets runs at about

\[ \text{rate} \approx \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}} \cdot \frac{C}{\sqrt p}, \qquad C = \sqrt{3/2} \approx 1.22 \]

for periodic loss and one ack per packet (Mathis et al. Eq. 3 and Table 1, p. 68). Halve the RTT or quarter the loss rate, and the rate doubles.

Derivation · from the packets per loss (the congestion-avoidance chapter's Appendix C toggle)

The previous chapter showed that a tooth from \(W/2\) to \(W\), one packet per RTT, carries \(\tfrac{3}{8}W^2\) packets in \(W/2\) RTTs. One loss per tooth means \(p = 8/(3W^2)\), so \(W = \sqrt{8/(3p)}\) (Eq. 1, p. 68). The rate is packets per tooth over time per tooth:

\[ \text{rate} = \frac{\text{MSS} \cdot \tfrac38 W^2}{\text{RTT}\cdot \tfrac W2} = \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}} \cdot \frac{3W}{4} = \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}}\sqrt{\frac{3}{2p}}. \]

With delayed acks the window grows half a packet per RTT, which divides \(C\) by \(\sqrt2\): 0.87. Ott's stochastic derivation with random, independent losses gives 1.31, or 0.93 with delayed acks (Table 1, p. 68).

On the bench, the previous chapter's Reno peaked at \(W = 83\), so the model wants \(p = 8/(3 \times 83^2)\), one loss per 2,583 packets; the measured tooth was \(3.4\ \text{s} \times 825 \approx 2{,}800\) packets. With the RTT the flow saw, 78.9 ms, the formula gives 9.1 Mbit/s against 9.54 measured. Turned around: to fill this link at the empty-path RTT of 41.3 ms, Reno may lose one packet in 775.

The RTT is the one the flow sees, queueing included; the fit was poor until the authors used it (p. 71), and minrtt here would overpredict 1.9 times. The model leaves out timeouts (p. 70), short flows and receiver-limited flows (p. 69).

Where it matters: flows sharing a bottleneck see the same \(p\), so their rates go like \(1/\text{RTT}\): RTTs of 20 and 80 ms split the link 4 : 1, which Jain's fairness index (Chiu and Jain, p. 5) scores 0.74. Add 60 ms of queue to both and the split is 1.75 : 1, \(J = 0.93\): a big buffer makes sharing fairer by making everyone slow (Appenzeller p. 288).

Since the paper. Linux defaults to CUBIC, whose rate on large pipes falls like \(p^{-3/4}\) (RFC 9438, 2023, "Fairness to Reno"). On small pipes like the bench its Reno-friendly region, on by default (net/ipv4/tcp_cubic.c:53), keeps the Mathis form. Linux Reno grows the window by the segments each ack covers (tcp_cong_avoid_ai(), net/ipv4/tcp_cong.c:470), so delayed acks no longer lower \(C\) to 0.87.

How big a buffer

One long-lived flow keeps the link busy through each halving only if the bottleneck buffer holds at least one pipe, \(B \ge \text{RTT} \times C\) (Appenzeller et al. p. 284). Many long flows whose sawtooths are out of step need about \(\text{RTT} \times C / \sqrt n\) (pp. 285–286).

The one-flow argument is a drain-time race. At the top of a tooth the buffer is full; the loss halves the window, and the sender stops until \(W_{\max}/2\) packets are acked, which takes \((W_{\max}/2)/C\). The buffer drains in \(B/C\), so the link stays busy if \(B \ge W_{\max}/2\), and the halved window must then fill the pipe alone: \(W_{\max}/2 = \text{RTT}\times C\) (p. 284).

The bench's 50-packet buffer is more than its 33-packet pipe needs. The window peaks at 83 and halves to 42, still 9 above the pipe: the previous chapter's "queue never below 8". A 33-packet buffer would give the same 9.5 Mbit/s with the queue touching zero once per tooth; a 10-packet buffer would idle the link for part of every tooth.

With many flows, each window swings evenly between \(\tfrac23\) and \(\tfrac43\) of its mean (p. 286). Independent swings add in quadrature, so the total's spread shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\) relative to one BDP, and Eq. 3 gives at least 98.99% utilisation for \(B = \text{BDP}/\sqrt n\) at \(n = 10{,}000\) (p. 286). Flows in step need the full BDP (p. 284); the authors saw that below about 100 flows (p. 285). The rule assumes windows of two packets or more (p. 288): on a 10 Gbit/s, 100 ms link it means 6.25 MB instead of 125 MB; on the spine, nothing.

The paper's slips. Page 283 says the sender "times-out" and then halves its window; a timeout drops the window to one packet (the previous chapter's Appendix B), and the halving the argument needs comes from fast retransmit. The table on p. 286 lists \(1.5 \cdot \text{BDP}/\sqrt n\) as "Util ≥ 99.99988%", where Eq. 3 gives \(\operatorname{erf}(2.715) = 99.988\%\).

Since the paper. The authors' 2019 "Sizing Router Buffers (Redux)" reports a Level 3 backbone link run for weeks with its buffer cut from 190 ms to 1 ms and no measurable throughput loss, and an Internet2 link with 150 flows that needed 26 kB where the old rule said 648 kB (CCR 49(5), p. 71).

Real systems

Meet the system · netem, the kernel's network emulator

What it is. A Linux qdisc, attached with tc, that holds each outgoing packet until a computed time, and can add delay and jitter, drop, duplicate, corrupt or reorder packets, and limit the rate. It is the bottleneck in every bench in this dossier.

Why it exists. To test protocols against a wide-area path on one desk. Stephen Hemminger wrote it, borrowing from NIST Net; the source calls it "a simple way to delay outgoing packets to test TCP" (net/sched/sch_netem.c:39–41).

The parts you need.

  • delay TIME [JITTER]: a fixed or random delay per packet; the bench's 20 ms each way.
  • rate RATE: charges each packet \(\text{len} \times 8 / \text{rate}\) (packet_time_ns(), sch_netem.c:367–380).
  • limit N: packets netem may hold; the next is dropped at enqueue (sch_netem.c:552). tc's default is 1000.
  • The time-ordered queue: dequeue releases a packet once its time_to_send has passed, else arms a timer (sch_netem.c:752, 798).

Where this chapter lives inside it. With rate set, enqueue gives each packet a release time of the later of now and the previous packet's release time, plus its own transmission time (sch_netem.c:588–626): the textbook's single first-in first-out server. Fixed 1514-byte frames make service deterministic, so Poisson arrivals give an exact M/D/1 queue; replaying that rule on the lab's send times reproduces the measured wait to within 1%. limit is the buffer, and tc -s's backlog is Little's \(N\) at that instant.

The first lab needs a pencil. The other two run without sudo, and each script builds its own bench with labs/common/qnet.sh. Run them from the dossier folder, in fish or bash.

Hands-on · The spine's arithmetic by hand

~30 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil

Task. For the spine (10 Mbit/s, 40 ms, 1514-byte frames, 1448-byte segments) compute: (a) the pipe in bytes, frames and segments; (b) the Mathis rate at \(p = 10^{-2}\) and an RTT of 41.3 ms, and the loss rate that just fills the link; (c) the buffer for 1 flow and for 400 flows; (d) \(W\), \(T\) and \(N_Q\) at \(\rho = 0.9\) for M/M/1 and M/D/1.

Done when. Four lines of numbers within 5% of the solution, and one sentence on why the 400-flow buffer means nothing on this link.

Hint 1 · direction

(a) is rate times RTT. (b) uses the Mathis formula forwards, then solved for \(p\). (c) uses \(B = \text{BDP}\) and \(\text{BDP}/\sqrt n\), then the paper's condition on the average window (p. 288). (d) uses the M/M/1 results on p. 169 and Eq. 3.45 on p. 187, then Little for the queue length.

Hint 2 · mechanism

One frame takes \(\bar X = 1.211\) ms, so \(\mu = 825.6\) per second and \(\lambda = 0.9\mu = 743\) per second. M/M/1: \(W = \rho/(\mu-\lambda)\), \(T = 1/(\mu-\lambda)\). M/D/1: \(W\) is half, and \(T = W + \bar X\). In every case \(N_Q = \lambda W\).

Hint 3 · nearly there

\(\text{MSS}/\text{RTT} = 1448 \times 8 / 0.0413 = 280.5\) kbit/s and \(C/\sqrt{p} = 1.22/0.1 = 12.2\) at \(p = 10^{-2}\). For the inverse, \(p = (C \cdot (\text{MSS}/\text{RTT}) / \text{rate})^2\) with the rate 9.56 Mbit/s, the link's goodput.

Solution 
(a) pipe = 10e6 × 0.040 / 8 = 50,000 B
         = 33.0 frames of 1514 B = 34.5 segments of 1448 B
(b) rate(p = 1e-2) = 280.5 kbit/s × 12.25 = 3.4 Mbit/s
    p that fills the link = (1.2247 × 280.5e3 / 9.56e6)^2
                          = 1.3e-3, one loss in 775 packets
(c) 1 flow:    B = 50 KB = 33 packets
    400 flows: 33 / sqrt(400) = 1.7 packets
(d)                 W         T         N_Q
    M/M/1       10.90 ms  12.11 ms   8.1 packets
    M/D/1        5.45 ms   6.66 ms   4.05 packets

What this shows. Every number in this chapter comes from rate, RTT and \(\rho\). The 400-flow buffer is meaningless because 400 flows on a 33-packet pipe have an average window under one packet, far below the two packets the rule assumes (Appenzeller p. 288); such flows live on timeouts, and Mathis's model does not cover them either (p. 70). The rule is for big pipes: a 10 Gbit/s, 100 ms link with 400 flows needs 6.25 MB instead of 125 MB.

Common wrong turn. Adding the service time to \(W\) or leaving it out of \(T\). \(W\) is the wait before the link starts sending the packet, and \(T = W + \bar X\). At low load the two differ by almost the whole delay; at \(\rho = 0.9\), M/D/1's \(W\) is 5.45 ms and its \(T\) is 6.66 ms.

Hint 1 · direction

Hands-on · Find the operating point by holding the window

~30 min · measure · no sudo, needs Python 3

Task. Predict goodput, RTT and normalised power for one Reno flow with its window held at 10, 33, 60 and 80 packets, and uncapped; then measure. wsweep.sh caps the window with a locked route metric, ip route replace default via 10.0.1.2 cwnd lock W, which Linux copies into snd_cwnd_clamp (tcp_init_metrics(), net/ipv4/tcp_metrics.c:487–488).

cd labs/ch02-queues   # from the dossier folder
./wsweep.sh           # 14 windows, a fresh bench each, about 2.5 minutes
cut -d, -f1,2,4,5,7,8 out/wsweep.csv | column -s, -t

Done when. Predictions within 10%, and you can name the window where power peaks and say why Reno does not sit there.

Hint 1 · direction

Two regimes, as in Example 3.7 (p. 161) and the learning on where one sender should sit: below the pipe, throughput grows with the window; above it, delay does.

Hint 2 · mechanism

Below the pipe, goodput is \(W \times 1448 \times 8/\text{RTT}_0\) with \(\text{RTT}_0 \approx 41.3\) ms. Above it, goodput is 9.56 Mbit/s and the RTT is \(W\) divided by 825.6 packets per second. Normalised power is \((\text{goodput}/9.56) / (\text{RTT}/41.3)\).

Hint 3 · nearly there

The pipe is \(825.6 \times 0.0413 = 34\) packets. For the uncapped flow, use the previous chapter's sawtooth: it averages about 65 packets.

Solution

Predictions: \(W = 10\): 2.8 Mbit/s, 41.3 ms, power 0.29. \(W = 33\): 9.3 Mbit/s, 41.3 ms, 0.97. \(W = 60\): 9.56 Mbit/s, 72.7 ms, 0.57. \(W = 80\): 9.56 Mbit/s, 96.9 ms, 0.43. No cap, average 65: 9.56 Mbit/s, about 79 ms, 0.52. The author's run, 2 October 2026 (selected rows of data/wsweep.csv):

What this shows. Power peaks at a window of about one pipe, 0.95 at 36 packets, which is the operating point; past it every packet of window adds 1.21 ms and nothing else, Example 3.4's lesson (p. 158). The uncapped Reno flow gets the same throughput at almost twice the RTT, power 0.52, and loses 90 packets to find it. The littles_inflight column, packets per second times RTT, equals the window in every row. The measured RTTs below the pipe sit 1 to 2 ms above 41.3 because the receiver acks every second segment, so each RTT sample includes about one extra packet time.

Common wrong turn. Setting the route and expecting the next connection to obey it. Linux reads a locked cwnd through its TCP metrics cache, and the cache entry for a peer is created only when a connection to it closes, so the first connection runs uncapped (in one try, its window reached 164). The script makes a short connection first. It also builds a fresh bench per window, because ip tcp_metrics flush is refused inside a user namespace.

From the book

Six of the chapter's 66 problems, the ones that test this page's strands; stems paraphrased, two carried onto the bench.

1. Problem 3.1: customers reach a fast-food restaurant at five per minute and wait 5 minutes for their order; half then eat in for 20 minutes, half leave. How many are inside on average? p. 242

Answer

75, the printed answer. Little's law on the whole restaurant: the average time inside is \(5 + 0.5 \times 20 = 15\) minutes, so \(N = 5 \times 15 = 75\) (p. 153). Near miss: \(5 \times 25 = 125\), which gives every customer the meal; the time must be averaged over all arrivals.

2. Problem 3.4: in a traffic system \(T = \alpha + \beta N^2\). What is the largest arrival rate \(\lambda^*\) it can sustain, and below it, is \(T\) unique? p. 242

Answer

\(\lambda^* = 1/(2\sqrt{\alpha\beta})\), at \(N = \sqrt{\alpha/\beta}\). With \(N = \lambda T\), throughput \(\lambda = N/(\alpha + \beta N^2)\) rises, peaks and falls, because more cars slow every car. Below \(\lambda^*\) each \(\lambda\) has a light state and a crowded one, and the crowded one is unstable: extra cars raise \(T\) and cut departures (p. 158). The solutions manual prints \(1/\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\), dropping the ½.

3. Problem 3.9, predict the values: a 50 kbit/s line carries 10 Poisson sessions of 150 packets per minute, exponential lengths of mean 1000 bits. Find \(N_Q\), \(N\) and \(T\) with ten equal time-division channels, and with statistical multiplexing. p. 243

Answer

Per channel, M/M/1 with \(\mu = 5\)/s and \(\lambda = 2.5\)/s: \(N = 1\), \(N_Q = 0.5\), \(T = 0.4\) s. Shared, one M/M/1 at ten times the rates: the same \(N\) and \(N_Q\) for the whole line, and \(T = 0.04\) s (Example 3.9, p. 170). The book's printed 5 and 10 for the split line are totals over the ten sessions, though the stem says "for each session".

4. Problem 3.21, then predict: show that the M/M/1/\(m\) queue (at most \(m\) packets, arrivals lost when full) has \(p_n = \rho^n(1-\rho)/(1-\rho^{m+1})\). What fraction would the bench's 50-packet queue drop under M/M/1 traffic at \(\rho = 0.9\) and 0.95? p. 247

Answer

\(5.2\times10^{-4}\) and \(4.2\times10^{-3}\). The balance equations are M/M/1's (p. 168) cut off at \(m\), so \(p_n = \rho^n p_0\) and a finite geometric sum gives \(p_0\). Poisson arrivals see time averages (p. 172), so the lost fraction is \(p_{50}\). Same-size packets have a thinner tail, so netem's queue drops less: the netem card's 4 s run at 95% lost none of 3,116.

5. Problem 3.31, here is a wrong argument: an arrival finds the server busy with probability \(\lambda \bar X\), the packet in service has mean \(\bar X\), so \(\bar X/2\) remains and the mean leftover is \(\lambda \bar X^2/2\). What is wrong? p. 248

Answer

It treats the packet on the wire as typical. An arrival is more likely to land in a long service, in proportion to its length, so the interrupted service averages \(\overline{X^2}/\bar X\) and half of it remains; times \(\lambda\bar X\), that gives \(R = \lambda\overline{X^2}/2\) (Fig. 3.16, p. 189). The argument holds for fixed service and is low by half for exponential (p. 166). Near miss: blaming the busy probability, which is right.

6. Problem 3.43, then predict: with Poisson batches of random size \(n\), show \(W = \frac{\lambda \bar n \overline{X^2}}{2(1-\rho)} + \frac{\bar X(\overline{n^2} - \bar n)}{2\bar n(1-\rho)}\). What is the mean wait on the bench for bursts of exactly 10 frames at \(\rho = 0.49\)? p. 251

Answer

11.3 ms, against 0.6 ms for single frames; the lab measured 11.0 ms. Each packet also waits for the earlier members of its own batch, so \(W = R + \rho W + W_B\) (the book's hint). For fixed batches of \(b\) same-size frames, \(W = \bar X(\rho + b - 1)/(2(1-\rho))\). Bursts cost delay at any load: slow start's two packets per ack build queues on an idle link.

The rest, by topic. Little's theorem: 3.2, 3.3 (p. 242), 3.41, 3.42 (pp. 250–251). Poisson and M/M/1 variants: 3.5–3.8, 3.10–3.18 (pp. 242–246). Many servers and blocking: 3.19, 3.20, 3.22–3.27 (pp. 246–247). M/G/1 and P-K: 3.28–3.30, 3.32, 3.36, 3.37 (pp. 248–249; 3.36 and 3.37 have printed answers), 3.44–3.49 (pp. 251–253). Priorities and reservations: 3.33–3.35, 3.38–3.40 (pp. 248–250). Networks of queues: 3.50–3.66 (pp. 254–258).

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Here is a symptom: tc -s qdisc on a home router's 10 Mbit/s uplink shows 12,000 to 75,000 bytes of backlog whenever you look, and ping past it averages 79 ms on a 40 ms path. One upload is running. What is happening, and what keeps the upload's speed but cuts the delay?

Answer

The window sits far above the pipe, and a buffer larger than one pipe never lets the queue drain. Little turns the 39 ms extra into 32 packets on average. Reno peaks at pipe plus buffer, 83, and halves to 42, still above the 33-packet pipe (Appenzeller Fig. 6, p. 284). A 33-packet buffer keeps the link busy and halves the average queue. Near miss: a slow receiver; the goodput is the link's full rate.

2. Here is a symptom: one-minute graphs show a 10 Mbit/s link 70% busy, yet users see 30–60 ms latency spikes several times a minute. A colleague says queueing cannot cause that below 100%. What do you check?

Answer

The load at a finer time scale, and the shape of the arrivals. Random single packets at a steady 70% wait 1.4 ms. A one-minute 70% can hide seconds at 100%, where the queue grows without limit (p. 168), and 25 frames sent back to back make the last one wait 29 ms at any load (Problem 3.43). Sample the backlog every 20 ms with qwatch.py. Near miss: "the link is too slow"; a faster link shortens the waits (Example 3.8, p. 170), but bursts remain.

3. Choose and defend: buffers for (a) a 10 Gbit/s backbone port, 100 ms RTT, about 50,000 long flows; (b) a 100 Mbit/s home uplink, 40 ms RTT, one or two uploads. What size for each, and what do you give up?

Answer

(a) About \(\text{BDP}/\sqrt n\), \(125\ \text{MB}/224 = 0.56\) MB, perhaps doubled for margin: that many flows are out of step (pp. 285–286), and the rule held in Level 3's backbone (Redux, p. 71). (b) A full BDP, 500 KB, because one or two flows halve together (p. 284). The price is up to 40 ms of queue at each tooth's top; a smaller buffer trades throughput for delay.

4. Here is a symptom: a colleague takes 1% loss and a minrtt of 20 ms, applies the Mathis formula with 1448-byte segments, predicts 7 Mbit/s, and the flow gets 2 Mbit/s with several timeouts in ss -ti. Which assumptions failed?

Answer

Two at least. At 1% loss the window peaks near 16 packets and halves to 8, so two losses in one window starve fast retransmit and force timeouts, which the model leaves out (pp. 69–70). And the RTT must be the one the flow sees (p. 71): a queue that doubles it halves the prediction. Use the mean rtt and treat the answer as an upper bound (Eq. 4, p. 69). Near miss: blaming \(C\), which moves the answer by 30% at most.

Recap

Little's law turns any backlog into a delay, for a queue, a link or a whole path, where the window is the \(N\). Random arrivals make the wait grow like \(\rho/(1-\rho)\), scaled by \((1+c^2)/2\); bursts wait longer still. Power peaks at a window of one pipe, which loss-driven senders overshoot by design. One flow needs one pipe of buffer; many flows out of step need \(1/\sqrt n\) of it.
