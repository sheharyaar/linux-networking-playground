Chapter 5 · before you read

Small queues on the host: TSQ, TSO autosizing, and fq

~6 min before · two LWN articles, tc-fq(8) and about 300 lines of kernel source (~1 h) · ~23 min after · HOST
One netperf session with 3 MB sitting in pfifo_fast on its own sender (the TSQ article, §7): TSQ cuts what one socket may keep below TCP, TSO autosizing cuts each skb to about a millisecond of data, and fq lets those skbs out at the socket's rate.

The question this chapter answers

In July 2012 Eric Dumazet reported that a single netperf session kept 3 MB queued in the qdisc of its own sender, pfifo_fast, the default at the time (TSQ article, §7). At 1 Gbit/s, 3 MB is 24 ms of waiting; at 100 Mbit/s it is a quarter of a second. Every other flow on that host waits behind it, and so does the sender's own RTT estimate. The router and the receiver were fine. The queue was on the sender.

The congestion-avoidance chapter gave the reason in one line: a window larger than the pipe size leaves a queue somewhere, and the queue sits at the slowest point. On a busy host the slowest point is often the host's own interface, and its qdisc has room for a thousand packets or more (pfifo_fast takes the interface's txqueuelen, 1000; today's usual default, fq_codel, takes 10,240). That chapter's bench put its bottleneck in a separate router namespace on purpose; this chapter explains why it had to.

Linux answered with three small changes. A per-socket limit on bytes below TCP (2012). A rule that cuts what TCP hands down into chunks of about one millisecond (2013). And a qdisc that keeps each flow's packets apart and lets them out at the flow's rate (2013). The readings are short, two LWN articles and a man page, so a few hundred lines of the v7.2 kernel source are part of the book this time.

Words you will meet

TCP small queues (TSQ)
A per-socket limit on the bytes of that socket's packets that sit below TCP, in qdiscs and the device, and have not been freed yet. Over the limit, TCP stops sending until a packet is freed.
TSO autosizing
TCP sizes each skb it hands down to about 1 ms of data at the socket's pacing rate, at least two segments and at most the 64 KB GSO limit. TSO is TCP segmentation offload: the device, or the stack just before it, cuts the skb into packets.
pacing
Spreading a window's packets evenly over the round trip at a set rate, instead of sending each burst as fast as the acks or the application allow.
pacing rate
The rate Linux TCP computes for every socket, sk_pacing_rate: a ratio times \(\text{cwnd} \times \text{MSS} / \text{srtt}\), where cwnd is the congestion window, MSS the maximum segment size and srtt the smoothed RTT. The ratio is 200% until cwnd reaches half of ssthresh, then 120%. Computing it does not enforce it.
fq flow (sch_fq's per-flow state)
fq, the fair-queue qdisc sch_fq, keeps one record per socket: a queue of its packets, a byte credit, and the earliest time its next packet may leave.
new and old lists
fq's two round-robin lists of active flows. A flow that wakes up joins the new list, which is served first; when its credit runs out it moves to the old list.
throttled tree
The red-black tree in fq that holds flows whose next packet may not leave yet, ordered by that time. One timer is armed for the earliest one.
byte queue limits (BQL)
A limit on the bytes queued inside one transmit ring of a network device. The driver reports bytes sent and bytes completed, and the limit adapts to what the device finishes per interval.

Chapter 5 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. The TSQ article says the limit holds "regardless of where the data is queued". What exactly is counted, and at what moment does a byte stop counting? TSQ §3, §5–§6

Answer

The count is sk_wmem_alloc, the truesize of every skb TCP has handed down that nobody has freed (§5). Each transmitted skb gets tcp_wfree as its destructor (tcp_output.c:1624). A byte stops counting when its skb is freed: after the driver sends it on a real NIC, or earlier if something orphans it, as netem does at enqueue (sch_netem.c:499–500). Near miss: the socket's unsent data is outside the count. It waits in the write queue and delays nobody.

2. The 2013 patch computes a pacing rate but does not enforce it. Why not, and who does? TSO/FQ §5–§6, §9

Answer

Pacing inside TCP would add complexity it did not need; a qdisc could do it for every flow (§6). The patch only writes sk_pacing_rate, then \(2 \times \text{cwnd} \times \text{MSS}/\text{srtt}\), and fq enforces it (§9). In v7.2 the ratio is 200% while \(\text{cwnd} < \text{ssthresh}/2\) and 120% after (tcp_input.c:1154–1159). Since v4.13 TCP can also pace with its own timer, once the socket asks. Near miss: with pfifo or fq_codel and no request, nobody enforces the rate. It still sizes skbs and sets the TSQ limit.

3. How can fq pace thousands of flows with a single timer? TSO/FQ §9

Answer

By keeping one time per flow, in one ordered tree (§9). A flow whose next packet is not due goes into the throttled tree, keyed by that time, and fq arms one timer, the qdisc watchdog, for the smallest key (sch_fq.c:745). The next dequeue moves every flow that is due back to the old list (fq_check_throttled(), sch_fq.c:664). An insert costs \(O(\log n)\) in waiting flows. Near miss: a timer per flow. fq never arms more than one.

The chapter on one screen

The TSQ article starts from bufferbloat inside the sender, below TCP (§1–§2). Byte queue limits capped the device ring; nothing capped the qdisc (§2). TSQ caps the bytes each socket has below TCP, wherever they sit, with a counter the stack already kept, sk_wmem_alloc (§3, §5). Over the limit TCP stops; when an skb is freed, its destructor and a tasklet send more (§6). The 3 MB in pfifo_fast went away, and 10 Gbit/s still ran at full speed (§7–§8).

The 2013 article adds two halves of one idea. TSO autosizing hands down about 1 ms of data per skb instead of 64 KB (§2–§4). Pacing spreads a flow's packets at a rate; TCP computes the rate and leaves enforcing it to FQ (§5–§6). FQ keeps one state per flow in a hash table of red-black trees (§8), parks flows whose next packet is not due in a second tree keyed by time under one timer (§9), and serves active flows from a new list, then an old list (§10). Limits, a priority queue and three-second garbage collection complete it (§11–§12).

All three run in v7.2 with new numbers. TSQ allows two skbs or about 1 ms of pacing rate, at most 4 MB; the rate ratio is 200% or 120%; fq has 1024 trees and three bands. And TCP now turns the rate into a send time for every packet, which is the departure-timestamps chapter.

A limit on bytes below the socket

TSQ caps sk_wmem_alloc, the truesize of a socket's skbs that TCP has handed down and nobody has freed. Over the cap TCP stops and waits for a free, and the data it holds back stays in the socket (TSQ article, §5–§6). Truesize is an skb's memory cost, about 1 KB above its payload. In v7.2 the cap is

\[ \text{limit} = \min\!\big(\max(2\,\text{truesize},\ \text{pacing rate} \gg 10),\ \texttt{tcp\_limit\_output\_bytes}\big), \]

doubled for retransmissions (tcp_small_queue_check(), tcp_output.c:2857–2900; its comment says "two packets / or ~1 ms"). Shifting by 10 divides bytes per second by 1024 (sk_pacing_shift, net/core/sock.c:3800). The refill: tcp_wfree() subtracts the truesize and queues a throttled socket on a per-CPU list, and a bottom-half work item calls tcp_write_xmit() (lines 1387–1430, 1304–1328).

On the bench, move the 10 Mbit/s bottleneck onto the sender: tbf on s0 over a 10,000-packet pfifo, delays moved to the router. One Reno flow kept 4–7 packets on s0 and a 48 ms RTT on a 40 ms path, at 9.52 Mbit/s with no loss; 540 KB waited in the socket. Each skb held three segments (the pacing rate stayed at 200%, 40.6 Mbit/s), roughly 5–6 KB of truesize, and two of them beat \(40.6\ \text{Mbit/s}/8/1024 = 4.96\) KB. The 4 MB sysctl never mattered: 64 KB changed nothing, 4 KB halved the queue.

TSQ is as good as the moment of the free. netem with a delay or a rate orphans each skb at enqueue (sch_netem.c:499–500), and the count drops to zero: the top panel, the 2012 article's 3 MB back. That is also why the congestion-avoidance chapter's netem delay on s0 never throttled TCP. Across a veth the skb stays charged until IP receive in the next namespace orphans it (ip_input.c:583), so that chapter's 50-packet router queue was invisible to TSQ.

Byte queue limits do the same job one layer down, per transmit ring: the driver reports bytes queued and completed (dql_completed(), lib/dynamic_queue_limits.c:83), and the limit grows when the ring ran dry. The ring stays short, so the queue rises into the qdisc, where TSQ keeps each socket's share small. The limits live in /sys/class/net/DEV/queues/tx-N/byte_queue_limits/; neither wlan0 nor veth uses BQL (net/core/net-sysfs.c:1940).

Since the article. The default went from 128 KB (v3.6, 2012) to 256 KB (v4.2), 1 MB (v5.0) and 4 MB (v6.16, 2025: "Modern NIC speeds got a 4x increase", tcp_ipv4.c:3491). The formula's shape dates from v3.19 (2014); in effect it allows two autosized skbs up to about 1 Gbit/s, and 1 ms above. Wi-Fi widens it to 8 ms (sk_pacing_shift 7, net/mac80211/main.c:951). The tasklet became a BH workqueue item in v6.17.

One skb per millisecond

TCP sizes each skb at about 1 ms of data at the pacing rate, between two segments and the 64 KB GSO limit (TSO/FQ article, §4). Before 2013 a TSO sender handed down 64 KB whatever its rate (§3–§4): for a 10 Mbit/s flow, 45 packets and 54 ms of the link in one burst. v7.2 computes (tcp_tso_autosize(), tcp_output.c:2256–2271):

\[ \text{segs} = \max\!\Big(\Big\lfloor \tfrac{\min(\text{pacing rate} \gg 10\ +\ \text{RTT bonus},\ 64\ \text{KB})}{\text{MSS}} \Big\rfloor,\ 2\Big). \]

The RTT bonus adds \(64\ \text{KB} \gg \lfloor \text{min RTT}/512\,\mu\text{s} \rfloor\), so close peers keep big skbs; beyond 3 ms it is under one segment. The floor is tcp_min_tso_segs, which a congestion-control module may override (tcp_tso_segs(), line 2276).

TSO is off on the bench's s0, but TCP builds GSO skbs anyway (sk_setup_caps(), net/core/sock.c:2647) and the stack cuts them after the qdisc, so each skb reaches the router as one back-to-back burst. With fq, no bottleneck and a pacing cap, the capture counted:

Derivation · the 200 Mbit/s row

The cap is \(200 \times 10^6 / 8 = 25 \times 10^6\) bytes/s, and \(25{,}000{,}000 \gg 10 = 24{,}414\) bytes. The min RTT is 40 ms, so \(40{,}000 \gg 9 = 78\), which is 32 or more, and the bonus is zero. \(\lfloor 24{,}414 / 1448 \rfloor = 16\) segments, above the floor. One burst carries \(16 \times 1448 = 23{,}168\) bytes of payload, and TCP spaces bursts by payload at the capped rate: \(23{,}168 / (25 \times 10^6) = 0.927\) ms. At the floor (20 Mbit/s) the burst is 2 segments, \(2896 / (2.5 \times 10^6) = 1.158\) ms apart.

At 10 Mbit/s in congestion avoidance, \(1.5\ \text{MB/s} \gg 10 = 1465\) bytes: one segment, raised to two, hence the pairs in this chapter's 10 Mbit/s captures. At 1 Gbit/s the rule gives 146 KB, cut to 64 KB, 45 segments; above that the GSO limit decides, until the last chapter raises it.

Since the article. The 2013 code sized skbs at sk_pacing_rate / 2000, at sendmsg() time; v3.19 (2014) moved the decision to transmit time as \(\text{rate} \gg 10\), and v5.18 (2022) added the RTT bonus. v7.2 keeps the min_tso_segs hook; net-next replaced it on 5 September 2026 with a tso_segs() callback that returns the final count (cb145191e9d3).

A rate TCP computes and someone else enforces

Pacing spreads a window's packets over the RTT at a rate. The ack clock spaces packets only while acks arrive evenly; at start-up, after idle, or when acks bunch, a window leaves as a burst. Linux computes a rate per socket on every ack (tcp_update_pacing_rate(), tcp_input.c:1138–1170):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{pacing rate} &= \text{ratio} \times \frac{\max(\text{cwnd},\ \text{packets out}) \times \text{MSS}}{\text{srtt}},\\ \text{ratio} &= 200\%\ \text{if}\ \text{cwnd} < \text{ssthresh}/2,\ \text{else}\ 120\%. \end{aligned} \]

The ratios are sysctls (tcp_ipv4.c:3501–3502). In slow start the window doubles each RTT, so 200% lets it; later, 120% sits just above the flow's own rate. SO_MAX_PACING_RATE caps the result (TSO/FQ article, §5). The congestion-avoidance chapter's ss line had cwnd:61 rtt:72.016: \(1.2 \times 61 \times 1448 \times 8/0.072016 = 11.77\) Mbit/s, printed as pacing_rate 11774304bps.

Two things can enforce the rate. fq reads it, marking the socket fq-paced when it first sees it (sch_fq.c:400, 452). Or TCP's own timer holds the next skb (tcp_pacing_check(), tcp_output.c:2815), but only for a socket that asked: SO_MAX_PACING_RATE sets the pacing-needed mark (net/core/sock.c:1263–1266). With pfifo or fq_codel and no request, the rate only sizes skbs and the TSQ limit.

After slow start the two look the same: pairs 2.42 ms apart, the bottleneck's spacing returned by the acks, and only 282 throttles in 10 s. Pacing earns its keep in bursts, which the TCP pacing chapter takes further.

Since the article. The ratio was 200% always in 2013; the 200/120 split came in v4.3 (2015), and TCP's own timer in v4.13 (2017). Since v4.20 TCP turns the rate into a send time per skb (tcp_update_skb_after_send(), tcp_output.c:1446–1468), and fq holds the skb until then: the departure-timestamps chapter.

One flow per socket, served in turns

fq keeps one fq flow per socket and serves active flows round robin with a byte credit (TSO/FQ article, §8, §10). It is the shapers chapter's deficit round robin with different bookkeeping: a flow sends while its credit is positive, may overdraw by one packet, and gets one quantum when it reaches zero. The quantum is 2 MTU, 3028 bytes; a new flow starts with initial_quantum, 10 MTU, enough for an initial window of ten (tc-fq(8), PARAMETERS).

Enqueue hashes the socket pointer to one of 1024 red-black trees and finds or creates the flow (sch_fq.c:405); packets without a socket hash their headers. An idle flow that gets a packet joins the new list (line 638). Dequeue serves the new list first (line 694). A flow out of credit gets a quantum and goes to the tail of the old list (line 753), and one that empties on the new list passes through the old list once (line 781), so it cannot stay new.

New flows first gets a DNS answer or a short GET out ahead of bulk flows (§10). Each flow may queue flow_limit packets, 100, inside a total limit of 10,000. Under TSQ a TCP socket rarely queues more than two skbs: on the bench even flow_limit 2 dropped nothing for two paced flows. The per-flow limit is for traffic TSQ cannot see, such as UDP and forwarded packets.

Since the article. The patch it read used 256 trees; the merged version (v3.12) used 1024, as v7.2 does (line 1244). Idle flows still go after 3 s (FQ_GC_AGE, line 252), but since v6.7 every lookup cleans its tree. The high-priority queue became three bands with weighted round robin in v6.7 (lines 1239–1241), so highprio reads 0.

Waiting flows in a tree of times

When a flow's next packet may not leave yet, fq puts the flow in the throttled tree, keyed by time_next_packet, and keeps one timer for the smallest key (TSO/FQ article, §9). Each dequeue first returns due flows to the old list (fq_check_throttled(), sch_fq.c:664–690). So fq is non-work-conserving: it may idle with packets queued.

In fq_dequeue() the due time is the later of the packet's own send time and the flow's time_next_packet (lines 761–762). If it is in the future, fq_flow_set_throttled() inserts the flow, \(O(\log n)\) (lines 220–247); if nothing is ready, the watchdog is armed for the earliest time (line 745). A packet without a send time gets one from fq, its length over \(\min(\text{pacing rate}, \texttt{maxrate})\), at most one second ahead (lines 802–833). That is the 2013 arithmetic; TCP's packets now carry their own time.

In the 5 Mbit/s capture, 4,628 packets in pairs made 2,314 skbs, and fq's insert counter read 2,303: nearly every skb waited in the tree once.

Since the article. The timer is still the one qdisc watchdog; since v5.7 it may fire up to timer_slack, 10 µs, late so the kernel can batch wakeups. Packets stamped more than 10 s ahead are dropped as horizon_drops, a matter for the departure-timestamps chapter.

Real systems

Meet the system · sch_fq, the fair-queue and pacing qdisc

What it is. A classless qdisc by Eric Dumazet, in Linux since 3.12 (2013), that keeps one queue per socket, serves the queues in turns, and holds each flow's packets until its pacing allows them out: tc qdisc replace dev DEV root fq.

Why it exists. In 2013 TCP started computing a pacing rate and nothing enforced it. A qdisc sees every packet of every local flow, so pacing there costs one timer for the whole host (TSO/FQ article, §6). Google runs it on its production servers (commit 29f834aa326e, 2023).

The parts you need.

  • The flow table: 1024 red-black trees indexed by a hash of the socket pointer (buckets), one fq flow per socket.
  • The new and old lists, with quantum and initial_quantum as the per-turn credit.
  • The throttled tree and one watchdog timer (timer_slack).
  • Three priority bands (priomap, weights), each with its own new and old lists.

Where this chapter lives inside it. Per-flow scheduling: flows N (inactive M …) counts flows and those with no packet; flows_plimit counts drops at flow_limit, gc flows freed after 3 s idle, fastpath packets sent at once because nothing was waiting. The throttled tree: the bracketed throttled is flows waiting now, the later throttled is inserts so far, next_packet_delay is when the timer fires and latency how late it fires on average. maxrate caps every flow's rate, and pkts_too_long counts gaps cut to one second. TSQ shows as about two skbs of backlog per TCP flow. highprio reads 0 since the bands; alloc_errors and horizon_drops (the departure-timestamps chapter) print only when non-zero.

Meet the system · bpftrace

What it is. "A high-level tracing language for Linux" (its site): short scripts attach to kernel functions, tracepoints or user functions, are compiled to eBPF, and count or histogram values inside the kernel.

Why it exists. To ask "how big, how often, from where" of a running kernel without rebuilding it or logging every event.

The parts you need.

  • Probes: kprobe:FUNC, kretprobe:FUNC on any kernel function that is not inlined.
  • Builtins: retval in a kretprobe, comm, pid, arg0….
  • Maps: @name[key] = count(), hist(), lhist(), printed on Ctrl-C.

Where this chapter lives inside it. TSO autosizing answers through tcp_tso_segs(): tcp_tso_autosize() is inlined and absent from /proc/kallsyms, so you probe the caller, whose retval is segments per skb. The TSQ decision is tcp_small_queue_check(), built as tcp_small_queue_check.isra.0, whose retval is 1 when TSQ said stop. The refill starts at tcp_wfree. Any user can check the names in /proc/kallsyms; only the addresses read as 0.

Try it (~5 min; the bpftrace lines need sudo, so run them yourself during a lab flow):

grep -wE 'tcp_tso_segs|tcp_tso_autosize|tcp_small_queue_check.isra.0|tcp_wfree' /proc/kallsyms

Hands-on · The TSQ limit and the skb size at four speeds

~25 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil

Task. A Reno flow in congestion avoidance, 40 ms path, MSS 1448, 64 KB GSO limit, at 10 Mbit/s, 1 Gbit/s, 10 Gbit/s and 100 Gbit/s. For each, compute the pacing rate, segments per skb and the TSQ limit (truesize = payload + 1.3 KB), name the deciding term, and convert the limit to milliseconds. Then redo 10 Gbit/s with the 2012 rule, a flat 128 KB.

Done when. You have a four-row table with the deciding term in every cell, and you can say how many times more bytes TSQ allows at 10 Gbit/s than at 10 Mbit/s.

Hint 1 · direction

Both formulas start from the pacing rate, which in congestion avoidance is 120% of the flow's rate. Then shift right by 10, which divides bytes per second by 1024.

Hint 2 · mechanism

Segments: \(\max(\lfloor \min(\text{rate} \gg 10,\ 65{,}536)/1448 \rfloor,\ 2)\); the RTT bonus is zero at 40 ms. Limit: \(\min(\max(2\,\text{truesize},\ \text{rate} \gg 10),\ 4\ \text{MB})\), where truesize is that of one skb of the size you just found.

Hint 3 · nearly there

At 10 Mbit/s: \(1.2 \times 10^7 / 8 = 1.5 \times 10^6\) bytes/s, and \(\gg 10\) gives 1,465 bytes, one segment, so the floor of two decides. One skb is 2,896 bytes plus 1.3 KB, about 4.2 KB, and two of them beat 1,465 bytes.

Solution 
speed       rate>>10   segs/skb     TSQ limit (deciding term)   below the socket
10 Mbit/s   1,465 B    2 (floor)    8.4 KB (2 truesize)         ~4.8 ms
1 Gbit/s    146 KB     45 (64 KB)   146 KB (1 ms term)          1.17 ms
10 Gbit/s   1.46 MB    45 (64 KB)   1.46 MB (1 ms term)         1.17 ms
100 Gbit/s  14.6 MB    45 (64 KB)   4 MB (the sysctl)           0.34 ms
2012 rule at 10 Gbit/s: a flat 128 KB, two 64 KB skbs         0.10 ms

Expected output. The pacing rates are 1.5 MB/s, 150 MB/s, 1.5 GB/s and 15 GB/s, and the 1 ms term is \(1.2/1024\) s of the flow's real rate, 1.17 ms. At 10 Mbit/s two skbs are 4 packets, 4.8 ms of the link. At 10 Gbit/s TSQ allows 1.46 MB against 8.4 KB at 10 Mbit/s, about 175 times more for a thousand times the rate. At 100 Gbit/s, \(4{,}194{,}304 \times 8 / 10^{11} = 0.34\) ms. The bench agrees at the low end: at 10 Mbit/s with the 200% ratio the skbs were three segments and the queue swung between 4 and 7 packets.

What this shows. Each term of the limit decides in its own range: two skbs for slow flows, so a flow is never starved; about a millisecond for fast ones; the sysctl only at the very top (the TSQ learning, TSQ article §4). The 2012 flat 128 KB was 0.1 ms at 10 Gbit/s, which is why the default kept rising and the 1 ms term took over.

Common wrong turn. Dropping the max with two truesizes and predicting 1.5 KB at 10 Mbit/s, less than one packet. TSQ would then allow one skb at a time and stall on every completion; the floor exists for that case.

Hint 1 · direction

The TSO-autosizing learning gives the burst: \(5\ \text{Mbit/s} \gg 10\) is far below one segment. The pacing learning says what TCP spaces by: payload bytes at the capped rate.

Hint 2 · mechanism

Gap \(= \text{segments} \times 1448 / 625{,}000\) bytes per second. Frames are 1514 bytes, so the wire rate is \(1514/1448\) times the payload rate. For pfifo: which flag did SO_MAX_PACING_RATE set on the socket, and who acts on it when fq is gone?

Predictions: two segments per burst (the floor), gap \(2 \times 1448 / 625{,}000 = 4.634\) ms, payload 5.000 Mbit/s, frames \(5 \times 1514/1448 = 5.23\) Mbit/s.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: a flow on a 40 ms path is capped at 100 Mbit/s with SO_MAX_PACING_RATE, fq on the sender. How many segments per burst does a capture show, and how far apart?

Answer

8 segments, 0.93 ms apart. \(12.5 \times 10^6\) bytes/s \(\gg 10\) is 12,207 bytes, and \(\lfloor 12{,}207/1448 \rfloor = 8\); the RTT bonus is zero at 40 ms. TCP spaces bursts by payload: \(8 \times 1448 / (12.5 \times 10^6) = 0.927\) ms. The bench measured 8 and 920 µs (TSO/FQ §4, tcp_output.c:2256). Near miss: the pre-2013 64 KB skb gives 45-packet bursts 5.2 ms apart.

2. Predict the value: cwnd 40, srtt 50 ms, MSS 1448, ssthresh 200. What pacing_rate does ss print? And once a loss sets ssthresh to 60, cwnd still 40?

Answer

18.5, then 11.1 Mbit/s. 40 is below \(200/2\), so 200%: \(2 \times 40 \times 1448 \times 8 / 0.05 = 18.5\) Mbit/s. 40 is not below \(60/2\), so 120%: 11.1 Mbit/s (tcp_input.c:1154–1159). Near miss: switching at cwnd < ssthresh, slow start's own test, keeps 200% in the second case. The kernel slows down at half of ssthresh, as the end of slow start nears.

3. Here is a symptom: a load-test box sends one upload through netem delay 50ms rate 100mbit limit 100000 on its own eth0. The qdisc shows megabytes of backlog, the RTT climbs into seconds, and ss -tm shows skmem:(…,t0,…). TSQ is on. What is happening?

Answer

netem orphans each skb at enqueue when it has a delay or a rate (sch_netem.c:499–500), so sk_wmem_alloc, the t field, is zero and TSQ never stops TCP (TSQ article §5–§6). The queue grows with whatever the application writes; the bench reached 14.9 MB and 8 s. Put the emulator on another hop. Near miss: lowering tcp_limit_output_bytes. No value helps when the count is zero.

4. Here is a symptom: after a switch from fq_codel to fq, the second throttled counter barely moves during long uploads near the link rate, and ss -ti shows pacing_rate about 20% above send. Is fq's pacing broken?

Answer

No. In congestion avoidance the pacing rate is 120% of \(\text{cwnd} \times \text{MSS}/\text{srtt}\), so while the ack clock spaces packets evenly none is early, and fq sends each at once; it throttles a flow only when its next packet is not due (TSO/FQ §9). The bench: 282 throttles in 10 s uncapped, 2,303 with a 5 Mbit/s cap. Near miss: blaming TSQ, which limits how many skbs wait in fq and never turns pacing off.

5. Here is a symptom: on an fq root, flows_plimit climbs while a UDP video sender on the same host stutters; TCP flows are fine and the backlog is small. What drops the video, and what would you change?

Answer

fq's per-flow limit. UDP has no TSQ, so a frame sent in one burst puts more than flow_limit (100) packets into one flow and the rest are dropped (sch_fq.c:631; tc-fq(8), PARAMETERS). TSQ keeps TCP far below it. Pace the UDP socket with SO_MAX_PACING_RATE, which fq applies to packets without a send time (lines 802–804), or raise flow_limit. Near miss: the global limit of 10,000.

6. Choose and defend: a host serves bulk TCP downloads and forwards traffic for containers behind it. Root qdisc on the uplink: fq or fq_codel?

Answer

fq, if local TCP dominates: it enforces each socket's pacing rate, favours new flows, and under TSQ each socket queues about two skbs, so local flows need no drop policy (TSO/FQ §8–§10). Forwarded packets carry no socket, so fq gives them no TSQ and no pacing, only a header hash and a 100-packet limit. fq_codel drops those by sojourn time, as the CoDel chapter showed, but leaves TCP unpaced unless a socket asks. If forwarding dominates, choose fq_codel.

Recap

A sender's own qdisc fills like any queue when the window exceeds the pipe. TSQ caps what each socket keeps below TCP, counted until each skb is freed, at two skbs or about a millisecond; a local bottleneck holds a handful of packets and the rest waits in the socket. TSO autosizing makes each skb about a millisecond of data, at least two segments. TCP computes a pacing rate, 200% or 120% of cwnd over srtt, and fq enforces it with one flow per socket, new and old lists, and one throttled tree under one timer.
