Chapter 3 · before you read

Controlling queue delay

~6 min before · pp. 42–50 (~1 h) · ~23 min after · AQM
A 25-packet window pushed into a 20-packet pipe, which leaves 5 packets standing in the bottleneck queue for good (Figs 1–3, pp. 44–45). Then a Wi-Fi-like link that falls from 100 to 1 Mbit/s under an 830-packet buffer: about 10 s of queue (Fig. 7, p. 48).

The question this chapter answers

The congestion-avoidance chapter ended on a queue that never drains. On the bench, Reno's window after each halving was still about 9 packets larger than the pipe size, so the router's queue never fell below 8 packets: 11 ms on every round trip, for nothing. Give the same router 1000 packet slots and the same sender keeps about 513 of them full: 0.65 s on every packet, at the same 9.5 Mbit/s (measured in the hands-on section). Nichols and Jacobson call this leftover queue the essence of bufferbloat (p. 44). Bufferbloat is their name for buffers that stay full and add delay without adding throughput.

Neither end can fix it alone. The sender would need the pipe size, and the pipe changes whenever the rate or the path changes. The router sees its queue, but it has to tell two kinds apart: a burst that is about to drain, which it must keep, and a queue that will sit there for good, which only adds delay. The paper shows that the obvious signals, how many packets are queued and how long the buffer stays busy, both get this wrong (p. 45).

So the question is what a router can measure, with almost no state and no tuning, that separates the two kinds of queue, and how hard it should push back once it finds the bad kind. The paper's answer, CoDel, is under 300 lines of include/net/codel_impl.h today, and it already runs inside your laptop's Wi-Fi stack.

Words you will meet

bufferbloat
Buffers so large that they stay full and add delay without adding throughput (pp. 42, 44).
active queue management (AQM)
A router that decides to drop packets before its buffer is full, instead of only when it overflows (p. 42).
RED (random early detection)
The AQM the Internet Research Task Force recommended in 1998: drop with a probability that rises with the average queue length. It has four settings and no good rule for choosing them (p. 42).
standing queue
The part of a queue that stays longer than a round trip. With one flow it is the window minus the pipe size (p. 44).
good queue and bad queue
Good queue absorbs a burst and drains within about one round trip; bad queue stays for several round trips and only adds delay (p. 45).
sojourn time
How long a packet spent in the queue: the time it leaves minus the time it arrived (p. 46).
target
The standing-queue delay CoDel accepts: 5 ms (pp. 46–47).
interval
How long the queue delay must stay above target before CoDel acts, about a worst-case round trip: 100 ms (pp. 46–47).
control law
The rule for the next drop once dropping has started: \(\text{interval}/\sqrt{\text{count}}\) after the last one, where count is the number of drops so far (p. 46).
CoDel
Controlled Delay, this paper's AQM: drop when the smallest sojourn time over an interval stays above target (p. 46).
flow queueing (fq_codel)
(after reading) Give each flow its own small queue, run CoDel on each, and serve the queues in turn. It is not in the paper; it is Linux's fq_codel and RFC 8290.

Chapter 3 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. A sender's window is 25 packets and the pipe holds 20. Why does the bottleneck queue settle at 5 packets however steadily the sender transmits, and why can't the sender fix it on its own? pp. 44–45

Answer

Because after one round trip the ack clock makes arrivals equal departures, so the window's excess stays queued: from the 26th packet on, one arrives for each one that leaves, and five stay (Fig. 2, p. 44). The sender cannot fix it because it would need the pipe size, which changes as connections and paths come and go (p. 45). Near miss: "the sender is too fast". A 20-packet window sends at the same rate and leaves no queue.

2. Why are the queue's length and the time the buffer stays busy both useless for telling good queue from bad, and what does CoDel measure instead? pp. 45–46

Answer

Because startup needs a big queue, so size says nothing about excess, and a receiver that acks once per window keeps the buffer busy all the time with no excess (Figs 3–4, p. 45). CoDel reads each packet's sojourn time at dequeue and watches its minimum over a sliding interval; good queue drains to near zero within a round trip (p. 46). Near miss: the average sojourn. Fig. 4's bursts average half their drain time, a standing queue that is not there (RFC 8289 §3.1).

3. Once CoDel starts dropping, when does the next drop come, and what makes it stop? p. 46

Answer

The next drop comes \(\text{interval}/\sqrt{\text{count}}\) after the last: drops at 0, 100, 170.7 and 228.4 ms (p. 46 in words; codel_impl.h:93 as a formula). It stops when one dequeued packet waited less than target, or when under one MTU is queued (p. 46). A quick return resumes near the last drop rate (codel_impl.h:233–250). Near miss: "when the queue is empty". One short sojourn is enough, with packets still queued.

The chapter on one screen

The paper opens with a diagnosis: bufferbloat is a standing queue, and it is "almost universally misclassified" as congestion (pp. 44–45). A window larger than the pipe leaves window minus pipe packets queued for good: 25 into 20 leaves 5 (Figs 1–2, p. 44). The startup burst on top of it is good queue, which drains in about a round trip; the leftover is bad queue (Fig. 3, p. 45).

Detecting it is the hard part. Queue size fails because startup needs a big queue; busy time fails because an ack-per-window receiver keeps the buffer occupied with no excess (Fig. 4, p. 45). The minimum queue over a window a little longer than a round trip works (p. 45).

CoDel rests on three ideas (p. 46): the local minimum; one variable that records how long the minimum has been above target; and sojourn time instead of bytes or packets. All the work happens at dequeue; enqueue only stamps a time. After an interval above target CoDel drops a packet and schedules the next drop sooner, in inverse proportion to the square root of the drop count, until sojourn falls below target; it never drops with under one MTU queued (p. 46). Target is 5 ms and interval 100 ms (p. 47).

The evidence is ns-2 simulation (pp. 47–49): one setting from 64 kbit/s to 100 Mbit/s, RED over-controlling (Fig. 5), and on a link stepping down to 1 Mbit/s, tail drop holding 10 s of queue while CoDel re-converges within 100 ms (Fig. 7). Last, the queue that fills may sit in a cable modem, out of the router's reach (Fig. 10, p. 50).

Window minus pipe is a standing queue

A window larger than the pipe leaves its excess queued at the bottleneck, and that queue stays however smoothly the sender transmits. It adds delay and nothing else, which is why the paper calls it the essence of bufferbloat (p. 44).

Follow the spine. The pipe is 10 bottleneck packet times each way, 100 ms at 10 ms per packet, so 20 packets fill it (p. 44). The sender launches 25 back to back. In the paper's squeeze example the \(k\)-th packet of a burst entering a ten-times-slower link waits \((k-1) \times 9\) ms, so the 25th waits 216 ms. That burst is good queue. A round trip later the acks arrive spaced by the bottleneck and each releases one packet (Fig. 2): \(25 - 20 = 5\) packets stand, 50 ms on every packet, for exactly the throughput of a 20-packet window.

This is the queue a window leaves behind from the congestion-avoidance chapter (its footnote 9: window 22, pipe 16), and the bench queue there that never fell below 8. With a 1000-packet pfifo at 10 Mbit/s, Reno's window sat near 540 against a 33-packet pipe, and the router held a median of 513 packets, window minus pipe again: 0.65 s of RTT at 9.54 Mbit/s.

Little's law from the queueing chapter turns the count into delay: \(W = L/\lambda = 513/825 = 0.62\) s, plus the 40 ms path, matches the measured 655 ms. What those models miss is the arrival process. TCP's arrivals are not Poisson arrivals: the ack clock ties each arrival to a departure, an equality the paper says theorists dismissed as wildly improbable (p. 45).

Where it matters: the sender cannot shrink its window to a pipe it cannot see (p. 45), so detection belongs at the bottleneck, with TCP's response to a drop as the actuator.

Length and busy time are blind

Two natural signals both fail (p. 45). Queue length cannot separate good from bad queue, because startup needs a big queue. The time the buffer stays occupied fails on Fig. 4.

Fig. 4 uses a 20-packet window that exactly fills a 20-packet pipe, with a receiver that acks once per window. Each round trip the sender fires 20 packets back to back; the queue jumps to 20 and drains to zero. The buffer is never empty, yet shrinking this queue would idle the link (p. 45). Beside the tail of Fig. 3:

The average is the worst: a burst that drains every round trip averages half its size (RFC 8289 §3.1), so an averaging AQM sees Fig. 4's harmless queue as twice Fig. 3's standing one.

That is RED's design: an exponentially weighted average of the queue length, and a drop probability that rises between two thresholds, four settings in all. The right values depend on link rate and traffic; ns-2's RED read the link's bandwidth to set itself (p. 47) and still over-controlled at 10 Mbit/s and up, with low utilisation (Fig. 5, p. 46).

The minimum sojourn over an interval

Good queue drains within about one round trip; bad queue does not. So the smallest sojourn time over a window a little longer than a round trip is near zero for good queue and stays up for a standing one (pp. 45–46).

The one-variable trick is plain logic: "the minimum sojourn over the last interval is above target" means "every packet dequeued in the last interval waited at least target". So CoDel keeps one timestamp, first_above_time: set to now plus interval when a packet first leaves above target, cleared by any packet below it. Once that time passes, the minimum has stayed above target for a whole interval (codel_impl.h:128–142).

The minimum can only fall when a packet leaves, so CoDel runs entirely at dequeue, with no lock shared with enqueue, which only stamps the arrival time (p. 46; sch_codel.c:122).

Sojourn time is also independent of link rate: the second act of the spine. The paper's Wi-Fi-like link steps down to 1 Mbit/s under an 830-packet buffer, one BDP at its nominal 100 Mbit/s. At 1 Mbit/s that buffer takes \(830 \times 12{,}000 / 10^6 = 9.96\) s to drain, and tail drop holds nearly that; CoDel's delay spikes, and it finds a new control point within 100 ms (Fig. 7, p. 48).

The paper's slip. Page 48 says the delays spike when the rates drop "at 50, 100, and 250 seconds". By the schedule on the same page and Fig. 7's own labels the drops are at 50, 100 and 200 s; at 250 s the rate jumps back to 100 Mbit/s. On p. 49, "512KB and 1.5MB" are the table's 512 Kbps and 1.5 Mbps.

The 100 ms is when CoDel starts acting; the backlog still drains at the new rate while drops ramp up, and the authors chose not to force it out faster (p. 48).

Five milliseconds and a hundred

Target is the standing delay CoDel accepts, 5 ms; interval is about a worst-case round trip, 100 ms (pp. 46–47). Below a 5 ms target utilisation suffered in some runs, and above it barely improved. A 100 ms interval worked for round trips from 10 ms to 1 s, best from 10 to 300 ms (p. 47).

Interval must cover a round trip, because good queue needs that long to drain (RFC 8289 §3.1).

On the bench codel cut TCP's RTT from a median of 655 ms to 42.2 ms (95th percentile 49.8), and the backlog from 513 packets to a median of 1. At 1.21 ms per frame the 5 ms target is about 4 frames.

The price was goodput: 8.35 Mbit/s against 9.54. One Reno flow halves from about 42 packets to 21, under the 33-packet pipe, and with 5 ms of queue to draw on, the link idles until the window regrows. That is the buffer-sizing rule from the queueing chapter: one flow needs about a BDP of buffer to ride through a halving, and \(\text{BDP}/\sqrt{n}\) with \(n\) flows. Four Reno flows through the same codel got 9.40 Mbit/s, split 2.30 to 2.42 (Jain's index 0.9996).

Target also has a floor: no drops with under one MTU queued (p. 46). On slow links one packet outlasts the target: 94 ms at 128 kbit/s, as in Fig. 6 (p. 47), and 12.1 ms after the bench's step to 1 Mbit/s, where codel settled near 45 ms of sojourn.

Since the paper. RFC 8289 (January 2018) derives the target from Kleinrock's power, the measure behind the queueing chapter's operating point: 5–10% of the round trip, and 5% of 100 ms is 5 ms (§3.2, §4.3). RFC 8290 §5.2.2 asks for at least one MTU's transmission time at the egress rate. Linux's Wi-Fi stack uses 20 ms (net/mac80211/tx.c:1620).

The next drop at interval over root count

After an interval above target CoDel drops a packet and schedules the next. The gap after the \(k\)-th drop is \(I/\sqrt{k}\), with \(I\) the interval, until a packet leaves below target (p. 46). The paper says this only in words; RFC 8289 §5.6 and codel_impl.h:93 write t + interval/sqrt(count).

\[ t_{k+1} = t_k + \frac{I}{\sqrt{k}}, \qquad t_k = I\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}} \approx I\,\bigl(2\sqrt{k} - 1.46\bigr). \]

With \(I = 100\) ms the drops fall at 0, 100, 170.7, 228.4, 278.4 and 323.2 ms. After 1 s there have been 33, now 17.4 ms apart. For large \(k\), \(k \approx (t/2I)^2\), so the drop rate \(dk/dt \approx t/(2I^2)\) climbs in a straight line: 50 more drops per second for every second the queue stays bad.

It starts at one drop per interval because a drop shows its effect a round trip later (RFC 8289 §3.3). For the square root the paper cites Mathis et al. "to get a linear change in throughput" and stops (p. 46). The checkable step, derived in the toggle from the Mathis formula: one Reno flow holds a rate proportional to the spacing \(\delta\) between its drops, \(X = 3\delta/(2\,\text{RTT}^2)\). At 723 packets/s and 42 ms that is one drop per 0.85 s; the bench measured one per 0.97 s.

Derivation · the schedule, and what the square root buys

The gap after drop \(j\) is \(I/\sqrt{j}\), so drop \(k\) comes at \(t_k = I\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} j^{-1/2}\). The sum is \(2\sqrt{n} - 1.46 + 1/(2\sqrt{n}) + \dots\) for \(n = k-1\), where \(-1.46\) is \(\zeta(1/2)\). Check: \(n = 32\) gives \(11.31 - 1.46 + 0.09 = 9.94\), so \(t_{33} = 994\) ms, and the gap after it is \(100/\sqrt{33} = 17.4\) ms. Inverting, \(k(t) \approx (t/2I + 0.73)^2\) and \(dk/dt \approx (t/2I + 0.73)/I\): 57 drops per second at \(t = 1\) s, rising by \(1/(2I^2) = 50\) per second each second.

Now the Mathis formula: a Reno flow with loss rate \(p\) sends \(X = \frac{1}{\text{RTT}}\sqrt{3/(2p)}\) packets per second. If the bottleneck drops one of its packets every \(\delta\) seconds, that is one in \(X\delta\), so \(p = 1/(X\delta)\). Substituting, \(X^2\,\text{RTT}^2 = \tfrac32 X\delta\), so

\[ X = \frac{3\,\delta}{2\,\text{RTT}^2}. \]

The rate a Reno flow can hold is proportional to the spacing between its drops: the square root in Mathis cancels when the controller sets a spacing instead of a probability. That is the linear relation the paper points at; it does not show the algebra. Bench check: the codel run carried 723 packets/s at a 42 ms RTT, so \(\delta = 2 \times 723 \times 0.042^2/3 = 0.85\) s; it measured 36 drops between 5 s and 40 s, one per 0.97 s.

The schedule shows best with traffic that does not back off. 11 Mbit/s of UDP into the 10 Mbit/s limit leaves an excess of 83 packets/s. One second into dropping, CoDel's count was 34 (model 33–34). Near count 69 the drop rate passed the excess (\(\sqrt{69}/0.1 = 83\) per second) and the queue began to drain; at count 286 a packet left below target. Over 10 s drops averaged 84 per second, the excess.

Two refinements live in the code (codel_impl.h:217–254). Re-entering within 16 intervals resumes with count set to count minus lastcount, the paper's "recent control level" (p. 46); the UDP episodes started at counts 1, 285, 15, 164, 20. And Linux refines \(1/\sqrt{\text{count}}\) by one Newton step per drop, so its second gap is 50 ms where the formula says 70.7 (the source lab).

Since the paper. RFC 8289 (January 2018) published the pseudocode and adopted Linux's re-entry rule (§5.5). Linux also marks an ECN-capable packet instead of dropping it when ecn is on (codel_impl.h:188, 220); Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN) is not in the paper. With the bench sender asking for ECN, fq_codel marked 26 packets in 20 s and dropped none.

Manage the queue that fills

An AQM helps only at the queue that actually fills, the fast-to-slow transition. Put CoDel in a home router in front of a cable modem, and the router's queue stays short while the modem's buffer, where gigabit Ethernet meets a 2 Mbit/s upstream, holds the standing queue in a sealed box (Fig. 10, p. 50).

The paper offers three ways out (p. 50): rate-limit the Ethernet just under the upstream rate so the queue forms in the router; put buffer management in the modem; or let the modem push back with Ethernet flow control. The first needs no vendor, but it gives away capacity, and if the upstream rate falls the queue returns to the modem.

The bench uses the first option: a 10 Mbit/s rate limiter on rtr's outgoing interface with the queue under it, where codel can see it. A bottleneck on the sender's own interface would stay nearly empty, because Linux TCP limits how much each socket may park in the local qdisc and driver; the chapter on the sending host's own queues takes that apart.

Your laptop has the same shape. tc qdisc show dev wlan0 prints noqueue, because mac80211 marks the interface IFF_NO_QUEUE (net/mac80211/iface.c:1621) and keeps its own per-station queues, with CoDel on each flow at 20 ms and 100 ms, ECN on (tx.c:1618–1621). Uploads are managed there; downloads queue at the access point or beyond.

Since the paper. The "known mitigations" for mixing acks and data (p. 49) became flow queueing: fq_codel hashes each flow to one of 1024 queues, runs CoDel on each, and serves them in turn by bytes, queues that just became active first (RFC 8290 §1.3, §4). It went into Linux 3.5 together with CoDel (commits 76e3cc126bb2, 4b549a2ef4be); RFC 8289 §1 recommends it. On the bench a ping beside the Reno flow peaked at 52 ms through codel and at 41.3 ms through fq_codel (Diagram 3.3).

Real systems

Meet the system · The Linux qdisc layer and fq_codel, plus mac80211's own fq_codel

What it is. A qdisc is the object between the IP stack and a driver where packets wait their turn; the packet-path dossier's transmit chapter shows where it sits (Transmit: from sendmsg to the wire). codel and fq_codel run this paper's algorithm. fq_codel is this laptop's default, and mac80211, the Wi-Fi stack, runs the same CoDel code inside itself.

Why it exists. Dave Taht and Eric Dumazet put both qdiscs into Linux in May 2012, the month the paper first appeared in ACM Queue (Linux 3.5). The kernel's own default is still pfifo_fast (net/sched/sch_generic.c:37); systemd sets fq_codel in /usr/lib/sysctl.d/50-default.conf:48.

The parts you need.

  • codel: one FIFO with CoDel at dequeue; tc -s prints count, lastcount, ldelay (the last sojourn), drop_next and dropping (sch_codel.c:258–282).
  • fq_codel: 1024 flow queues, each with its own CoDel state; tc -s adds new_flows_len and old_flows_len (queues on the just-active and the backlogged list), new_flow_count and ecn_mark (sch_fq_codel.c:630–641).
  • mac80211's queues: per station and traffic class, CoDel at 20 ms and 100 ms with ECN (net/mac80211/tx.c:1618–1621, Linux 4.8). So wlan0 has no qdisc: noqueue.

Where this chapter lives inside it. Sojourn time is ldelay = now - enqueue time (codel_impl.h:123); the minimum over an interval is the single variable first_above_time (lines 135–142). Target and interval are the 5 ms and 100 ms of codel_params_init() (lines 56–57); the one-MTU floor is *backlog <= params->mtu (line 129), with mtu the device MTU plus 14 header bytes (sch_codel.c:208). The control law is codel_control_law() (lines 92–102), visible as count and drop_next, and the recent-control-level rule is lastcount (lines 237–251). Managing the right queue is the qdisc's position, and why wlan0 shows noqueue. Flow queueing is the list walk in __fq_codel_dequeue() (sch_fq_codel.c:284–322). Overflow at the packet limit counts in drop_overlimit, never in CoDel's state (sch_codel.c:127).

Hands-on · Run the control law by hand

~30 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil, or a Python prompt

Task. CoDel has just entered its dropping state, interval 100 ms. List the first ten drop times, then the count and gap at 1 s. The episode ends after drop 12; 300 ms later CoDel enters again: with what count, and how soon is the next drop? Last, the drop spacing one Reno flow at 825 packets/s and a 45 ms RTT can live with.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: the paper's dynamic-link buffer holds 830 packets of 1500 bytes. How long does the last one wait at 100 Mbit/s, and at 1 Mbit/s?

Answer

About 0.1 s and 10 s. One packet is 12,000 bits: 0.12 ms at 100 Mbit/s, 12 ms at 1 Mbit/s. So \(830 \times 0.12\ \text{ms} = 99.6\) ms, one BDP for a 100 ms path, and \(830 \times 12\ \text{ms} = 9.96\) s, the "as much as 10 seconds" of p. 48. Near miss: dividing by bytes per second, eight times too little.

2. Predict the value: a home uplink runs at 2 Mbit/s (p. 50). How long does one 1514-byte frame take, and what does default fq_codel make of two queued frames?

Answer

6.1 ms per frame, \(1514 \times 8 / (2 \times 10^6)\). With two queued the second waits about 6 ms, over the 5 ms target, so after 100 ms of that CoDel drops; with one queued it never drops, the MTU floor (p. 46; codel_impl.h:129). The queue sits at one or two frames; RFC 8290 §5.2.2 would raise the target to a frame time. Near miss: "target 5 ms means under 5 ms of queue".

3. Here is a symptom: a home router runs fq_codel on its WAN port and tc -s qdisc shows dropped 0, backlog 0b 0p, yet video calls stall for two seconds whenever someone uploads a large file. What is happening, and where?

Answer

The queue that fills is beyond the router, most likely in the cable modem, where 1 Gbit/s Ethernet meets a few Mbit/s of upstream (Fig. 10, p. 50). The router's own queue never builds, so CoDel never sees a long sojourn. The paper's first option fixes it: rate-limit the WAN port just under the upstream so the queue forms in the router (p. 50). Near miss: "lower the target". CoDel cannot act on a queue it never holds.

4. Here is a symptom: with codel in place of the bench's 1000-packet pfifo, one Reno upload's goodput falls from 9.54 to 8.35 Mbit/s while its RTT falls from 655 to 42 ms. Is codel broken?

Answer

No. One Reno flow halves at each drop, from about 42 packets to 21, below the 33-packet pipe, and codel keeps only about 5 ms of queue to cover the gap, so the link idles until the window regrows. It is the buffer-sizing rule with \(n = 1\); four flows through the same codel reached 9.40 Mbit/s. Near miss: "codel drops too often". It dropped about once a second, as Mathis predicts.

5. Choose and defend: an access point's link rate moves between 5 and 300 Mbit/s with its client's signal. Size a tail-drop FIFO for it, or run CoDel with its defaults?

Answer

CoDel. A FIFO size is the right delay at one rate only: 2500 packets is a 100 ms BDP at 300 Mbit/s and 6 s at 5 Mbit/s, while 42 packets starves 300 Mbit/s, like the paper's 10-packet buffers that lost 75% of throughput (p. 48). Sojourn time ignores the rate, so 5 ms and 100 ms work at both ends (p. 46). The costs: some single-flow throughput, and at low rates a frame outlasts the target, which is why mac80211 uses 20 ms.

6. Predict the value: in the UDP run tc -s showed count 300 lastcount 285 as CoDel left its dropping state, and 100 ms later it entered again. With what count, and what gap does the control law give next?

Answer

Count 15, and \(100/\sqrt{15} = 25.8\) ms. Re-entering within 16 intervals, Linux sets count to \(300 - 285 = 15\), the drops the last episode added (codel_impl.h:237–241), the paper's "recent control level" (p. 46), as the bench row at 3.655 s shows. The measured gap was shorter, 6–10 ms: Linux refines \(1/\sqrt{\text{count}}\) from the old value with one Newton step (lines 242–246). Near miss: resuming at 300.

Recap

A window larger than the pipe leaves its excess standing at the bottleneck: bufferbloat, delay with no throughput. Length and busy time cannot tell it from a burst; the minimum sojourn over about a round trip can, with one timestamp per packet and one variable, all at dequeue. CoDel accepts 5 ms, waits 100 ms, then drops at gaps of \(\text{interval}/\sqrt{\text{count}}\) until a packet leaves below target, and it helps only where the queue builds.
