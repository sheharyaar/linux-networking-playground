Chapter 8 · before you read

Teaching NICs about time

~6 min before · Jacobson's 19 slides, Dumazet's cover letter and about 250 lines of kernel source (~1 h) · ~22 min after · HOST
A 24 Mbit/s flow whose RTT drops from 2.195 ms to 0.165 ms (the cover letter's two ss lines): the same flow, the same pacing rate, and one change in who decides when each packet leaves.

The question this chapter answers

On 21 September 2018 Eric Dumazet posted nine patches. The cover letter shows one TCP flow on a LAN, capped at 24 Mbit/s with SO_MAX_PACING_RATE, before and after the patches. Before: rtt:2.195. After: rtt:0.165. Both lines show the same cwnd (20), the same 4 unacknowledged packets, and the same 24 Mbit/s pacing rate. The network did not change. The sender changed where each packet's send time is decided, and with it the moment TCP starts the clock that measures its RTT.

The small-queues chapter left Linux in the 2013 arrangement. TCP computes a pacing rate. fq turns that rate into gaps and parks each fq flow in its throttled tree until the next packet is due. TCP hands an skb down and forgets it; fq alone decides when it leaves. Two months before the patches, Van Jacobson's netdev keynote called this split the old model of networking in miniature: the sender says what to send, and a queue decides when. His fix is to tell the queue, and in the end the network card, the time each packet should leave.

The readings are short: 19 slides, a one-page cover letter, and about 250 lines of v7.2 source. The slides give the reason, the cover letter gives the change and its measurement, and the source shows the mechanism in TCP, in fq, in the socket layer, and in a second qdisc, ETF, built for cards that can send at an exact instant.

Words you will meet

AFAP (as fast as possible)
Jacobson's name for the contract between a protocol and its device queue: the sender says what to send and how much, and the queue's drain rate decides how fast (slide 7). His footnote calls it work-conserving. The opposite is AFAN, "as fast as necessary".
earliest departure time (EDT)
A time written into each packet before which it must not leave. A qdisc or a network card holds the packet until then; it may leave later.
skb->tstamp
The 64-bit field of an skb that carries the departure time on the way out (on the way in, it holds the arrival time). TCP reads the same bits under the name skb_mstamp_ns.
CLOCK_TAI
International Atomic Time: a clock with no leap seconds, 37 s ahead of UTC once the system has been told the offset. Network cards that keep time with PTP, the Precision Time Protocol, count TAI.
SO_TXTIME
A socket option, since Linux 4.19, that lets a socket attach a departure time to each packet it sends, in a control message called SCM_TXTIME, on a clock the socket names.
ETF (earliest txtime first) qdisc
A qdisc that sorts packets by departure time, releases each one a fixed delta before its time, and drops any packet whose time has passed. It is made for cards that can launch a packet at a given instant.

Chapter 8 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. The cover letter's flow kept the same pacing rate, cwnd and unacknowledged count, yet its RTT fell from 2.195 to 0.165 ms. What did the RTT sample include before, and why not after? cover letter §2, §9

Answer

The time each skb waited in fq. TCP's RTT sample is the ack's arrival minus the skb's stamp (tcp_input.c:3647, 3714). Before the series the stamp was the hand-down time, and fq then held the skb for two 1.009 ms gaps: TSQ keeps two 2-segment skbs below TCP. After it, the stamp is TCP's departure time (tcp_output.c:1553–1555), so the wait falls outside the sample (§2). Near miss: nothing got faster.

2. Slide 14 says Carousel replaces a stack of queues with an EDT timestamp in every skb and a timing wheel. Which of the two did Linux adopt for fq, and what does fq do with a stamp too far in the future? slides 14–16; sch_fq.c

Answer

The timestamp. fq kept its per-flow queues, sorted by stamp when needed (sch_fq.c:508–538), and its throttled tree under one timer (lines 761–766). A stamp beyond the horizon, 10 s by default, is dropped into horizon_drops, or with horizon_cap moved to now plus the horizon (lines 615–623). The wheel exists only as a card offload no v7.2 driver offers.

3. ETF accepts only CLOCK_TAI, while SO_TXTIME lets any user pick CLOCK_MONOTONIC but needs CAP_NET_ADMIN for any other clock. Why the difference? sock.c:1625–1632, sch_etf.c:62–65

Answer

Each clock has a different consumer. ETF feeds cards whose clocks follow PTP, which counts TAI (sch_etf.c:62–65). fq runs on CLOCK_MONOTONIC and, since v5.8, has a horizon that bounds what a bad stamp can do, so any socket may use that clock: fq "has enough safe guards" (sock.c:1625–1626). Near miss: TAI is no more precise; it only skips leap seconds.

The chapter on one screen

Jacobson starts from the contract IP and TCP inherited. Reliable delivery limits how much is sent, never how fast, so the speed is whatever a device queue drains (slide 7). That runs the bottleneck at 100%, which queueing theory calls fragile (slides 8–9). Fat buffers and host links slower than the fabric hid the cost until about 2012 (slides 10–11). The thing that decides when now sits upstream of the wire and sees only local traffic (slides 12–13).

The cure is Carousel's pair: a departure time in every skb, and a time-ordered scheduler in front of or in the card (slide 14). A timing wheel with an event horizon is cheap, can answer "ETooFar", and turns qdiscs into pure computation (slides 15–18).

Dumazet's series took the first half. From 2013 to 2018 fq derived each flow's gaps from its pacing rate, which inflated TCP's RTT samples by the time packets sat in fq and added a quantum artifact to RPC latency (§1, §2, §10). From 4.20 TCP writes its own departure time into skb->tstamp and fq honours it (§6), a role SO_TXTIME and ETF had given the field a release earlier (§5). The 24 Mbit/s flow's RTT fell from 2.195 to 0.165 ms (§9). In v7.2 fq also drops or caps stamps beyond a 10 s horizon, any socket may stamp on CLOCK_MONOTONIC, and ETF wants CLOCK_TAI and drops late packets.

AFAP: the queue decides when

TCP's reliable delivery limits how much is sent and says nothing about how fast (slide 7). So the contract between a protocol and its device is a queue, and the speed is whatever it drains. Jacobson calls this AFAP and footnotes it as work-conserving, a virtue of DRR and fq_codel in the shapers chapter. A work-conserving link never idles while a packet waits, so a sender that keeps it full runs the bottleneck at 100% utilisation.

Slide 9 is the price, and the queueing chapter measured it on the bench's 10 Mbit/s link: the M/D/1 wait is 0.61 ms at \(\rho = 0.5\), 5.45 ms at 0.9 and 11.5 ms at 0.95. Slides 10–11: small bandwidth-delay products hid it until about 1995, then fat buffers and host links slower than the fabric until about 2012.

The slide's slip. Slide 9's right panel is labelled M/D/1, but its curve is \(\rho/(1-\rho)\), the M/M/1 wait. At 300 dpi it reads 0.34, 1.00, 3.01 and 8.9 packet times at 25, 50, 75 and 90% load, against \(\rho/(1-\rho) = 0.33,\ 1,\ 3,\ 9\). The M/D/1 wait is half that (Bertsekas and Gallager, Eq. 3.45, p. 187), as the queueing chapter's netem points show. The argument stands: both curves grow without limit as \(\rho \to 1\).

Slides 12–13 give the fix: "AFAP isn't working for us now because it's local and our problems aren't." Whatever decides when must see every outgoing packet, so it belongs just in front of the card or in it, and the card must be told when as well as what. In the spine, TCP knew the 24 Mbit/s it wanted but could only hand fq a rate. Spacing packets by time lets a sender sit at the queueing chapter's operating point, a full pipe with nothing queued.

TCP writes the departure time

Since Linux 4.20 every TCP socket keeps a departure clock, tcp_wstamp_ns, the "departure time for next sent data packet" (include/linux/tcp.h:283). Each transmission stamps the skb with the clock, or with now if the clock is behind (cover letter §6):

/* __tcp_transmit_skb(), tcp_output.c:1553–1555; the last line writes skb->tstamp */
prior_wstamp = tp->tcp_wstamp_ns;
tp->tcp_wstamp_ns = max(tp->tcp_wstamp_ns, tp->tcp_clock_cache);
skb_set_delivery_time(skb, tp->tcp_wstamp_ns, SKB_CLOCK_MONOTONIC);

skb->tstamp and TCP's skb_mstamp_ns are one union (include/linux/skbuff.h:909–912): the qdisc's departure time and the start of TCP's RTT sample are the same 64 bits. After each send, tcp_update_skb_after_send() (lines 1446–1468) adds \(\text{len}/\text{rate}\). At the 24 Mbit/s cap a two-segment skb holds 2,896 bytes of payload, so the clock moves \(2896 / (3 \times 10^6) = 965\) µs; the TCP lab's capture shows pairs 964 µs apart.

If a send was late, the clock was pulled forward to now, and the difference becomes a credit that shortens the next step by up to half (lines 1460–1464). A send 300 µs late gives the next skb a 665 µs step, and the flow is back on schedule. The commit calls it "a token bucket with a depth of half an skb" (a7a2563064e9), the shapers chapter's token bucket.

The stamp does not enforce itself. fq marks the socket fq-paced (sch_fq.c:399–401) and holds each skb until its stamp. Without fq, tcp_pacing_check() arms TCP's own hrtimer at tcp_wstamp_ns (lines 2815–2832), so the fallback with "very different behavior" (§3) enforces the same time: fq and fq_codel sent the same 964 µs pairs.

Since the cover letter. The series moved TCP and fq to CLOCK_TAI to match ETF. A week later, before 4.20 shipped, Dumazet moved them back to CLOCK_MONOTONIC (fb420d5d91c1, 28 September 2018) because TAI jumped forward by almost 50 years on systems that set the offset late. v7.2 stamps TCP on SKB_CLOCK_MONOTONIC (line 1555).

The RTT clock starts at departure

TCP's RTT sample is the ack's arrival minus the stamp of the oldest skb it newly acknowledges (tcp_clean_rtx_queue(), tcp_input.c:3647, 3714). Before 4.20 the stamp meant "handed to the qdisc"; since, "allowed to leave". That is the spine.

Take the "before" line apart (§9). At 24 Mbit/s, TSO autosizing gives \(3 \times 10^6 \gg 10 = 2929\) bytes: two segments, 3,028 bytes on the wire. TSQ allows two such skbs below TCP. The 2013 fq gave each skb a gap of \(\max(\text{length}, \text{quantum})/\text{rate}\) as it left, with a 3,028-byte quantum: \(3028 \times 8 / (24 \times 10^6) = 1.009\) ms. A new skb waits behind the other one, two gaps: 2.02 ms, which is Little's law with two skbs queued and one leaving every 1.009 ms. Add the after line's 0.165 ms: 2.18 ms, against the printed 2.195. The after line still shows unacked:4, because the same two skbs still wait. Their stamps now start the clock, so the sojourn time in fq (§2) stops counting as path delay.

The goodput, bytes_acked over busy, is \(36{,}897{,}937 \times 8 / 12.851\ \text{s} = 22.97\) Mbit/s before and 24.00 after: the old fq paced wire bytes, TCP paces payload, and \(24 \times 2896/3028 = 22.95\). send is \(\text{cwnd} \times \text{MSS} \times 8/\text{srtt}\): 105.5, then 1,404 Mbit/s, arithmetic on the RTT.

The bench replays both lines. fq's maxrate still gives each skb a gap of its wire length over the rate from the moment it leaves (sch_fq.c:796–833). Under fq maxrate 24mbit plus the socket cap, those 1.009 ms gaps outrun TCP's 965 µs steps, so TCP's stamps collapse to hand-down times: the old model. ss printed rtt:2.031/0.01 … minrtt:0.023 at 22.955 Mbit/s; with plain fq, rtt:0.027/0.002 … cwnd:20 ssthresh:20 … unacked:4 at 24.001.

Where it matters: an RTT inflated by the sender's own qdisc misleads the retransmit timer and any controller that models the path from its minimum RTT.

fq honours the stamp

fq reads skb->tstamp as "not before". At enqueue it converts the stamp to its monotonic clock, checks the horizon, and files the skb in its fq flow in stamp order (fq_enqueue(), sch_fq.c:594–662). A flow may send once the later of its head's stamp and its time_next_packet has passed (lines 761–766); until then it waits in the throttled tree, as in the small-queues chapter. fq never drops a packet for being late. The UDP lab's datagrams, stamped 1 ms apart, showed four cases:

  • On time: 1,000 stamps left a median 13 µs late (p99 25 µs); throttled 1000.
  • Out of order: groups of eight handed down in reverse left in stamp order. A stamp that goes backwards enters a per-flow red-black tree; in-order ones stay on an O(1) list (flow_queue_add(), lines 508–538).
  • In the past: stamps 45 ms old left at once.
  • Too far ahead: beyond horizon (10 s, line 1250) the packet is dropped and counted in horizon_drops, or with horizon_cap restamped to now plus the horizon and counted in horizon_caps (lines 615–623). Under horizon 2s horizon_cap, 20 stamps 5 s ahead and 1 ms apart became one time and left 0.9 µs apart.

Here Linux parted from Carousel: it kept fq's red-black trees and single timer, \(O(\log n)\) per waiting flow, over the Carousel chapter's O(1) timing wheel, and took only the wheel's horizon rule, with its two choices, drop or clamp. The wheel came back as a card feature, offload_horizon (v6.13, f26080d47007, whose commit cites Carousel). No v7.2 driver sets max_pacing_offload_horizon, so the bench prints offload_horizon 0us.

For TCP, EDT replaced the small-queues chapter's per-flow arithmetic: a stamped skb sets time_next_packet from a rate only under maxrate (lines 795–801). That removed the quantum artifact (§10), a gap of at least one quantum however small the packet; the EDT commit (ab408b6dc744) saw request-response p99 latency fall from 3,825 to 63 µs.

Since the talk. The per-flow tree came in v5.2 (eeb84aa0d0af) and the horizon in v5.8 (39d010504e6b), both for QUIC. v7.2 moves a throttled flow's time earlier when an earlier stamp arrives (fabcf8cad67b, lines 654–656) and converts CLOCK_TAI and CLOCK_REALTIME stamps to monotonic (c4f796c4f16b, lines 564–582).

Any socket can stamp, on fq's clock

SO_TXTIME names a clock and two flags, deadline mode and error reporting (sock.c:1613–1644). Each sendmsg() then carries an SCM_TXTIME control message with a 64-bit time in nanoseconds (lines 3054–3090; 0 means none), which the IP layer copies into skb->tstamp with the socket's clock (ip_output.c:1471–1474).

The clock rule is one if (lines 1628–1632): CLOCK_MONOTONIC is open to everyone, while CLOCK_REALTIME and CLOCK_TAI need CAP_NET_ADMIN in the user namespace that owns the socket's network namespace. The comment gives the reason: "CLOCK_MONOTONIC is only used by sch_fq, and this packet scheduler has enough safe guards", the horizon. As a normal user on this laptop, CLOCK_TAI fails with EPERM. In the rootless bench we own the user namespace, so it works, and fails again after setpriv --bounding-set=-net_admin.

The cover letter's user is QUIC (§8): write a burst of stamped datagrams and let fq space them, bounded only by fq's 100-packet flow limit, since UDP has no TSQ. BPF programs can write the stamp too, which is how the pods chapter holds a pod to a rate.

Since the cover letter. SO_TXTIME arrived in 4.19 (80b14dee2bea, Richard Cochran) with every clock privileged; 5.8 opened CLOCK_MONOTONIC "now sch_fq has horizon feature" (790709f24972). Until v7.2, fq read a TAI stamp as monotonic, decades ahead, so the horizon dropped it.

ETF: a launch time that can be missed

ETF came with SO_TXTIME (cover letter §5; 25db26a91364, Vinicius Costa Gomes and Jesus Sanchez-Palencia) for time-sensitive networking. It keeps one red-black tree of packets ordered by stamp and arms its watchdog at the earliest stamp minus delta. fq says "not before"; ETF says "between \(\text{stamp} - \delta\) and the stamp, or never".

validate_input_params() accepts only clockid CLOCK_TAI (sch_etf.c:62–65). is_packet_valid() (lines 75–106) wants a full socket with SO_TXTIME on the same clock and deadline mode, and a stamp no earlier than now or the last packet sent; anything else is dropped with TXTIME_INVALID_PARAM on the error queue. At dequeue (lines 253–294) a packet whose stamp has passed is dropped with TXTIME_MISSED and counted as an overlimit (line 224).

It attaches on the bench's veth without sudo; with no launch-time hardware a packet leaves on release, about delta early. 1,000 stamps 1 ms apart:

deltaleft, against the stamp (median)dropped as missed, of 1,000
500 µs497 µs early0
20 µs17 µs early0
10 µs7 µs early30 (5 in an earlier run)
0nothing left1,000, overlimits 1000

The release comes about 3 µs after \(\text{stamp} - \delta\), the timer's lateness, so a small delta misses. With offload the card launches at the stamp (igb, igc, ice, stmmac and enetc implement it in v7.2). ETF also drops everything without an SO_TXTIME socket: ping, ARP (hence a permanent neighbour entry), and 6 kernel-sent IPv6 packets in 8 idle seconds. So tc-etf(8) puts it "under another qdisc", one queue of an mqprio.

Since the sources (October 2026). On this laptop CLOCK_TAI equals CLOCK_REALTIME: systemd-timesyncd never sets the kernel's TAI offset. The 37 s matter once a card's PTP clock counts real TAI.

Real systems

Meet the system · SO_TXTIME and the ETF qdisc

What it is. A socket option and a qdisc, both added in Linux 4.19 (2018). SO_TXTIME lets an application put a departure time on each packet it sends; ETF holds such packets in time order and hands each to the card just before its time. fq honours the same time.

Why it exists. Time-sensitive networking: industrial and audio-video streams need frames on the wire at planned instants, and some cards can launch a frame at a time on their PTP clock (tc-etf(8), DESCRIPTION). TCP and QUIC servers came later (cover letter §5, §8).

The parts you need.

  • setsockopt(SOL_SOCKET, SO_TXTIME, {clockid, flags}), with the flags SOF_TXTIME_DEADLINE_MODE and SOF_TXTIME_REPORT_ERRORS.
  • An SCM_TXTIME control message on each sendmsg(): 64 bits of nanoseconds, copied into skb->tstamp.
  • A qdisc that honours it: fq on CLOCK_MONOTONIC, or ETF on CLOCK_TAI, optionally offloaded to the card.
  • The error queue (MSG_ERRQUEUE): an ETF drop arrives as SO_EE_ORIGIN_TXTIME with its code and stamp (sch_etf.c:135–160).

Where this chapter lives inside it. AFAP is the world before the field: an skb with tstamp 0, whose time a queue decides. TCP's departure clock writes the field SCM_TXTIME writes, so TCP is a built-in user on CLOCK_MONOTONIC. The RTT learning lives in the union: the bits the qdisc honours are the bits TCP measures from. fq honouring the stamp shows in tc -s as throttled, horizon_drops and horizon_caps. The clock rule is the clockid argument and its EPERM. ETF shows as dropped and overlimits, and on the error queue.

Try it (~5 min, no sudo, from the dossier folder):

cd labs/ch08-edt
../common/qnet.sh
ip netns exec rcv python3 txtime.py --listen &
tc -n snd qdisc del dev s0 root
tc -n snd qdisc add dev s0 root fq
ip netns exec snd python3 txtime.py --dst 10.0.2.1 --count 20 --burst --offset-ms 11000
tc -n snd -s qdisc show dev s0 | tail -1
tc -n snd qdisc del dev s0 root
tc -n snd qdisc add dev s0 root etf clockid CLOCK_TAI delta 500000
ip netns exec snd python3 txtime.py --dst 10.0.2.1 --clock tai --count 50 --offset-ms -50 --report-errors

What you should see. Real output, 2 October 2026:

txtime: clock=mono sent 20 of 20 datagrams of 200 B, stamps 1000 us apart, first 11005 ms after start, all handed down at once
 horizon_drops 20
txtime: clock=tai sent 0 of 50 datagrams of 200 B, stamps 1000 us apart, first -45 ms after start
txtime: 50 sendmsg() calls failed: ['ENOBUFS']
txtime: 50 SO_EE_ORIGIN_TXTIME errors on the error queue
  EINVAL TXTIME_INVALID_PARAM: 50

fq dropped all 20 at enqueue, 11 s being beyond its 10 s horizon. ETF refused all 50 past stamps, and the sender heard twice: ENOBUFS, and one error per packet with its stamp.

Clean up. exit the lab shell; the listener stops by itself after 60 s.

Stable: the control message, the error codes, ETF's clock rule and its drop when late. Drifting (as of October 2026, kernel 7.2, iproute2 7.2.0): the clocks fq converts, the CAP_NET_ADMIN rule, the drivers with ETF offload, and offload_horizon. tc-fq(8) omits horizon; tc qdisc add dev lo root fq help lists it.

Hands-on · Trace the departure clock, then predict the old model's RTT

~30 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil, then one CSV to check against

Task. (a) A socket capped at 24 Mbit/s sends 2,896-byte skbs: skb 1 at \(t = 0\) with the clock at 0, skb 2 at 0.1 ms, skb 3 at 2.4 ms (a late wake-up), skb 4 at 2.45 ms. Write each stamp and the clock after each send; then redo it with skb 3 at 3.0 ms. (b) Under the old model on a LAN, predict the RTT at caps of 12, 34 and 36 Mbit/s.

Done when. You can say how much time the flow loses in each case of (a), and your three RTTs are within 0.05 ms of labs/ch08-edt/data/rate-model-sweep.csv.

Hint 1 · direction

The clock never lags behind now: the stamp is the larger of the clock and now. The step after a send is the skb's length at the rate, minus a credit for lateness. For (b), use the RTT learning's decomposition: TSQ's two skbs, one gap each.

Hint 2 · mechanism

The step is \(2896 \times 10^9 / (3 \times 10^6) = 965{,}333\) ns. The credit is the stamp minus the clock before the send, and the step loses \(\min(\text{step}/2, \text{credit})\). For (b), \(\text{segs} = \max(2, \lfloor (\text{cap}/8 \gg 10)/1448 \rfloor)\) and the gap is \(\text{segs} \times 1514 \times 8 / \text{cap}\).

Hint 3 · nearly there

skb 3 in the first case: the clock is 1.931 ms and now is 2.4 ms, so the stamp is 2.4, the credit 0.469 ms, the step \(0.965 - 0.469 = 0.496\) ms, and the clock 2.896 ms. At 34 Mbit/s, \(4.25 \times 10^6 \gg 10 = 4150\) bytes is still two segments, so the gap shrinks.

Solution 
(a) all times in ms
skb  sent at  clock before  stamp  credit  step   clock after
 1   0.000    0.000         0.000  0       0.965  0.965
 2   0.100    0.965         0.965  0       0.965  1.931
 3   2.400    1.931         2.400  0.469   0.496  2.896
 4   2.450    2.896         2.896  0       0.965  3.861
skb 3 at 3.000: stamp 3.000, credit 1.069,
                step 0.965 - min(0.483, 1.069) = 0.483, clock 3.483
skb 4 then gets 3.483 instead of 2.896: 0.587 ms lost for good

(b) cap  rate>>10  segs  gap       RTT predicted  bench
    12   1,464 B   2     2.019 ms  4.04 ms        4.051 ms
    34   4,150 B   2     0.712 ms  1.42 ms        1.431 ms
    36   4,394 B   3     1.009 ms  2.02 ms        2.026 ms
payload at 36: 36 x 1448/1514 = 34.43 Mbit/s (bench 34.438)

Expected output. The table above. In the first case skb 4's stamp, 2.896 ms, is exactly three steps after skb 1, so the late send cost nothing. In the second, the lateness (1.069 ms) is more than half a step (0.483 ms), and the excess, 0.587 ms, is gone.

What this shows. The credit lets TCP make up lateness of up to half a step and no more, which is the half-skb token bucket in the learning on writing the departure time (tcp_output.c:1460–1464). Part (b) is the RTT learning's decomposition: the inflation is two gaps, and a gap is the skb's wire size over the cap, so a cap with the same skb size and a higher rate shows a smaller RTT. 34 Mbit/s shows less than either 24 or 36 (cover letter §9).

Common wrong turn. Computing the old model's gap from payload, 2,896 bytes. That gives 1.93 ms at 24 Mbit/s and a goodput of exactly 24 Mbit/s, so it cannot explain the cover letter's 22.97. The 2013 fq paced the bytes it put on the wire.

Hint 1 · direction

fq's rule is "not before", with one drop, for stamps too far ahead. ETF's rule is a window that closes at the stamp. Before each run, say which qdisc drops which bad stamp.

Hint 2 · mechanism

Rerun the capture and send lines with --reverse 8 --lead-ms 10, then --offset-ms -50. Task. On a path with no delay, pace one CUBIC flow at 24 Mbit/s three ways, each with the socket cap: fq maxrate 24mbit (fq derives the gaps), plain fq (fq honours TCP's stamps), and fq_codel (TCP's own timer). Predict the RTT, pair gap and payload rate for each, then read ss -tin and a capture.

cd labs/ch08-edt                            # from the dossier folder
../common/qnet.sh

tc -n snd qdisc del dev s0 root

sleep 6; ip netns exec snd ss -tin dst 10.0.2.1
sleep 5; python3 gaps.py t.pcap --skip 2

Done when. You have three rows next to your predictions and can say which row is the cover letter's "before" line, and why.

Hint 1 · direction

The RTT learning: which stamp does each setup's RTT sample start from, and how long does an skb wait in fq beyond that stamp?

Hint 2 · mechanism

For the next run, tc -n snd qdisc del dev s0 root, then tc -n snd qdisc add dev s0 root fq (or fq_codel), and rerun the last five lines with new file names. Under maxrate a gap is 3,028 wire bytes at the rate; under TCP's clock it is 2,896 payload bytes.

Hint 3 · nearly there

Predictions: fq maxrate: \(2 \times 1.009 = 2.02\) ms, pairs 1,009 µs apart, \(24 \times 2896/3028 = 22.95\) Mbit/s. TCP's stamps: the path's 0.03 ms, 965 µs, 24.00 Mbit/s.

Solution

Expected output (the author's runs, 2 October 2026; labs/ch08-edt/data/tcp24-transcript.txt has the full ss lines and tc -s):

s0 root            ss rtt       median   unacked  pair gap  Mbit/s
fq maxrate 24mbit  2.031/0.01   2.026 ms  4       1008 us   22.955
fq                 0.027/0.002  0.028 ms  4       964 us    24.001

What this shows. The maxrate row is the 2018 "before": fq derives each gap from wire bytes when the skb leaves, TCP's stamps fall behind and become hand-down times, and two TSQ skbs wait two gaps inside every RTT sample. The fq row is the "after" line, down to cwnd:20 ssthresh:20 … unacked:4. The fq_codel row is TCP's own timer enforcing the same clock: it holds each skb itself, so nothing waits below TCP and each pair is acked before the next is due (the RTT learning; cover letter §2, §3, §9).

Common wrong turn. Leaving tcp_tso_rtt_log at its default of 9. On a 0.03 ms path, TSO autosizing's RTT bonus makes 64 KB skbs of 45 segments, so the maxrate run shows rtt:45.435 and EDT sends 45-packet bursts every 21.7 ms. And do not switch qdiscs with tc qdisc replace dev s0 root fq: on an existing fq, replace keeps the maxrate you did not name.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: under the old model, a flow capped at 48 Mbit/s on a 0.03 ms LAN path. Segments per skb, RTT in ss, payload rate?

Answer

Four segments, about 2.03 ms, 45.9 Mbit/s. \(6 \times 10^6 \gg 10 = 5859\) bytes and \(\lfloor 5859/1448 \rfloor = 4\), so each skb is 6,056 wire bytes and each gap \(6056 \times 8 / (48 \times 10^6) = 1.009\) ms; TSQ's two skbs make 2.02 ms. Payload: \(48 \times 1448/1514 = 45.9\). The bench: 2.025 ms and 45.906 (rate-model-sweep.csv). Near miss: twice the rate, half the wait. At multiples of 12 Mbit/s the gap is 1.009 ms.

2. Predict the value: at 96 Mbit/s TCP's skbs are 11,584 bytes, and one send happens 2 ms late. How long is the clock's next step, and how much time is lost for good?

Answer

0.483 ms, and 1.517 ms lost. The full step is \(11{,}584 \times 8 / (96 \times 10^6) = 0.965\) ms. The 2 ms credit may cut at most half the step (tcp_output.c:1460–1464), so \(2 - 0.483 = 1.517\) ms is never made up. Near miss: "it sends 2 ms of data at once to catch up". The half-skb bucket (a7a2563064e9) stops that burst.

3. Here is a symptom: an operator caps uploads with fq maxrate 24mbit on the uplink. A LAN flow's ss -ti shows rtt:7.076/0.005 … pacing_rate 88400920bps … unacked:14 … minrtt:0.03, and an RTT alert fires. The network is idle. What is going on?

Answer

fq derives each gap from maxrate as the skb leaves, so TCP's stamps are only hand-down times and the wait in fq lands in every RTT sample. With no socket cap, TCP paces at its own 88 Mbit/s, so autosizing builds 7-segment skbs (\(11.05 \times 10^6 \gg 10 = 10{,}791\) bytes) and TSQ keeps two in fq: \(2 \times 7 \times 1514 \times 8 / (24 \times 10^6) = 7.07\) ms. The bench printed this line. With SO_MAX_PACING_RATE TCP stamps at the cap: 0.03 ms. Near miss: a network queue; minrtt rules it out.

4. Here is a symptom: after a clock bug, a UDP sender's SO_TXTIME stamps run 15 s ahead. With fq's defaults its packets vanish and horizon_drops climbs. With horizon_cap they arrive 10 s late, in bursts that overflow the receiver. Explain both.

Answer

Both are fq's horizon rule (sch_fq.c:615–623). A stamp more than 10 s ahead is dropped unless horizon_cap is set (line 1251). A capped stamp becomes now plus 10 s, so it takes its time from the moment it was handed down: datagrams written together get one time and leave together. On the bench, 20 datagrams stamped 1 ms apart left 0.9 µs apart. Near miss: fq's per-flow limit, which shows as flows_plimit.

5. Here is a symptom: someone puts etf clockid CLOCK_TAI delta 0 on a host's root to send "exactly on time". Every packet of the timed application is dropped, ping prints "No buffer space available", and overlimits equals the application's packet count. What happened, and what fixes it?

Answer

With delta 0 the watchdog fires at the stamp, so any lateness expires the packet: dropped as TXTIME_MISSED and counted as an overlimit (sch_etf.c:265–270, 224). Ping has no SO_TXTIME socket, so ETF drops it at enqueue and sendmsg() returns ENOBUFS (lines 85–89). On the bench delta 0 dropped 1,000 of 1,000. Fix: a delta above the timer's lateness (20 µs held here), and ETF on one mqprio queue (tc-etf(8)). Near miss: blaming the clock.

6. Choose and defend: a user-space media sender wants packets 1 ms apart from a laptop with an ordinary card. CLOCK_MONOTONIC through fq, or CLOCK_TAI through ETF?

Answer

fq. It needs no privilege on CLOCK_MONOTONIC (sock.c:1628–1632), a late packet still leaves, ARP and other traffic pass, and the bench's packets left a median 13 µs after their stamps. ETF needs CAP_NET_ADMIN and its own queue, drops whatever misses its window, and without launch-time hardware sends delta early. The trade-off: fq promises "not before", never an instant. Choose ETF when the card launches at a PTP time.

Recap

Under AFAP a queue decides when, running the bottleneck at 100% with only local traffic in view. Jacobson's cure, after Carousel, is a departure time in every skb. Since 4.20 TCP writes its departure clock into skb->tstamp on CLOCK_MONOTONIC, advancing it by length over rate with a half-skb credit, and fq honours it as "not before" up to a 10 s horizon. TCP's RTT sample starts at that stamp, so the cover letter's 2.195 ms, two TSQ skbs of 1.009 ms each, became 0.165 ms. SO_TXTIME lets any socket stamp on fq's clock; ETF wants CLOCK_TAI and drops what misses its window.
