Chapter 6 · before you read

TCP pacing

~6 min before · PDF pp. 1–9 (~1 h) · ~22 min after · CC
Fifty paced Reno flows start together across a 57 Mbit/s, 100 ms bottleneck with a 312-packet drop-tail buffer, overflow it in the same round trip, and collapse together (Fig. 8, PDF p. 6).

The question this chapter answers

The congestion-avoidance chapter left the ack clock with two weak points: it cannot start itself, and it trusts the spacing of the acks. In slow start each ack releases two packets, so every round leaves the sender at twice the bottleneck's rate and parks half a window in its queue. The obvious repair is a timer that spreads each window evenly over the round trip. Queueing theory backs it, because evenly spaced arrivals wait less than bunched ones (the queueing-maths chapter), and the small-queues chapter showed Linux computing a pacing rate for every socket.

In 2000 Amit Aggarwal, Stefan Savage and Thomas Anderson put that timer into the ns simulator's TCP Reno and measured it. In most of the realistic cases they tried, pacing gave lower throughput and higher latency (PDF p. 1). The spine of this chapter is their sharpest case. Fifty flows start together across a 57 Mbit/s, 100 ms bottleneck whose drop-tail buffer holds 312 packets, a quarter of the 1,250-packet pipe. The paced flows' windows add up to about 3,300 packets, then fall to about 200 within five round trips. The unpaced flows peak near 2,400 and fall less far (Fig. 8, PDF p. 6).

So the question is how removing bursts can make a loss-driven sender slower. The answer lies in when, and to whom, the network reports congestion. The chapter then asks how much of it applies to Linux, which paces today, at different rates, and only when something enforces it.

Words you will meet

slow-start burst
In slow start each ack releases two packets, so a round leaves the sender at twice the bottleneck's rate, and a round that ends with window \(W\) leaves \(W/2\) packets queued (PDF p. 2).
ack compression
Acks that queue behind data on the return path lose their spacing and arrive in a clump, so the ack clock releases a burst (PDF p. 3).
paced Reno
The paper's sender: Reno plus a timer that sends the next packet every \(\text{RTT}/\text{window}\), for the whole life of the connection. The window decides how much to send and the timer decides when (PDF p. 3).
late congestion signal
Paced traffic builds no queue until the link is full, so a loss-driven sender hears of congestion only after it has overloaded the link (PDF pp. 5–6).
mixing of flows
Paced flows spread their packets over the whole round trip, so in a shared FIFO each flow's packets sit between everyone else's (PDF p. 6).
congestion epoch
One overflow of the bottleneck buffer, plus the round trip in which the senders find out. Flows that lose a packet in it lose in the same epoch.
synchronized drops
Most flows lose packets in the same congestion epoch and cut their windows together, so the link swings from overloaded to underused (PDF p. 1).
desynchronization
Flows lose in different epochs, so their sawtooths are out of step and the sum of their windows swings less. The paper writes de-synchronization (PDF p. 6).

Chapter 6 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. Pacing builds no queue until the link is full. Why is that a problem for a sender that learns about congestion only from loss? PDF pp. 3–6

Answer

Because the only signal is a drop, and a drop-tail FIFO drops only when it is full. Evenly spaced arrivals keep the queue near empty until the windows exceed the pipe (Fig. 1, PDF p. 3), so the first drop comes after overload, and slow start doubles once more while the loss travels back: Fig. 8's paced sum reached about 3,300, twice pipe plus buffer (PDF p. 6). Near miss: pacing does not slow the signal down. It removes the early losses that bursts gave some flows.

2. Why do paced flows lose packets in the same round trip, while unpaced Reno flows lose at different times? PDF pp. 5–6

Answer

Because the signal is late and the flows are mixed (PDF pp. 5–6). When the buffer finally overflows, every paced flow is still in slow start and has packets spread over the whole round trip, so the overflow catches all of them. Unpaced clusters overflow it early, for the few flows whose clusters overlapped, and those back off while the rest grow. On the bench: eight of eight paced flows in the first congestion epoch, six of eight unpaced. Near miss: both kinds started together; only the paced ones stayed in step.

3. One paced flow shares a bottleneck with bursty Reno flows. Why does it finish later, when its packets are the better behaved? PDF pp. 1, 7–8

Answer

Because a loss-driven sender halves once per loss event, and spread packets collect events: any Reno burst that fills the queue is "very likely" to catch one of the paced flow's packets, while a Reno cluster either misses the full queue or loses several packets for one halving (PDF pp. 1, 7). In Fig. 14, with half the flows paced, they took 3.75 s against 2.7 s. On the bench every paced 300-packet flow lost in the first epoch, two thirds of the unpaced ones. Near miss: it is not more lost packets. The paced flows retransmitted a third as many.

The chapter on one screen

The paper starts from queueing theory, which says bursty arrivals bring more delay and loss, and from TCP's four sources of bursts: slow start, the ack of a retransmission, ack compression, and windows that travel as clusters (PDF pp. 1–3). Pacing sends each window over the round trip on a timer, one packet every \(\text{RTT}/\text{window}\) (PDF p. 3). Fig. 1 states the hope, no queue until the link is full, and the next paragraph the problem: feedback is delayed until the network is saturated (PDF pp. 3–4).

In ns, with 576-byte packets and drop-tail FIFOs (PDF p. 4), one flow with a quarter-BDP buffer gains from pacing at start-up, because Reno's slow start overflows the buffer at twice its size. The gain grows with the BDP and goes away once the buffer is half of it (Figs 3–5, PDF pp. 4–5).

With fifty flows the result turns over (PDF pp. 5–6). Paced flows lose late and together, through late congestion signals and mixing, and their aggregate window collapses, while Reno's clusters lose early and unevenly. In steady state pacing desynchronizes, and with mixed RTTs it is fairer (PDF pp. 6–7). With flows arriving over time, every new slow start brings synchronized drops (Figs 12–13). A paced flow among Reno flows finishes later (Figs 14–15, PDF pp. 7–8). The remedy is a queue manager that reads even a small queue as congestion: RED with a low threshold, or BLUE (Fig. 16, PDF p. 8).

Half a window in the queue

In slow start, a round that ends with window \(W\) sends \(W\) packets in the time the bottleneck needs for \(W/2\), so it leaves \(W/2\) of them queued (PDF p. 2). With a buffer of \(B\) packets, unpaced Reno overflows it once \(W/2 > B\), near \(W = 2B\). Paced Reno builds no queue until the pipe is full, so it overflows near pipe plus \(B\) (PDF p. 5).

This is the slow-start cost from the congestion-avoidance chapter, meeting a buffer smaller than the pipe. The paper adds three sources (PDF pp. 2–3): the ack of a retransmission that fills a hole, ack compression on two-way traffic, and windows that travel as clusters, which make bigger bursts where they overlap.

The paper's single flow (Fig. 4, PDF p. 4) has a pipe of \(5 \times 10^6 \times 0.1/(576 \times 8) = 108.5\) packets and a 25-packet buffer. Reno should overflow near 50: its line tops out near 62, times out, and climbs one packet per round trip until about 150 round trips. Paced Reno should overflow near \(108 + 25 = 133\): it spikes to about 213 and is on its sawtooth by about 90 (PDF p. 5).

On the bench, with 576-byte packets, the 10 Mbit/s, 40 ms path holds 85 packets, and the router's buffer was 21. One unpaced Reno flow overflowed it in the round from 32 to 64, and the router dropped 34 packets. Paced by fq, the queue stayed at 0–3 packets until the round from 64 to 128, past \(85 + 21 = 106\), and 74 were dropped.

Fig. 5 gives the condition: the gain grows with the BDP and goes away once the buffer is half the BDP, the \(W/2\) queue of a window that has just filled the pipe. There pacing does slightly worse, because it "lags behind by a round trip time" (PDF p. 5).

The paper's slip. Footnote 1 (PDF p. 5) sets Fig. 5's flows to one sawtooth, \(T_{cycle} = RTT^2 \cdot BW/\text{packet size}\), "proposed in [21]". For Fig. 4's flow that is \(0.1^2 \times 5 \times 10^6/4608 = 10.85\) s, 108 round trips, but Fig. 4 repeats every 75, with Reno peaks near 150, 225, 300, 376 and 451. The footnote is Mathis et al.'s simpler bound with \(C = 1\), given for receivers that delay acks (p. 69). With an ack for every packet, as here (PDF p. 4), their derivation (p. 68) gives \(\tfrac23\, RTT^2 \cdot BW/MSS = 72\) round trips, which fits the figure. So each Fig. 5 flow ran about one and a half sawtooths.

Derivation · the cycle from Mathis et al.'s sawtooth

A sawtooth from \(W/2\) to \(W\) grows one packet per round trip, so it lasts \(W/2\) round trips and carries \(\tfrac38 W^2\) packets (Mathis et al. p. 68). Its average rate is \(\tfrac38 W^2 / (\tfrac{W}{2} \cdot RTT) = \tfrac34 W/RTT\) packets per second. Set that equal to the link, \(BW/MSS\), so \(W = \tfrac43\, BW \cdot RTT / MSS\), and the cycle is \(\tfrac{W}{2}\, RTT = \tfrac23\, RTT^2 BW / MSS\). With \(BW \cdot RTT/MSS = 108.5\) packets, that is 72.3 round trips. The footnote's version drops the \(\tfrac23\); it equals the simpler bound \(BW = MSS/(RTT\sqrt p)\) with one loss per cycle of \(1/p\) packets.

No queue, then too much

Paced traffic keeps the bottleneck queue near empty until the windows add up to more than the pipe, so a loss-driven sender's first signal comes when it is already overloading the link, and in slow start it keeps doubling while the loss travels back (PDF pp. 3–6). That is the late congestion signal.

In Fig. 1 (PDF p. 3), perfectly spaced arrivals see no queueing until the load reaches capacity: the queueing-maths chapter's operating point, pipe full and nothing queued, where Kleinrock's power peaks. Pacing puts traffic right there, where a loss-driven sender hears nothing: feedback "is delayed until the network is saturated" (PDF p. 4).

On the spine, pipe 1,250 plus buffer 312 is 1,562 packets. Paced, nobody loses before the sum of windows passes 1,562, and one more doubling gives \(2 \times 1{,}562 = 3{,}124\); Fig. 8 shows about 3,300 (PDF p. 6). Reno's sum peaks near 2,400, because some flows hit each other's bursts early and backed off.

On the bench, eight flows started together (pipe plus buffer 106), and the late signal shows as a clock time: the first flow entered recovery 0.183 s after the start unpaced and 0.244 s paced by fq, in all five runs of each. By then the paced sum of windows was 188, against 157.

The remedy follows from the cause (PDF p. 8): with pacing, "even a small queue size should be interpreted as congestion". RED worked best with a low minimum threshold (5 packets), a high maximum and a low top drop probability (Fig. 16). CoDel's 5 ms target is a rule of the same shape.

Since the paper. BLUE, the paper's other suggestion, is in Linux as its per-flow variant sch_sfb, whose header cites the paper's [9] (net/sched/sch_sfb.c:8–12). The chapter where the sender models the path (chapter 10) takes the other way out: a sender that measures the pipe can stop where Fig. 1 is still flat.

Mixed packets, shared losses

Through one drop-tail FIFO, paced flows lose together. Their packets are mixed, so an overflow catches a packet from every flow and every flow halves; Reno's clusters lose a few flows at a time (PDF pp. 5–6). In steady state the paper finds the opposite, desynchronization: pacing randomizes who loses, so the flows drift out of step (PDF p. 6).

In five runs of each, all eight paced flows lost in the first congestion epoch every time, six of eight unpaced; TCP's own timer gave the same eight. With a buffer of one pipe the unpaced flows lost together too, as the paper says of Reno once "the bottleneck queue absorbs its burstiness" (PDF p. 6).

The collapse did not follow. Fig. 8's paced sum fell to about 200, four packets per flow, which is what timeouts leave; many TCPs without selective acks (SACK) "suffer from timeouts when they experience multiple packet losses within one round trip time" (Mathis et al. p. 69). Linux repairs several losses in one round trip and halves once, and half of 188 is still above the 85-packet pipe: no paced run timed out, and the first two seconds carried 7.77 Mbit/s paced against 7.66 unpaced. With SACK off (net.ipv4.tcp_sack=0) only the unpaced flows timed out.

Steady state is where flows in step cost most: the buffer-sizing rule, \(\text{BDP}/\sqrt n\), assumes \(n\) sawtooths out of step, and flows in step need a full BDP (Appenzeller et al. p. 284). After three seconds the paced sum of windows was a little larger and steadier, 97 against 95 packets, swinging 13% against 16%, with 8.41 against 8.24 Mbit/s: the paper's outcome. Counted by epoch, though, half or more of the paced flows still lost together in 73% of epochs, against 60%.

Since the paper. The paper cites SACK (its [22]) but simulates Reno without it. Linux adds proportional rate reduction (RFC 6937, net/ipv4/tcp_input.c:2962–2985) and RACK, which finds lost packets by their send times (RFC 8985, default at tcp_ipv4.c:3475): a synchronized loss becomes one halving per flow.

A paced flow among bursty ones

A paced flow sharing a drop-tail bottleneck with Reno flows is "very likely" to lose a packet whenever a Reno burst fills the queue, because its packets are everywhere in the round trip; a Reno cluster either misses the full queue or loses several packets at once, for one halving (PDF pp. 1, 7). A loss-driven sender halves once per loss event, so the paced flow finishes later.

In Fig. 14 (PDF p. 8), twenty 300-packet flows start together behind a quarter-BDP buffer; with half of them paced, the paced flows take about 3.75 s and the Reno flows about 2.7 s. Fig. 15 puts Reno over paced latency at 0.65–0.76 for most mixes, and as low as 0.26 when flows arrive over time (line C). The early adopter loses (PDF p. 1).

Derivation · why spread packets draw more loss events

Say Reno bursts keep the queue full for a fraction \(q\) of each round trip, and a flow sends \(k\) packets per round trip. Spread at random times, at least one of them meets a full queue with probability \(1 - (1-q)^k\). A cluster arrives at once, so it is hit with probability about \(q\), and then loses several packets. For \(k = 10\) and \(q = 0.1\): \(1 - 0.9^{10} = 0.65\) against 0.1. Each hit is one halving, and the Mathis formula's \(p\) counts these events, not packets. The formalization is mine; the paper argues it in words (PDF pp. 1, 7).

The bench ran the same mixes with eight 300-packet flows over pfifo, the paced ones paced by TCP's own timer, 24 runs per mix (Diagram 6.3). In the first congestion epoch every paced flow lost, against 66–71% of the unpaced flows in the same runs: the paper's mechanism, measured. The paced flows then trailed, 72 packets acked by 0.5 s against 105 with four of eight paced, and finished 9–14% later while they were the minority: Reno over paced completion was 0.88–0.92, against Fig. 15's 0.65–0.76.

Long flows turned it around. Over 15 s with four of eight paced, the paced flows averaged 1.18 Mbit/s each and the unpaced 0.94, with fewer loss events (31 against 38) and retransmissions, as at every mix in Diagram 6.3. After slow start, Linux's pacing is a ceiling over the ack clock, so the paced flows are no longer spread over the whole round trip, while unpaced bursts keep overflowing the queue themselves, the "unlucky" clusters that "suffer multiple losses in a burst" (PDF p. 1). On Linux the early adopter pays at start-up and gains later.

Which pacer runs on Linux, and how fast

Linux computes a pacing rate for every TCP socket on every ack, and enforces it in three cases only: fq is the qdisc, the socket set SO_MAX_PACING_RATE, or its congestion control asked. The rate is 200% of \(\text{cwnd} \times \text{MSS}/\text{srtt}\) while \(\text{cwnd} < \text{ssthresh}/2\) and 120% after, where the paper paced at exactly 100% (net/ipv4/tcp_input.c:1154–1159; defaults at tcp_ipv4.c:3501–3502).

The mark is sk_pacing_status: NONE, NEEDED or FQ (include/net/sock.h:612–616). SO_MAX_PACING_RATE sets NEEDED (net/core/sock.c:1263–1266), as one in-tree congestion-control module does when it starts, the one the path-model chapter reads. NEEDED turns on TCP's own hrtimer (tcp.h:1614; tcp_pacing_check(), tcp_output.c:2815); fq sets FQ when it meets the socket (sch_fq.c:451–453). With NONE, TCP does not even advance the next send time (tcp_output.c:1451).

That is the trap. ss -ti prints pacing_rate for every socket, because tcp_get_info() copies it whatever the mark (net/ipv4/tcp.c:4229–4231). A CUBIC or Reno socket over pfifo or fq_codel shows a rate and is not paced. fq's throttled counter, or a capture, is the proof.

The ratios change what pacing is. The paper's timer is the clock, so a paced flow "lags behind by a round trip time" (PDF p. 5). Linux's 200% lets slow start double each round while spreading it; after that, 120% sits above the flow's average rate, so the ack clock sets the pace and fq holds an skb only when acks bring it early (7,713 times for about 16,000 skbs in an eight-flow run). And Linux paces skbs of at least two segments (TSO autosizing), where the paper paced packets.

Both ratios at 100, the paper's rate, made the eight fq-paced flows worse on every count. The first loss came at 0.287 s instead of 0.244 s, with windows up to 30 packets ahead of what had been sent; they peaked at 279 against 188; retransmissions rose from 548 to 639; goodput fell from 8.41 to 8.21 Mbit/s.

So the paper's two mechanisms still apply to any loss-driven sender behind a shared drop-tail FIFO; Linux changed the rates and the recovery that made Fig. 8 collapse. Pacing pays off once the sender stops waiting for loss, in the chapter where the sender models the path (chapter 10). The rate's history is in the small-queues chapter.

Hands-on · Where each sender first overflows, by hand

~30 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil

Task. For the paper's single flow (5 Mbit/s, 100 ms, 576-byte packets, 25-packet buffer) and for the bench (10 Mbit/s, 40 ms, 590-byte frames on the wire, 21-packet buffer), compute the pipe, the slow-start round in which unpaced Reno first overflows the buffer and the window at that first drop, and the same for a paced sender. Then compute the paper's sawtooth period three ways: footnote 1, Mathis et al.'s derivation with an ack for every packet, and Fig. 4. Last, predict Fig. 8's paced peak.

Done when. You have a two-row table of pipe and both drop windows, three periods and one peak, and can say why the bench's measured ssthresh, 51 unpaced and 86 paced, fits your drop windows.

Hint 1 · direction

The half-window learning gives both drop points. Slow start works in rounds of doubling windows: 1, 2, 4, …, 32, 64. Ask in which round the queue first passes \(B\).

Hint 2 · mechanism

In the round from \(w\) to \(2w\), each ack releases two packets and the bottleneck drains one, so after \(k\) acks the window is \(w + k\) and the queue is \(k\). The first drop is at \(k = B\). A paced sender queues nothing until its window passes the pipe, so its first drop is at pipe plus \(B\). Reno halves the window it holds when the loss is noticed, about one round trip after the drop.

Hint 3 · nearly there

Paper pipe: \(5 \times 10^6 \times 0.1/4608 = 108.5\). Bench pipe: \(10^7 \times 0.04 / (590 \times 8) = 84.7\). The round from 16 to 32 ends with a queue of 16, under both buffers, so both unpaced drops fall in the round from 32 to 64.

Solution 
          pipe    unpaced: round, window at drop    paced: window at drop
paper     108.5   32 to 64, 32 + 25 = 57            108 + 25 = 133
bench      84.7   32 to 64, 32 + 21 = 53             85 + 21 = 106

period of the paper's sawtooth:
  footnote 1      0.1^2 x 5e6 / 4608 = 10.85 s = 108.5 RTTs
  Mathis, p. 68   (2/3) x 108.5       = 72.3 RTTs
  Fig. 4          peaks near 150, 225, 300, 376, 451 = 75 RTTs
Fig. 8 paced peak: 2 x (1250 + 312) = 3,124; the figure shows about 3,300

Expected output. The paper's figures agree within the reading error: Fig. 4's unpaced line tops out near 62, a little past 57 because the loss takes a round trip to come back, and its paced line goes far past 133, to about 213. On the bench, unpaced ssthresh was 51: in slow start the window roughly doubles during the round trip the loss needs to come back, and halving gives back about the window at the drop, 53. Paced ssthresh was 86, below 106: once the link is full, acks arrive only at the link's rate, so the window can no longer double in a round trip.

What this shows. The paper's "first loss at twice the buffer" (PDF p. 5) is the end of a round; the drop itself comes \(B\) acks into the first round whose half-window passes \(B\). Paced or not, the drop is followed by a round trip of growth, which is the late-signal learning in a single flow. The footnote's 108 against the figure's 75 is the paper's slip in the half-window learning.

Common wrong turn. Using 576 bytes on the wire for the bench. On the veth every frame also carries a 14-byte Ethernet header, and netem's rate counts it, so a packet takes \(590 \times 8 / 10^7 = 0.472\) ms, and the pipe is 85 packets. With 576 you get 87, close enough here, but at full-size packets the same slip is 1,500 against 1,514 bytes.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: a 20 Mbit/s, 50 ms path carries full-size packets (1,514 bytes on the wire) through a 30-packet drop-tail buffer. One Reno flow starts at one packet. At what window does it drop its first packet unpaced, and paced?

Answer

About 62 unpaced and 113 paced. The pipe is \(20 \times 10^6 \times 0.05 / (1514 \times 8) = 82.6\) packets. The queue grows one packet per ack in each round (PDF p. 2); the round from 16 to 32 ends with 16 queued, so the drop comes 30 acks into the round from 32 to 64: \(32 + 30 = 62\). Paced, nothing queues before the pipe is full: \(83 + 30 = 113\) (PDF p. 5). Near miss: the paper's \(2B = 60\) is the end-of-round view.

2. Predict the value: the spine's fifty flows, with the buffer raised from 312 packets to one pipe, 1,250. What sum of windows should the paced flows reach before the loss comes back, and do the unpaced flows differ?

Answer

About \(2 \times (1{,}250 + 1{,}250) = 5{,}000\), and the unpaced flows about the same. Nothing drops before the windows add up to pipe plus buffer, and slow start doubles once more while the loss travels back (PDF p. 6). With one pipe of buffer the unpaced bursts no longer overflow early either, so both lose together (PDF p. 6). The bench at one pipe peaked at 344 paced and 335 unpaced, against \(2 \times 170 = 340\). Near miss: expecting a bigger buffer to desynchronize them.

3. Here is a symptom: ss -ti on a CUBIC upload over fq_codel shows pacing_rate 23.4Mbps, and a capture shows bursts of ten or more packets at line rate after every idle period. A colleague says pacing is broken. What is going on?

Answer

Nothing paces this socket, so nothing is broken. TCP computes sk_pacing_rate for every socket on every ack, and tcp_get_info() copies it into ss whatever the socket's sk_pacing_status (tcp.c:4229–4231). Over fq_codel with no SO_MAX_PACING_RATE the status is NONE, so TCP's timer stays off (tcp.h:1614) and TCP does not even advance the next send time (tcp_output.c:1451). Put fq on the egress or set the option (Diagram 6.4). Near miss: blaming fq_codel, which nobody asked to pace.

4. Here is a symptom: a nightly job opens 40 uploads in the same second, from hosts that just moved from fq_codel to fq, through a switch with a small drop-tail buffer. Before the move, the first retransmissions were spread over a second; now all 40 connections retransmit within one round trip. Name the mechanism and one fix at the switch.

Answer

Synchronized drops of paced slow starts (PDF pp. 5–6). fq spreads all 40 windows, so the buffer stays empty until they overload the link (a late congestion signal), and the overflow then catches every flow (mixing). Unpaced, bursts overflowed it early for a few flows only; on the bench, 8 of 8 paced flows lost in the first epoch against 6 of 8. Fix at the switch: an early, random signal such as RED with a low threshold (Fig. 16, PDF p. 8) or CoDel. Near miss: fq's flow_limit; fq dropped nothing.

5. Choose and defend: a drop-tail bottleneck you run sits in front of a fleet moving to fq one rack at a time. Raise its buffer from a quarter BDP to one BDP, or keep the buffer and add RED with a low minimum threshold?

Answer

RED. The trouble is a signal that comes only after overload, from a FIFO that drops every flow at once. A bigger buffer delays the signal and keeps flows in step: at one BDP both kinds lost together (PDF p. 6), paced flows still did whenever new flows started (Figs 12–13), and every packet waits up to a round trip longer. Fig. 16's best RED (min_th 5, max_th 0.9 of the buffer, max_p 0.02) brought paced flows near Reno (PDF p. 8). The cost: RED is sensitive; Fig. 16's high-threshold setting did worse than drop-tail.

6. Here is a symptom: setting net.ipv4.tcp_pacing_ss_ratio and tcp_pacing_ca_ratio to 100 makes the bench's eight fq-paced flows retransmit 16% more. A colleague expected the paper's exact rate to pace better. Explain.

Answer

At exactly cwnd per srtt the pacer becomes the clock and the flow lags: a slow-start round cannot leave faster than one srtt, the windows run ahead of the packets sent (up to 30), the first loss is noticed later (0.287 s against 0.244 s) and the overshoot is bigger (279 against 188). That is the paper's "lags behind by a round trip time" (PDF p. 5). Linux's 200% and 120% keep the pacer above the ack clock (tcp_input.c:1154–1159). Near miss: blaming fq, which only enforces the rate TCP computes.

Recap

Slow start parks half a window in the bottleneck queue, so unpaced Reno overflows a small buffer early and unevenly. Paced traffic builds no queue until the link is full, so the loss signal comes late, and because paced packets are mixed, every flow loses in the same congestion epoch. In ns Reno that meant timeouts and a collapse; on Linux each flow halves once and the link stays busy, but the late, shared signal remains. Linux paces at 200% or 120% of cwnd over srtt, only through fq or TCP's timer, and ss shows the rate either way.
