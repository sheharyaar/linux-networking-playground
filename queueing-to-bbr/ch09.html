Chapter 9 · before you read

Eiffel

~7 min before · pp. 18–19, 21–22, 24, 26, 28 of 17–31 (selected, ~1 h) · ~22 min after · HOST
The paper's own kernel queue (p. 26): 20,000 buckets of 100 µs over a 2 s horizon, holding the packets of 20,000 paced TCP flows. Every dequeue must find the first non-empty bucket among the 20,000.

The question this chapter answers

The small-queues chapter left fq holding every waiting flow in its throttled tree, a red-black tree ordered by the time each flow may send next. Each insert walks about \(\log_2 n\) levels of that tree. Eiffel's authors paced 20,000 TCP flows to 24 Gbit/s in total between two EC2 machines, and fq used a median of 14 times as many CPU cores as their own qdisc did (p. 26).

The paper's argument starts from one observation. A scheduler is a priority queue, and the usual priority queues compare keys, which costs \(O(\log n)\) per packet for \(n\) queued packets, or \(O(m \log n)\) for a policy with \(m\) ranking functions (p. 18). Packet ranks are special: they are integers, they sit in a window that moves with time, and many packets share one. So the queue can index an array instead of comparing keys. This chapter follows the paper's kernel queue: 20,000 buckets of 100 µs over a 2 s horizon. On every dequeue it must find the first non-empty bucket, and a timer must fire when that bucket is due.

Eiffel has two halves. One is a data structure: buckets, one bit per bucket, and a CPU instruction that finds the first set bit of a word, stacked in levels. The other is a way to write scheduling policies, built on a hardware design called PIFO. One idea from that half matters for the rest of this reading list: every rate limit can become a time written on the packet, so one queue ordered by time can enforce them all.

Words you will meet

Eiffel
The paper's software scheduler: the queues below plus an extended PIFO way of writing policies, built as a Linux qdisc and as a module of the BESS software switch.
rank
The number a scheduling policy gives a packet; the scheduler sends the smallest rank first. A send time, a deadline, a finish number or a strict priority can each be a rank.
integer priority queue
A priority queue whose keys are integers in a known range, so it can find an element by indexing an array instead of comparing keys.
bucketed queue
An integer priority queue over the ranks 0 to \(C\), split into \(N\) buckets of \(C/N\) ranks each. Packets in one bucket count as equal and leave first-in first-out.
find first set (FFS)
A CPU instruction that returns the index of the lowest set bit of a machine word in a few cycles. With one bit per bucket, it names the first non-empty bucket.
hierarchical FFS queue
A tree of bitmap words over the buckets. Each bit says whether the word below it has any bit set. Finding the first non-empty bucket costs one word and one FFS per level: \(\lceil \log_w N \rceil\) levels for \(N\) buckets and \(w\)-bit words.
cFFS (circular hierarchical FFS queue)
Two hierarchical FFS queues: a primary for the current range of ranks and a secondary for the range after it. When the primary empties, their pointers swap, so the range moves forward without rebuilding a bitmap.
gradient queue
Eiffel's arithmetic stand-in for FFS: each non-empty bucket adds a weighted parabola to a running sum, and the sum's lowest point falls on the highest non-empty bucket. Its approximate form answers in one step and can miss.
PIFO (push-in first-out)
A queue that inserts each element where its rank puts it and always removes from the head. Sivaraman et al. (2016) build programmable hardware schedulers from trees of PIFOs.
single shaper
Eiffel's way to enforce every rate limit in a scheduling hierarchy with one queue ordered by time: each limit becomes a send time on the packet.

Chapter 9 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. The authors name three properties of packet ranks. What are they, and what does a bucketed queue give up in return for its speed? pp. 18–19

Answer

Ranks are integers, at any moment they fall inside a known range, and many packets share one (pp. 18–19). So the queue can be \(N\) buckets over the ranks 0 to \(C\), found by index (p. 19). It gives up order inside a bucket, where packets leave first-in first-out, and it allocates every bucket in advance, tens to hundreds of KB (p. 19). Near miss: "only the approximate queue loses order". The exact queues also lose every difference smaller than a bucket.

2. With 64-bit words, how many words must a hierarchical FFS queue read to find the first non-empty bucket among 20,000, and why does that number stay the same with ten packets queued or a million? p. 21

Answer

Three. The 20,000 bucket bits fill 313 leaf words, one bit per leaf word fills 5 words, and those 5 bits fit in one root word: \(\lceil \log_{64} 20{,}000 \rceil = 3\) (p. 21). The count depends only on \(N\), fixed when the policy is configured, so it is "independent of the number of enqueued elements" (p. 21). Near miss: \(\log_2 20{,}000 \approx 14\), the depth of a balanced tree of 20,000 entries, which grows with the entries.

3. Eiffel's kernel qdisc used 14 times fewer cores than fq and 3 times fewer than Carousel. The authors give a different reason for each gap. What are the two reasons? pp. 25–26

Answer

Against fq, the data structure: red-black trees reordered on every enqueue and dequeue, plus garbage collection of inactive flows (p. 26). Against Carousel, the timer: Carousel fires timers at constant intervals, Eiffel arms one for the soonest deadline (pp. 25–26); Fig. 9 puts the gap in softirq time. Near miss: crediting FFS for the Carousel gap. Carousel's timing wheel is \(O(1)\) too (Table 1, p. 19).

The chapter on one screen

The paper opens with cost. Software schedulers sort packets in comparison priority queues, red-black trees in qdiscs and binary heaps in C++, at \(O(\log n)\) per operation, or \(O(m \log n)\) for \(m\) ranking functions (pp. 17–18). Ranks are integers in a moving, bounded window, and many packets share one, so a bucketed integer queue fits (pp. 18–19). Table 1 sets fq, hClock, Carousel, OpenQueue and PIFO against two goals: \(O(1)\) queues and a way to express any policy (pp. 19–20).

The queues. FFS finds the first non-empty bucket in a word; a tree of words makes the search \(O(\log_w N)\), a constant once \(N\) is set (p. 21). Ranks move, and a bitmap indexed modulo its size reports the wrong minimum, so cFFS keeps a primary and a secondary queue and swaps them (pp. 21–22). The gradient queue does FFS by arithmetic, and its approximate form answers in one step (pp. 22–23).

The policies. Eiffel adds per-flow ranking and ranking on dequeue to the PIFO model, and enforces every rate limit in a hierarchy with a single shaper queue ordered by time (pp. 23–25).

The evidence. As a kernel qdisc pacing 20,000 flows, Eiffel used 14 times fewer cores than fq and 3 times fewer than Carousel (p. 26). pFabric on cFFS carried 5 times more flows than on a heap (Fig. 11, p. 26). A queue of 100 µs buckets cannot make gaps under 100 µs (p. 28). Below about 1,000 priority levels the queue barely matters; above that, Fig. 16 picks one (pp. 28–29).

Ranks are integers in a moving window

Sorting by comparison costs \(O(\log n)\) per insert or extract for \(n\) queued packets (p. 18). Eiffel's claim is that packet ranks need no comparisons (pp. 18–19). They are integers. At any moment they fall in a known range: 8 levels for 802.1Q, 50,000 for fairness over 50,000 flows, up to a million for a queue indexed by time. And many packets share a rank, since at 10 to 100 Gbit/s packets leave nanoseconds apart. So the queue is a bucketed integer queue (p. 19): insert is an array index, and moving a packet to another bucket is \(O(1)\).

Put numbers on the spine. A 2 s horizon over 20,000 buckets is \(2\ \text{s}/20{,}000 = 100\ \mu\text{s}\) per bucket. At 24 Gbit/s in total, 100 µs carries \(24 \times 10^9 \times 10^{-4} / 8 = 300\) KB, about 200 full-size packets. Each of the 20,000 flows gets 1.2 Mbit/s, one 1,500-byte packet every 10 ms, so one flow's packets sit 100 buckets apart. The order lost is among different flows' packets due in the same 100 µs. Memory: \(20{,}000 \times 16\ \text{B} = 320\) KB of bucket heads and tails plus 2.5 KB of bitmap, as p. 19 predicts.

The shapers chapter met ranks without the name. Weighted fair queueing sends the packet with the smallest finish number \(F = \max(F_\text{prev}, R(t)) + P\), approximating the fluid ideal behind max-min fairness, and keeping finish numbers sorted cost \(O(\log n)\) per packet (DRR paper, p. 233). DRR reached \(O(1)\) by not sorting and paid in latency. Eiffel keeps the sort and makes the rank an array index. fq's throttled tree sorts the rank time_next_packet in a red-black tree (sch_fq.c:220–239).

One find-first-set per level

With one bit per bucket, FFS finds the first non-empty bucket among \(w\) in one instruction (p. 21, Fig. 2). More buckets means more words. Scanned in sequence they cost \(O(M)\) for \(M\) words, fine for Linux's real-time scheduler, whose 100 priorities fit in two 64-bit words. A tree of words, each bit summing up a word below, costs \(O(\log_w N)\) (p. 21, Fig. 3).

Walk Diagram 9.1. The root's FFS picks one of 5 level-2 words, that word's FFS one of 313 leaf words, the leaf's FFS the bucket: three words, \(\lceil \log_{64} 20{,}000 \rceil = 3\). An insert climbs only while the word it changed had been zero. A red-black tree of 20,000 throttled flows is about \(\log_2 20{,}000 \approx 14\) levels deep, up to twice that at worst, each level a pointer to follow. \(N\) is fixed, so the lookup is a constant, "independent of the number of enqueued elements" (p. 21). Since the paper. In v7.2 on x86, __ffs() is the tzcnt instruction (arch/x86/include/asm/bitops.h:243–259; spelled rep; bsf until v6.16, the same bytes) and __fls() is bsr (line 289). find_first_bit() is the paper's word-by-word \(O(M)\) scan (lib/find_bit.c:29–42), and the real-time scheduler the paper cites still picks its next task with two __ffs() calls (include/asm-generic/bitops/sched.h:13–30).

The paper's slip. Page 21 writes "Bit-Scan-Forward (BSR)". On x86, BSF is bit scan forward and returns the lowest set bit; BSR is bit scan reverse and returns the highest. A minimum queue with bucket \(i\) at bit \(i\) needs the forward scan, which __ffs() compiles to. "Leftmost" on that page is a drawing convention: Fig. 2 puts bucket 0 on the left, a printed hex word puts bit 0 on the right. On p. 23 the gradient queue seeks the highest bucket, and there BSR fits.

A window that moves

Ranks indexed by send time grow with the clock, so the queue must move. The usual trick, bucket = rank modulo the queue size, breaks the bitmap: in Fig. 2's six buckets a packet of rank 6 lands in slot 0, and FFS reports it ahead of ranks 4 and 5 (p. 21). An overflow queue re-sorted as the range advances costs a pass over every waiting packet. cFFS makes the overflow a second FFS queue for the next range (p. 22, Fig. 4). When the primary empties, the two pointers swap and h_index, the primary's lowest rank, moves up one queue width; no bit is rewritten. A rank beyond the secondary goes into its last bucket, unsorted.

The Carousel chapter's timing wheel is a bucketed queue indexed by time modulo its size. It needs no minimum, because it walks its slots in time order, one per tick, empty or not; p. 19 says it supports only non-work-conserving schedules. Eiffel keeps the buckets and adds the bitmap, so the walk jumps to the next non-empty slot. In the race, with 3 packets in 20,000 buckets, the slot-by-slot wheel visited 3,347 slots per packet (4.9 µs); cFFS read 9.5 words (17.8 ns).

Linux had solved the wrap twice before. QFQ (Linux 3.0, 2011; the paper's reference [22]) shifts each group's slot bitmap so bit 0 is always the earliest slot (net/sched/sch_qfq.c:939–955, 967–973). The timer wheel searches from its cursor to the end of a level, then from the start up to the cursor (kernel/time/timer.c:1837–1848).

The paper's slip. Page 19 says a timing wheel "supports only non-work conserving time-based schedules", then that it "does not support operations needed for non-work conserving schedules (i.e., ExtractMin or ExtractMax)". The second should read work-conserving: a work-conserving scheduler sends the smallest rank now, whatever its time, which is ExtractMin; a wheel releases only what is due.

Bucket width against order

A bucket's width is the finest order the queue keeps: "a queue with a granularity of 100 microseconds cannot insert gaps between packets that are smaller than 100 microseconds" (p. 28). Finer buckets mean more of them, more empty, more memory; the authors suggest at least one packet per bucket, and coarser buckets once more than 30% sit empty (p. 28). It is the Carousel chapter's slot granularity, seen from the data structure.

On the spine, a 1.2 Mbit/s flow sends every 10 ms, 100 buckets apart, so a bucket moves each packet by at most 1% of its gap. One 10 Gbit/s flow sends a 1,500-byte packet every 1.2 µs, so \(100 / 1.2 \approx 83\) of its packets share a bucket and leave back to back. Many slow flows tolerate wide buckets; one fast flow does not.

The gradient queue (pp. 22–23) finds a bucket by arithmetic. Each non-empty bucket \(i\) adds \(2^i(x-i)^2\) to a sum, one parabola whose lowest point lies just below the highest non-empty bucket, so \(\lceil b/a \rceil\) names it (Theorem 1, p. 31). The approximate form covers hundreds of buckets in one sum, misses when buckets are empty, and gained at most 9% over cFFS (p. 27). Fig. 16 (p. 28) sums up: under about 1,000 levels any queue; a fixed range, FFS; a moving range with uneven use, cFFS; a moving range with every level busy, the approximate queue.

Derivation · the critical point, on buckets 1, 3 and 4

One bucket's term expands to \(2^i x^2 - 2\,i2^i\,x + i^2 2^i\). Summed over the non-empty buckets, the curvature is \(a x^2 - 2b x + c\) with \(a = \sum_i 2^i\) and \(b = \sum_i i2^i\). Its derivative \(2ax - 2b\) is zero at \(x = b/a\).

Buckets 1, 3 and 4: \(a = 2 + 8 + 16 = 26\), \(b = 2 + 24 + 64 = 90\), \(b/a = 3.46\), and \(\lceil 3.46 \rceil = 4\), the highest bucket.

Theorem 1's two extremes (p. 31), for buckets 1 to \(N\): all full gives \(a = 2^{N+1} - 2\) and \(b = N2^{N+1} - (2^{N+1} - 2)\), so \(b/a = N/(1 - 2^{-N}) - 1\), just above \(N - 1\); bucket \(N\) alone gives exactly \(N\). Emptying any lower bucket raises \(b/a\), so every pattern lies in \((N-1, N]\) and the ceiling is \(N\). For \(N = 4\) all full: \(4/(15/16) - 1 = 3.27\).

The paper's slip. Page 22 writes the curvature as \(ax^2 - bx + c\) with its critical point at \(x = b/2a\), and then defines \(b = \sum_i i2^i\). With that \(b\) the linear term is \(2bx\), so the form is \(ax^2 - 2bx + c\) and the critical point is \(b/a\). The two slips cancel: the rule the paper uses, \(\lceil b/a \rceil\), and Appendix A are right. Taken literally, \(b/2a\) gives 1.73 for buckets 1, 3 and 4, and the wrong bucket, 2.

Every limit becomes a time

The PIFO model builds a scheduler as a tree of scheduling transactions, each one ranking function over one PIFO, with shaping transactions on nodes (pp. 23–24). Eiffel adds per-flow ranking, one rank for a flow's whole queue, and ranking on dequeue, where sending a packet can re-rank its flow (p. 24). pFabric, which sends the flow with the fewest packets left, needs both; its policy is two lines (Fig. 10, p. 26).

The idea this reading list keeps is the single shaper (p. 24). A queue per rate limit costs CPU and memory. Carousel showed that any rate limit can become a timestamp per packet, so Eiffel keeps one shaper queue, ordered by time, for every limit in the hierarchy, and leaves the shares to work-conserving queues.

With numbers: a 1,500-byte packet of the 7 Mbit/s leaf is stamped \(1{,}500 \times 8 / (7 \times 10^6) = 1.71\) ms after that leaf's previous packet; at that time it joins PQ2 and is stamped for 10 Mbit/s, 1.2 ms per packet; then PQ1 and the root's pacing rate. The shapers chapter asked what a timer and a queue per shaper cost with thousands of flows. HTB keeps a red-black tree of waiting classes per level (sch_htb.c:146); Eiffel keeps one queue ordered by time. The departure-timestamps chapter is the same idea in Linux: TCP writes the time into skb->tstamp (EDT) and fq waits for it.

Where the cores went

In the kernel test (p. 26) Eiffel ran as a qdisc, shaper only, with 20,000 buckets over 2 s, on Linux 4.10, against 20,000 neper flows capped with SO_MAX_PACING_RATE to 24 Gbit/s in total. It used a median of 14 times fewer cores than fq and 3 times fewer than Carousel (Fig. 8). For fq the authors blame the data structure: red-black trees reordered on every enqueue and dequeue, and garbage collection of idle flows. Against Carousel the data structures cost about the same; Carousel fires timers at constant intervals, Eiffel arms one for the soonest deadline (Fig. 9, and p. 25).

The small-queues chapter's fq already arms one watchdog for its earliest throttled flow (sch_fq.c:745), Eiffel's timer policy. What it pays is the tree. 24 Gbit/s of 1,500-byte packets is 2 million packets a second; with every flow capped, nearly every packet sends its flow back into the tree, about 14 levels deep for 20,000 flows, where a cFFS lookup reads three words.

Read the numbers with their setting: Linux 4.10, before fq honoured departure times, and an Eiffel qdisc that kept per-flow state in sock.h (p. 26). The pFabric baseline is weak too: the paper charges its heap \(O(n)\) per re-rank (p. 27), but a heap that indexes each flow's position re-ranks in \(O(\log n)\).

Since the paper (checked in v7.2, 2 October 2026). fq still keeps throttled flows in a red-black tree, q->delayed (sch_fq.c:138; insert 220–239, drain 664–690). Since v5.2 (2019) each flow also has one, t_root, for packets whose departure times arrive out of order, with an \(O(1)\) list for the rest (lines 66–71, 508–537). Since v6.16 (2025) a qdisc can be written in BPF, and the selftests' BPF fq keeps its throttled flows in a bpf_rbtree (bpf_qdisc_fq.c:124, 430). No Eiffel code is in the kernel source.

Real systems

Back to sch_fq

fq is the qdisc Eiffel's kernel test raced (p. 26), and its throttled tree is what an Eiffel queue would replace. In tc -s qdisc, the bracketed throttled counts flows in the tree, the later one counts inserts, each \(O(\log n)\), and next_packet_delay is when its one timer fires. More in the full card in the small-queues chapter.

Meet the system · BPF qdiscs: Qdisc_ops written in BPF

What it is. Since Linux 6.16 a qdisc can be BPF programs: you fill a struct Qdisc_ops (enqueue, dequeue, init, reset, destroy) in a struct_ops map, register it, and tc attaches it by its .id like a built-in qdisc.

Why it exists. A new scheduler used to mean a kernel module. The 2025 patch argues that BPF's objects, lists and red-black trees now suffice "to realize sophisticated packet scheduling algorithms" (commit c8240344956e): the programmability Eiffel's second half asks for.

The parts you need.

  • The Qdisc_ops struct_ops type and its .id, the name tc uses (net/sched/bpf_qdisc.c:446–475).
  • Kfuncs for what BPF may not touch directly: bpf_qdisc_skb_drop, bpf_qdisc_watchdog_schedule, bpf_qdisc_bstats_update (lines 192–287).
  • The queue itself, built from BPF objects: bpf_rbtree, bpf_list, maps, and pointers to held skbs.
  • bpftool struct_ops register to load, which needs root here (kernel.unprivileged_bpf_disabled = 2).

Where this chapter lives inside it. Ranks: the selftest bpf_fq orders flows by time_next_packet and packets by tstamp, each in a bpf_rbtree, so it pays fq's \(O(\log n)\) (bpf_qdisc_fq.c:144–164, 373, 430). Cores: bpf_qdisc_watchdog_schedule is the soonest-deadline timer. FFS levels: an Eiffel queue would be BPF arrays of buckets and words, and BPF has no find-first-set instruction; clang 22 turns __builtin_ctzll into x & -x, a multiply by a de Bruijn constant and a table lookup. The bits iterator kfunc (v6.11, kernel/bpf/helpers.c:3522) runs find_next_bit() over up to 511 words.

Hands-on · Walk a two-level FFS lookup by hand

~25 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil

Task. 4,096 buckets of 100 µs in a two-level FFS queue: a 64-bit root and 64 leaf words; bit \(i\) (0 is least significant) of leaf \(j\) is bucket \(64j + i\). Root 0x0000000000000A00, leaf 9 0x0000000000400000, leaf 11 0x8000000000000001, the rest zero. (a) Find the next bucket and its time; count words read. (b) Dequeue its only packet, update the bitmaps, find the next bucket; count reads and writes. (c) Compare a one-level scan from word 0 and a binary heap of 20,000 packets. (d) A packet due 410.6 ms ahead goes in bucket rank mod 4,096. What does the next lookup return?

Done when. You have two bucket numbers with their times, the three counts side by side, and one sentence on what went wrong in (d).

Hint 1 · direction

Diagram 9.1 with one level fewer: FFS on the root picks the leaf word, FFS on the leaf picks the bucket. Emptying a bucket clears bits upward while words become zero.

Hint 2 · mechanism

FFS of a hex word: take the lowest non-zero hex digit, then its lowest set bit. In 0xA00, digit 2 (counting from 0 on the right) is A, binary 1010, whose lowest set bit is bit 1, so the word's FFS is \(2 \times 4 + 1 = 9\).

Hint 3 · nearly there

(a) is bucket \(64 \times 9 + 22\). For (d): \(4{,}106 \bmod 4{,}096 = 10\). Which root bit does bucket 10 set, and is it lower than bit 9?

Solution 
(a) FFS(root 0x...0A00) = 9; FFS(leaf 9 0x...400000) = 22
    bucket 9*64 + 22 = 598, due in 59.8 ms             2 words read
(b) clear bit 22 of leaf 9 -> 0; clear bit 9 of root -> 0x800
    FFS(root) = 11; FFS(leaf 11 0x8000000000000001) = 0
    bucket 11*64 + 0 = 704, due in 70.4 ms             2 written, 2 read
(c) one-level scan from word 0: words 0..9 = 10 reads  (worst case 64; 313 at 20,000 buckets)
    binary heap of 20,000: 14 levels, about 2 comparisons each = ~28 per pop
(d) bucket 10 sets bit 10 of leaf 0 and bit 0 of the root
    FFS(root) = 0; FFS(leaf 0) = 10: bucket 10, "due in 1.0 ms"
    the packet due in 410.6 ms leaves 409.6 ms early

Expected output. The numbers above. The race's heap counted 25.5 comparisons per pop and push with 10,000 packets and 28.5 with 30,000 (data/race-vs-packets.csv), around the estimate.

What this shows. The lookup read one word per level and would read the same two with 20,000 packets queued (the FFS learning, p. 21). The scan's cost is the distance from word 0 to the first packet, and the heap's is \(\log_2 n\). Part (d) is p. 21's wrap: FFS always starts at bit 0. cFFS would have put the packet in its secondary's bucket 10 (the moving-window learning).

Common wrong turn. Reading the hex left to right, the way Fig. 2 draws bucket 0 on the left. The leftmost set bit of 0x0000000000000A00 as printed is bit 11, which skips bucket 598 and returns 704 first.

Hands-on · Build an FFS queue and race it against a heap

~45 min · build, then measure · no sudo, needs Python 3 and cc

Task. Copy ffsq_starter.py to ffsq.py and write push() and pop_min() for a hierarchical FFS queue. First predict, for 20,000 buckets and 10, 1,000 and 20,000 packets, the words your queue touches per pop and the comparisons heapq makes per pop. Then run the C race and read where, at 20,000 buckets, the heap and the slot-by-slot wheel each cross cFFS.

cd labs/ch09-eiffel                          # from the dossier folder
cp ffsq_starter.py ffsq.py                   # then write push() and pop_min()
python3 ffsq.py --buckets 20000 --packets 1000
./race.sh                                    # about two minutes; writes two CSVs here

Done when. Your queue drains in rank order (the script asserts it), you have words per pop beside comparisons per pop for three sizes, and you can name the two crossing points.

Hint 1 · direction

Diagram 9.1: an insert climbs from the leaf and stops at the first word that was already non-zero; a lookup descends from the root with one FFS per level.

Hint 2 · mechanism

push: for each level from the leaf, i = b >> 6; set bit b & 63 of word i; stop if the old word was non-zero, else b = i. pop_min: start at b = 0 and walk reversed(self.levels).

Hint 3 · nearly there

b = (b << 6) | ((w & -w).bit_length() - 1) descends one level. After popleft() empties a bucket, clear its bit, and keep clearing upward while the word you cleared is zero.

Solution 
    def push(self, rank, item):
        if self.buckets[rank] is None:
            self.buckets[rank] = deque()
        self.buckets[rank].append(item)
        b = rank
        for words in self.levels:            # leaf first; stop at a word already non-zero
            i = b >> 6
            old = words[i]
            words[i] = old | (1 << (b & 63))
            self.touched += 1
            if old:
                break
            b = i

    def pop_min(self):
        b = 0
        for words in reversed(self.levels):  # root first: one find-first-set per level
            w = words[b]
            self.touched += 1
            if not w:
                raise IndexError('empty queue')
            b = (b << 6) | ((w & -w).bit_length() - 1)
        q = self.buckets[b]
        item = q.popleft()
        if not q:                            # bucket empty: clear bits upward while words empty
            c = b
            for words in self.levels:
                i = c >> 6
                words[i] &= ~(1 << (c & 63))
                self.touched += 1
                if words[i]:
                    break
                c = i
        return b, item

What this shows. Words per pop stayed between 3.65 and 5.5 from 10 to 20,000 packets: three reads, plus clears that climb further when buckets are sparse (the FFS learning, p. 21). heapq's comparisons grew from 2 to 13, the \(\log_2 n\) of p. 18. In C the heap is faster below about 20 packets and slower above; the slot-by-slot wheel catches up only near 10,000 packets, when it visits about 2 slots per packet.

Common wrong turn. Timing the Python version. heapq is written in C, so it wins on time while making more comparisons (275 against 973 ns at 1,000 packets). In Python you time the interpreter; count words, or time in C, which is why race.c exists.

Expected shape, from the source: a qdisc bpf_fq 1: root line with no parameters, and Sent and backlog counters, which bpf_fq keeps itself (bpf_qdisc_fq.c:366, 608–609). The flow holds about 5 Mbit/s. bpf_fq ignores the socket's pacing rate, so the spacing comes from TCP's own timer, switched on by SO_MAX_PACING_RATE, which also stamps each skb's time (tcp_output.c:1555); bpf_fq finds those times already due.

What this shows. A qdisc written in BPF in 2025 still sorts by comparison, both trees \(O(\log n)\) (the ranks and cores learnings, pp. 18, 26). An Eiffel queue in BPF would need arrays and seven instructions per FFS step, or the bits iterator.

Common wrong turn. Concluding from a steady 5 Mbit/s that bpf_fq paced the flow. It honours skb->tstamp only; the cap switched on TCP's internal pacing, as in the small-queues chapter's fq lab. To see bpf_fq hold packets, the times must come from somewhere else, such as a sender that stamps its own.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: 10 µs buckets over a 2 s horizon, 64-bit words. How many buckets and FFS levels, and how much memory for bitmaps and for bucket heads and tails at 16 bytes each?

Answer

200,000 buckets, 3 levels, about 25 KB of bitmap and 3.2 MB of buckets. \(2\ \text{s}/10\ \mu\text{s} = 200{,}000\); the leaf level is \(\lceil 200{,}000/64 \rceil = 3{,}125\) words, then 49, then one root, and \(64^3 = 262{,}144\) covers it (p. 21). Bitmap \(3{,}175 \times 8\ \text{B}\); buckets \(200{,}000 \times 16\ \text{B}\). Near miss: four levels. Ten times the spine's buckets adds no level; memory grows, the cost the paper names (p. 29).

2. Predict the value: page 29 says a cFFS with a billion buckets needs six bit operations to find the minimum. Which word width makes that true, and how many would 64-bit words need?

Answer

32-bit words; 64-bit words need five. The levels are \(\lceil \log_w N \rceil\) (p. 21): \(32^5 = 3.4 \times 10^7\) falls short of \(10^9\) and \(32^6 = 1.07 \times 10^9\) covers it, so six; \(64^5 = 1.07 \times 10^9\) already covers it, so five. Near miss: thirty, from \(\log_2 10^9\). That is a binary tree's depth, one bit per level, which a word-wide FFS avoids.

3. Here is a symptom: a home-made pacer indexes 4,096 buckets of 100 µs by send time modulo 4,096 and finds the next packet with one FFS from bit 0. Tests pass. In production a few packets leave about 410 ms early, all from senders that stamp times far ahead. What happened?

Answer

The window wrapped. A time more than 409.6 ms ahead lands in a low bucket, and FFS, which searches from bit 0, returns it before nearer packets: p. 21's rank 6 in slot 0. Fixes: cFFS's secondary queue (p. 22), a search from the cursor (the timer wheel), a shifted bitmap (QFQ), or dropping times beyond the horizon, as fq counts in horizon_drops. Near miss: clock skew between senders. The early packets are exactly those stamped past the window.

4. Here is a symptom: a 10 Gbit/s flow moves from fq to an Eiffel-style shaper of 20,000 buckets over 2 s. A capture now shows about 80 back-to-back packets every 100 µs where there was one every 1.2 µs. What happened?

Answer

Granularity. Packets due within the same 100 µs share a bucket, and a bucket empties first-in first-out at line rate, so the queue cannot make gaps under 100 µs (p. 28). At 10 Gbit/s, 100 µs holds \(10^{10} \times 10^{-4} / 12{,}000 \approx 83\) packets. Narrower buckets fix it, at the price of more buckets, more of them empty (p. 28). Near miss: TSO. A 64 KB skb is 45 packets; 83 every 100 µs is several separately timed skbs leaving one bucket.

5. Choose and defend: a host caps each of 5,000 VMs and the whole host at 20 Gbit/s, and busy VMs share spare capacity by weight. HTB with a class per VM, or one shaper queue ordered by time plus weighted queues, as in Fig. 7?

Answer

The single shaper, for CPU: every cap becomes a send time in one queue under one timer (p. 24), where HTB keeps a token bucket per class and a red-black tree of waiting classes per level (sch_htb.c:146). The trade is sharing: HTB's borrowing hands spare capacity to busy classes up to their ceil for free, while a single shaper needs separate work-conserving queues for the shares (PQ1 and PQ2 in Fig. 7). HTB ships today; Linux's nearest single shaper is per-packet departure times honoured by fq.

6. Choose and defend: a host paces at most about 50 flows, with send times up to 2 s ahead in 100 µs steps. A colleague wants to swap its binary heap for a cFFS because FFS is constant time. Do it?

Answer

Probably not. Fifty entries make a heap about \(\log_2 50 \approx 6\) levels deep in a few cache lines; at 20,000 buckets the race measured the heap at 14–20 ns for 10–30 packets against cFFS's 15–17 ns (Diagram 9.2). "Constant" means independent of \(n\): three words plus clears, while the heap's \(\log n\) is small when \(n\) is. The paper draws the same line for levels: below about 1,000 the choice hardly matters (p. 28). Switch when thousands queue and the heap keeps climbing.

Recap

Packet ranks are integers in a moving window, many packets share one, so a scheduler can bucket them and index instead of compare. A tree of 64-bit words finds the first non-empty bucket in \(\lceil \log_{64} N \rceil\) words, three for 20,000, whatever the queue holds. cFFS moves the window by swapping two trees; the bucket width is the order given up. Every rate limit can become a send time in one shaper queue. Eiffel beat fq on its trees and Carousel on timer policy; v7.2's fq still uses the tree, and a BPF qdisc is where an FFS queue could go.
