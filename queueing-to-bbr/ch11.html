Chapter 11 · before you read

Where BBR loses

~6 min before · Cao et al. pp. 130–136 and Ware et al. pp. 137–143 (~1.5 h) · ~22 min after · CC
The bench's 10 Mbit/s, 40 ms path, a pipe of 33 packets, which is also Ware et al.'s testbed (p. 138). One BBR flow and one CUBIC flow share its bottleneck, first behind a 17-packet buffer (half a BDP), then behind a 330-packet buffer (ten BDPs).

The question this chapter answers

The BBR chapter built a sender that keeps a model of the path. On the spine it learns a BtlBw of 10 Mbit/s and an RTprop of 40 ms, paces at a pacing gain times BtlBw, and caps what it has in flight at a cwnd gain of 2 times BtlBw times RTprop. Loss is not an input to that model. Other flows show up only through the rate and the round-trip time BBR measures.

Now give the path a neighbour that plays by the old rules: CUBIC, Linux's default, which grows its window until the buffer overflows and cuts it by 30% on each loss. Cao et al. ran 640 bandwidth, RTT and buffer combinations and found BBR up to 115% ahead of CUBIC in a 100 KB buffer, CUBIC up to 34% ahead in a 10 MB one, BBR losing ten times as many packets, and a loss rate near 20% where BBR's goodput falls off a cliff (pp. 131–134). Ware et al. saw one BBR flow take a fixed 35–40% of the link against as many as 16 CUBIC flows (p. 137).

So the question is: what decides who wins, the buffer, the loss rate or the neighbour? And each time, which part of BBRv1 is responsible?

Words you will meet

shallow and deep buffer
A bottleneck buffer smaller, or larger, than one BDP. Cao et al. test 100 KB as shallow and 10 MB as deep, because 10 MB is larger than most BDPs in their grid (p. 133).
goodput gain (GpGain)
Cao et al.'s score: BBR's goodput minus CUBIC's, as a percentage of CUBIC's (Eq. 1, p. 133). Positive means BBR got more. It is not symmetric: \(-34\%\) means CUBIC got \(1/0.66 \approx 1.5\) times BBR's goodput.
loss cliff (cliff point)
The random loss rate above which BBR's goodput collapses instead of shrinking slowly. Cao et al. predict it at \(p = 1 - 1/g\), where \(g\) is the largest pacing gain (Eq. 3, p. 134).
policer mode
BBRv1's long-term bandwidth mode. After two sampling intervals with steady delivery and heavy loss, it paces at their average rate with a pacing gain of 1 for 48 round trips (net/ipv4/tcp_bbr.c:184–194). Cao et al. call the trigger "a signal of policing" (p. 134).
inflight cap (in-flight cap)
The most data BBRv1 lets be in flight: cwnd gain times BtlBw times RTprop, so 2 BDPs as BBR estimates them (Ware et al. Eq. 1, p. 140). A sender at its cap is window-limited: it sends only when acks free room.
probe time
Ware et al.'s name for the time BBR spends in ProbeRTT, waiting for the queue to drain and then holding 4 packets in flight (Eq. 11, p. 142).
BBRv3 (after reading)
The current version of BBR, specified in the IETF draft draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-06 (July 2026) and shipped by Google outside the mainline kernel. It reacts to loss rates above 2% and keeps two limits on inflight, one long-term and one short-term. Linux 7.2 still runs BBRv1.

Chapter 11 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. Why does BBR get more goodput than CUBIC when the buffer is far smaller than a BDP, and what does it pay for that goodput? pp. 133–135

Answer

Because every probe overflows a small buffer and only CUBIC slows down. CUBIC cuts its window by 30% per loss and, at a large BDP, regrows for seconds; BBRv1's rate comes from a delivery-rate filter that loss does not touch. At 500 Mbit/s, 200 ms and 100 KB: 386.0 against 179.6 Mbit/s (p. 133). The price is loss: 10.1% against 0.9%, and 305,029 retransmissions against 1,398 sharing 100 KB (Table 1, p. 135).

2. Against CUBIC in a deep buffer, what sets BBR's share of the link, and why does adding more CUBIC flows not lower it? pp. 139–142

Answer

Its inflight cap. CUBIC keeps the queue full, so BBR's RTprop estimate includes CUBIC's queue (Eq. 3, p. 140), and BBR's probes push CUBIC back until BBR is window-limited at 2 × BtlBw × that RTT. Equating the cap with BBR's share of the queue gives \(p = 1/2\) (Eq. 5, p. 140). The number of CUBIC flows never enters it (p. 140). Near miss: "so BBR is fair". Against 16 CUBIC flows it still takes 35–40% (p. 137).

3. Under random loss, why does BBRv1's goodput stay nearly flat up to about 20% and then collapse, and what decides where the collapse starts? p. 134

Answer

Only the probe can renew BBR's bandwidth estimate, and a probe at pacing gain \(g\) delivers \(g(1-p)\) of it. At 1 or more, every cycle refreshes the max filter; below 1, the filter ages out and the rate shrinks cycle by cycle. Eq. 3 puts the cliff at \(1 - 1/1.25 = 20\%\) (p. 134). Near miss: the gain alone. BBRv1's policer detector fires at nearly the same loss (tcp_bbr.c:188), and the paper names it only for BBR_1.5.

The chapter on one screen

Cao et al. measure. They shape a dumbbell on a separate router, tbf for rate and buffer and netem for delay and loss, because shaping on the sender upsets TSQ (p. 132), and run Linux 4.15's BBRv1 against CUBIC in 640 configurations (pp. 132–133). Fig. 5 (p. 133) carries the result: in a 100 KB buffer BBR wins where the BDP is large, by up to 115%; in a 10 MB buffer CUBIC wins by up to 34%. BBR pays in losses, 10.1% against 0.9%, which the authors blame on the cwnd gain of 2.

Under random loss (Fig. 7, p. 134), CUBIC and Reno collapse within the first percent while BBR holds to about 20% and then falls. Eq. 3 puts that loss cliff at \(1 - 1/g\); BBR rebuilt with \(g = 1.1\) falls earlier, and with \(g = 1.5\) at the same place as BBR, which the authors put down to the policer logic. Sharing 1 Gbit/s and 20 ms (Fig. 8, Table 1, pp. 134–135), BBR takes 94% at 10 KB, the two split evenly near 5 MB, and CUBIC dominates at 10 MB.

Ware et al. model the deep-buffer case. In competition BBR becomes window-limited at its inflight cap (p. 137). CUBIC's queue inflates BBR's RTT estimate, BBR's probes push CUBIC back, and the cap stops BBR at half the queue (Eq. 1–5, p. 140). The extended model adds a finite queue, \(N\) BBR flows and the probe time (Eq. 6–11, pp. 140–142). The number of loss-based flows never enters the cap, which is why one BBR flow keeps 35–40% against sixteen.

The buffer picks the winner

Whether BBRv1 or CUBIC gets more goodput depends mostly on the bottleneck buffer counted in BDPs: far below one BDP, BBR wins; far above it, CUBIC wins. The authors' conclusion names "the relative difference between the bottleneck buffer size and BDP" (p. 135).

Read Fig. 5 (p. 133) that way. At 500 Mbit/s and 200 ms the BDP is 12.5 MB, so 100 KB is 0.8% of it: GpGain +115%. At 10 Mbit/s and 200 ms, 10 MB is 40 BDPs: \(-34\%\). The decision tree's two RTT splits (Fig. 4, p. 132) each end in leaves of one class.

The mechanism is the buffer-sizing rule from the queueing-maths chapter, applied to CUBIC. After a loss CUBIC's window is \(0.7\,(\text{BDP} + B)\) (RFC 9438 §4.6), which covers the pipe only if \(B \ge 0.43\) BDP. Below that the link idles until CUBIC regrows, and the time to its old peak, \(K = \sqrt[3]{0.3\,W_{\max}/0.4}\) (§4.2, §5.1), is 18 s for an 8,300-packet window. Above one BDP, the congestion-avoidance chapter's AIMD fills whatever buffer it gets, and BBR, capped at 2 BDPs in flight, cannot claim it back (p. 133).

The bench shows the other half: with a small BDP, a shallow buffer costs CUBIC little. One CUBIC flow on the spine path, 60 s per row (2 October 2026):

The price of a shallow buffer

In a shallow buffer BBRv1 keeps its goodput by not backing off, and pays in retransmissions: often ten times CUBIC's alone, over two hundred times when the two share a buffer (pp. 133, 135).

At 100 KB the average loss is 10.1% for BBR and 0.9% for CUBIC; at 10 MB it is 0.8% and 1.3% (p. 133). Sharing 1 Gbit/s and 20 ms through 100 KB, BBR retransmits 305,029 packets and CUBIC 1,398 (Table 1, p. 135). Ware et al. add that BBR's probing "causes buffer overflows and bursty loss for competing flows" (p. 137).

Cao et al. blame the cwnd gain: 2 BDPs in flight need a buffer of one BDP (p. 133). That is half the story, because 2 BDPs is a cap. With a correct windowed filter, BBR paces at 1.0 × BtlBw and keeps about one BDP in inflight. Two things push it past a small buffer:

  • Each probe sends 1.25 × BtlBw for one RTprop, 0.25 BDP extra: 3.1 MB at 500 Mbit/s and 200 ms, 31 times a 100 KB buffer, every eight rounds.
  • The max filter keeps its largest sample for 10 rounds (tcp_bbr.c:134, 801), so one high delivery rate sample paces BBR above the link until it ages out; the max "usually" gives "a slight overestimate" (Ware p. 140). Then BBR does fill its cap.

BBRv1's own loss handling is small: lost packets come off cwnd (tcp_bbr.c:493–494), recovery starts with one round of packet conservation (:481–515), and a 1.25 phase ends early on loss (:579–582). None of the three lowers BtlBw.

One claim lacks data: the abstract's "often exhibits large queue sizes" (p. 130). The paper reports retransmissions, never a queue length or an RTT. On a live host this case shows in ss -ti as a climbing retrans while bbr:(bw:…) stays at the link rate.

The cap sets the share

Against loss-based flows in a deep buffer, BBRv1 becomes window-limited at its inflight cap, and that cap sets its share of the link; the number of competitors does not enter (pp. 137, 140).

Ware et al. argue in two steps (pp. 139–140). BBR's probes push the total load over the link; everyone loses, CUBIC backs off, and BBR measures more delivery, until it reaches its cap. And CUBIC's packets stay queued while BBR drains in ProbeRTT, so BBR's RTprop estimate is CUBIC's queueing delay. With CUBIC holding a fraction \(p\) of a \(q\)-packet queue on a link of \(c\) packets per second:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{BtlBw}_{est} &= (1-p)\,c, \qquad \text{RTT}_{est} = pq/c,\\ \text{cap} &= 2\,\text{BtlBw}_{est}\,\text{RTT}_{est} = 2(p - p^2)\,q. \end{aligned} \]

BBR's own share of the queue is \((1-p)\,q\). Equal to the cap, it gives \(p = 1/2\) (Eq. 5, p. 140), however many CUBIC flows hold the other half. With a 4-BDP cap the same algebra gives 75%, where the authors' modified kernel converged (Fig. 5, p. 140). A capped sender sends one packet per ack: the ack clock of the congestion-avoidance chapter, inside a rate-based sender, "window-limited and ACK-clocked" (p. 143).

Derivation · the extended model, Eq. 7–11

With \(N\) BBR flows, each holds 4 packets in the queue during ProbeRTT, and the base RTT \(l\) is no longer negligible: \(\text{RTT}_{est} = (pq + 4N)/c + l\) (Eq. 7). The aggregate cap is \(2(1-p)c\,\text{RTT}_{est}\) (Eq. 8), and BBR's inflight is its share of queue plus pipe, \((1-p)(q + cl)\). Divide both sides by \((1-p)\): \(2(pq + 4N + cl) = q + cl\), so \(p = \tfrac12 - \tfrac{1}{2X} - \tfrac{4N}{q}\) with \(X = q/(cl)\) (Eq. 9). A negative \(p\) means BBR takes the whole link (p. 142). ProbeRTT waits for the full queue to drain, then holds for 200 ms and a round trip, every 10 s: \(\text{Probe}_{time} = (q/c + 0.2 + l)\,d/10\) (Eq. 11), and BBR's fraction is \((1-p)(1 - \text{Probe}_{time}/d)\) (Eq. 10).

On the spine, \(c = 10^7/8/1514 = 825.6\) packets/s, \(l = 0.04\) s, \(cl = 33.0\). At \(X = 10\), \(q = 330\): \(p = 0.5 - 0.05 - 4/330 = 0.438\); the probe time is \((0.40 + 0.2 + 0.04)/10 = 6.4\%\); BBR gets \(0.562 \times 0.936 = 0.526\). At \(X = 0.5\), \(p\) is negative, so BBR gets \(1 \times (1 - 0.026) = 0.974\). The knee is where \(p = 0\): \(X = 1 + 8N/(cl) = 1.24\).

On the spine at 10 BDPs, the model gives BBR 52.6% against one CUBIC flow, and Jain's index from the queueing-maths chapter is 0.997. Add fifteen CUBIC flows. Eq. 9 does not change, so BBR keeps 52.6%, each CUBIC flow gets 2.96%, and Jain's index over 17 flows is \(1/(17 \times (0.526^2 + 16 \times 0.0296^2)) = 0.20\). Ware et al. report 35–40% against 16 CUBIC flows (p. 137).

The paper names the model's limits: for one BBR flow at 2–8 BDPs it overshoots, 0.79 against a measured 0.54 at 2 BDPs (Fig. 8a, p. 142), and deep queues take minutes to converge (Fig. 2a, p. 138).

The paper's slips. Cao et al. write that at 10 MB "Cubic utilizes 3× more bandwidth than BBR" (p. 134); Fig. 8a's end points on the same page are about 810 and 140 Mbit/s, nearer 6×. Table 1's header (p. 135) reads 1e4, 1e5, 1e6, 5e6, 1e6, 5e7, 1e8: the second 1e6, between 5e6 and 5e7, must be 1e7.

Flat, then a cliff

Under random loss BBRv1's goodput stays near the link rate up to a loss cliff near \(p = 1 - 1/g\) and then collapses, while loss-based senders lose most of their rate within the first percent (Eq. 3 and Fig. 7, p. 134).

BtlBw is the largest delivery rate of the last 10 rounds, and only the 1.25 phase can beat the current estimate. With a fraction \(p\) of packets dropped at random, a probe at \(g \times \text{BtlBw}\) delivers \(g(1-p) \times \text{BtlBw}\). At 1 or more, each eight-round cycle renews the maximum before it ages out. Below 1, the old maximum expires and the estimate shrinks by about \(g(1-p)\) per cycle: at 25% loss that is 0.94, so ten cycles, about 3 s on the spine, leave 52%. Setting \(g(1-p) = 1\) gives Eq. 3: 20% for 1.25, and 9.1% and 33.3% for the authors' rebuilt BBR_1.1 and BBR_1.5.

Fig. 7a samples loss at roughly 0, 1, 2, 5.5, 11.5, 18, 26, 34 and 44%. BBR holds about 93 of 100 Mbit/s at 18% and drops to about 11 at 26%. BBR_1.1 has 84 near 11.5% and 13 near 18%; the text's "around 9%" falls between samples. BBR_1.5 tracks BBR. Retransmissions peak at the cliff (Fig. 7b): before it goodput holds while loss rises; after it BBR sends less, so it loses less.

Where it matters: radio links and lossy long paths, where loss is not congestion. BBRv1 delivers there, until the cliff.

The policer detector sits at the cliff

BBRv1 has a second loss rule, the policer detector, and for the default gain it fires at almost the same loss rate as Eq. 3's cliff, so Fig. 7's default-gain result cannot tell them apart (p. 134; tcp_bbr.c:184–194, 689–759).

A policer, from the shapers chapter, is a token bucket with no queue: traffic above its rate is dropped while delivery stays steady. The code looks for that pattern, citing Flach et al. (tcp_bbr.c:682–683):

  • After a first loss, intervals of 4 to 16 round trips, each ending at a loss (:186, 710–730).
  • An interval is lossy if lost << 8 >= 50 * delivered: lost over delivered of at least \(50/256 = 19.5\%\) (:188, 740–744; the comment says 20%).
  • Two lossy intervals in a row with delivery rates within 1/8, or 4 kbit/s, of each other mean "policed" (:190–192, 664–673).
  • Then BtlBw becomes their average (:228, 671) and the pacing gain is 1 (:673, 1002–1004) for 48 round trips (:194, 697–701). That is policer mode.

Put both thresholds in the same units. The detector divides by packets delivered, and tp->lost counts every loss, retransmissions lost again included (tcp_input.c:1326, 1337–1346). Under uniform random loss \(p\), each delivered packet costs \(p/(1-p)\) losses, so 19.5% of delivered is \(50/306 = 16.3\%\) of packets sent. Eq. 3 moved too: since v4.20 BBR paces 1% below its estimate (tcp_bbr.c:148, 252), so the 1.25 cliff is \(1 - 1/(1.25 \times 0.99) = 19.2\%\) on the reader's kernel, three points from the detector. A sender probing at 1.25 times a policer's rate loses 20% of what it sends, the same algebra as Eq. 3; the code does not say why it chose 50/256.

The paper's gap. Cao et al. invoke the policer logic to explain why BBR_1.5 collapses "much before the predicted 33%" (p. 134), yet count default BBR's collapse near 20% as confirming Eq. 3. For \(g = 1.25\) the two rules predict the same region, and Fig. 7a's samples at about 18% and 26% bracket both. Only BBR_1.1 separates them: Eq. 3 says 9.1%, below the detector, and Fig. 7a shows it already at 84 Mbit/s near 11.5%. Eq. 3 stands, on BBR_1.1 alone; the default-gain cliff is two rules at once. The verdict: a missing explanation. The arithmetic is right, and the authors name the detector in the next sentence.

On a live host they look different: policer mode shows pacing_gain:1 with cwnd_gain:2 for 48 round trips, about 2 s on the spine; the gain cycle shows 1.25 and 0.75 every eight rounds. The loss lab logs both.

What BBRv3 changes

Every behaviour in this chapter is BBRv1's, which Linux 7.2 still ships. BBRv3, the version the IETF draft specifies, slows down for loss above 2% per round, keeps a long-term and a short-term bound on inflight, and has no separate policer mode (draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-06 §2.8, §5.3.3, §5.5.10, Appendix A.17).

Since the papers. Checked 2 October 2026. v7.2's net/ipv4/tcp_bbr.c still describes BBRv1 in its header (lines 7–16) and has no inflight_hi, inflight_lo or ECN code; policer mode has changed once since Cao's 4.15 (commit 3aff3b4b986e, v4.16). BBRv3 lives in net/ipv4/tcp_bbr3.c on google/bbr branch bbr-v3-2026-09-16-01, whose header names the bounds inflight_hi and inflight_lo (lines 11–14); draft-06 (6 July 2026) calls them inflight_longterm and inflight_shortterm, and gives the DOWN gain as 0.90 (§5.3.3.1) where the code has 91/100.

For this chapter's three cases: in a shallow buffer v3 stops each probe at 2% loss; random loss meets a gradual response, since v3 aims to tolerate about 1% per round (§5.3.3.8.3); in a deep buffer with CUBIC the 2-BDP cap stays until overflow sets inflight_longterm. None of this is measured here.

The other fix sits at the bottleneck, as in the queue-delay chapter. CoDel holds the standing queue near 5 ms, so CUBIC cannot win by filling a deep buffer, and flow queueing serves each flow's own queue in turn, so shares stop depending on who holds the buffer. Neither paper tests an AQM; both used drop-tail queues.

Real systems

Back to Linux tcp_bbr, read through ss -ti

ss -ti prints bbr:(bw:…,mrtt:…,pacing_gain:…,cwnd_gain:…) from struct tcp_bbr_info (include/uapi/linux/inet_diag.h:234–241); bw is bbr_bw(), the policer-mode rate while that mode is on (tcp_bbr.c:228). This chapter adds two readings: policer mode is pacing_gain:1 with cwnd_gain:2 for 48 round trips, and the shallow-buffer storm is retrans climbing while bw stays at the link rate. On 2 October 2026 this host listed only reno cubic: the module is installed, and nobody had loaded it. After sudo modprobe tcp_bbr any user may select it per socket (TCP_CONG_NON_RESTRICTED, tcp_bbr.c:1145). Full card: the BBR chapter.

Back to netem, the kernel's network emulator

netem decides a random loss in netem_enqueue() (sch_netem.c:481), before the packet joins the queue its rate drains, so a sender can send above the rate and still fill it. Cao et al.'s links behaved the same way: BBR delivered about 93 Mbit/s at 18% loss (Fig. 7a, p. 134), more than \(100 \times 0.82 = 82\). Full card: the queueing-maths chapter.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: four BBRv1 flows and one CUBIC flow share the spine path behind 330 packets of buffer. From Ware et al.'s Eq. 9–11, what share do the BBR flows get together, what does each flow get, and what is Jain's index over the five?

Answer

About 56% for the BBR flows together, 14% each, 44% for CUBIC, and Jain's index 0.74. Eq. 9 with \(N = 4\): \(p = 0.5 - 0.05 - 16/330 = 0.402\); after probe time BBR gets \(0.598 \times 0.936 = 0.560\) (pp. 141–142). Jain: \(1/(5 \times (4 \times 0.140^2 + 0.440^2)) = 0.74\). Near miss: expecting more BBR flows to dilute BBR's share. The \(4N\) packets they leave queued during ProbeRTT raise every RTT estimate, and with it the aggregate cap.

2. Here is a symptom: on a 100 Mbit/s access link, ss -ti shows bbr:(bw:41.2Mbps,mrtt:38.5,pacing_gain:1,cwnd_gain:2) in every sample for about two seconds while retrans grows by a sixth of the segments sent; then the gains alternate again. What is BBR doing, and where does 41.2 Mbit/s come from?

Answer

It is in policer mode. Two intervals in a row lost at least 50/256 of what they delivered at a steady rate, so BBR paces at their average delivery rate, gain 1, for 48 round trips: \(48 \times 38.5 = 1.85\) s (tcp_bbr.c:184–194, 664–673, 697–701). bw shows that average (:228), what the path let through: likely a token-bucket policer near 41 Mbit/s. Near miss: ProbeRTT, which has cwnd_gain:1 and lasts 200 ms.

3. Here is a symptom: behind an office switch with a shallow buffer, a CUBIC backup crawls whenever one BBR upload runs. The switch counts thousands of drops a minute, and the BBR host's retrans is in the tens of thousands while bbr:(bw:…) stays at the link rate. What is going on?

Answer

BBR's probes overflow the small buffer and only CUBIC reacts. Every eight rounds BBR sends a quarter BDP more than the link drains, so both flows lose packets; CUBIC cuts by 30% and regrows slowly, while BBRv1 only trims cwnd (Cao pp. 133–135; Ware p. 137). Near miss: "the cwnd gain of 2 fills the buffer". The cap binds only when BtlBw is overestimated; the probe alone overflows this buffer.

4. Choose and defend: a home uplink with about 40 BDPs of buffer carries your BBRv1 uploads and the family's CUBIC traffic. For fair shares and low delay, do you (a) switch every host you control to BBRv1, or (b) leave the congestion controls alone and run fq_codel as the uplink's bottleneck qdisc?

Answer

(b), if you control the device in front of the uplink. Flow queueing serves each flow's queue in turn, so shares stop depending on who fills the buffer, and CoDel holds the standing queue near 5 ms (the queue-delay chapter). With (a), any remaining CUBIC flow still fills 40 BDPs and the cap sets BBR's share against it (Ware pp. 140–142), at hundreds of milliseconds of RTT. The cost of (b): it must rate-limit just below the modem, so the queue forms where fq_codel runs.

5. Here is a symptom: a student repeats Cao et al.'s loss sweep on Linux 7.2 and sees BBR's goodput already falling at 17% random loss, earlier than Eq. 3's 20%. The pacing-gain log shows two-second stretches at 1.0. Is Eq. 3 wrong?

Answer

No; a second rule fired. Lost over delivered passes 50/256 at about 16.3% of packets sent, and with steady delivery the policer detector holds the gain at 1.0 for 48 rounds, the two-second stretches (tcp_bbr.c:188, 740–744, 697–701). Eq. 3 with the 1% margin gives 19.2% on 7.2 (:148, 252); Cao et al. name the detector only for BBR_1.5 (p. 134). Near miss: blaming the margin, which moves the cliff by under a point.

6. Of this chapter's three BBRv1 failures, the shallow-buffer retransmissions, the random-loss cliff and the deep-buffer share against CUBIC, which does BBRv3's 2% loss threshold address directly, and which does it leave mostly in place?

Answer

The first two; the third mostly stays. A probe losing over 2% in a round ends and sets inflight_longterm, and lossy rounds in CRUISE lower the short-term bounds (draft-06 §5.3.3.7, §5.5.10). In a deep buffer CUBIC causes no loss until the buffer overflows, and the cwnd gain of 2 remains (§5.6.1), so Ware's cap mechanism holds until overflow sets the long-term bound. Neither paper measured v3.

Recap

BBRv1 against CUBIC is decided by the buffer in BDPs. Far below one BDP, BBR's probes overflow it and only CUBIC backs off, so BBR wins goodput and pays in retransmissions. In a deep buffer CUBIC fills the queue, BBR is window-limited at its 2-BDP inflight cap, and the cap sets its share whatever the number of CUBIC flows. Under random loss BBR holds until \(g(1-p)\) falls below 1, near 20%, where the policer detector also fires. BBRv3 adds a 2% loss threshold and inflight bounds; Linux 7.2 still ships BBRv1.
