Chapter 12 · before you read

Pods with a speed limit

~7 min before · EyeQ pp. 299–305 of 297–311, a cover letter, one docs page and 50 lines of Cilium's BPF (~1.5 h) · ~22 min after · HOST
One packet from a pod annotated kubernetes.io/egress-bandwidth: "10M", the bench's 10 Mbit/s: TCP writes its departure time inside the pod, the time crosses the pod's veth into the host, Cilium's BPF program at the host's network card moves it later when the pod is over its rate, and fq on the card holds the packet until then.

The question this chapter answers

At Linux Plumbers 2021, Daniel Borkmann and Martynas Pumputis showed four netperf runs from one pod whose TCP socket was paced at 4 Gbit/s: 656, 1,275, 1,519 and 850 Mbit/s (slide 22). Then they patched the kernel so that it stopped zeroing skb->tstamp when a packet left the pod's network namespace, and ran again: 3,976, 3,961, 3,958 and 3,977 Mbit/s (slide 23). The pod and the rate were the same. The departure time TCP writes into every skb, the field the departure-time chapter followed into fq, was being thrown away at the pod's veth.

That loss matters most on a shared node. A Kubernetes node runs the pods of many tenants, the customers who share a cluster, and TCP shares a link per flow, so a tenant with more flows, or with UDP, takes more. Kubernetes lets you write a cap on a pod as an annotation, kubernetes.io/egress-bandwidth. Cilium enforces that cap by writing a departure time into the same field, from a BPF program on the host's network card. So one field has two writers, TCP inside the pod and Cilium on the host, and one reader, fq on the card, with a namespace hop between them that used to erase it.

The readings come in three parts. EyeQ (NSDI 2013) asks what a tenant should be promised and where the promise must be enforced. Martin KaFai Lau's 2022 patch series lets the stamp cross the hop. Cilium's Bandwidth Manager, read in its docs and in your fork, is the system that needs both.

Words you will meet

tenant
A customer of a shared cluster, owning some of its VMs or pods. The shapers chapter said "customers" for the same thing. EyeQ promises bandwidth per tenant endpoint; TCP knows nothing about tenants (p. 298).
hose model
A guarantee written per endpoint: a rate in and a rate out, whoever the endpoint talks to, as if all of a tenant's endpoints hung on one private switch (p. 299, footnote 1). Its rival, the "pipe" model, needs a rate for every pair of endpoints (p. 308).
admissible traffic
Traffic whose senders never send one receiver, in total, more than the bottleneck on the way can carry (p. 300). TCP keeps traffic admissible by backing off; EyeQ makes every transport keep it.
EyeQ
The paper's system: on every server a sender module (SEM) of rate limiters and a receiver module (REM) of rate meters, joined by small feedback packets, so that each tenant endpoint gets a minimum and a maximum rate (pp. 298, 301).
RCP (rate control protocol)
A congestion control in which a meter computes one explicit rate and tells the senders to use it, so they need not probe by losing packets (refs [27, 29]). EyeQ runs a variant per receiving endpoint (p. 302).
mono delivery time (skb->tstamp_type)
A mark on an skb saying that skb->tstamp holds a departure time on CLOCK_MONOTONIC, so the kernel may keep it when it forwards the packet. One bit in Linux 5.18 (2022); since 6.11 a two-bit field that also names realtime and TAI.
EDT rate limiter
A rate limit enforced by moving each packet's departure time: a packet may leave no earlier than its length over the rate after the previous packet of the same group. Cilium's runs in BPF at the host's network card (bpf/lib/edt.h).
Bandwidth Manager
Cilium's feature that holds each pod to its kubernetes.io/egress-bandwidth with the EDT rate limiter, puts fq on the host's network cards, and can make BBR the pods' congestion control. It is off by default.
BBR for pods
The Bandwidth Manager's option (bandwidthManager.bbr=true) that makes BBR the default congestion control for new pods. It needs Linux 5.18, so the departure time crosses the veth, and BPF host routing, so the packet keeps its socket.

Chapter 12 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. EyeQ's UDP tenant averages 2.5 Gb/s, under its 3 Gb/s share, yet its TCP neighbour waits ten times longer. What does a 100 ms average miss, and how often does EyeQ measure? pp. 299–302

Answer

It misses 5 ms bursts at line rate. 10 Gb/s for 5 ms in every 20 averages \(10 \times 5/20 = 2.5\) Gb/s, while each burst fills the receiver's switch port and the TCP tenant's requests queue behind it (Fig. 2, pp. 299–300). A 1 MB buffer fills in 800 µs, so EyeQ meters every 200 µs (p. 301). Near miss: TCP timeouts; the Fig. 2 caption says there were none.

2. EyeQ detects excess traffic at the receiver but slows it at the sender. Why not drop the excess at the receiver, which sees it first? pp. 301–302

Answer

Because a drop at the receiver slows only senders that react to drops. UDP keeps sending, and its packets have already crossed the switch port where the tenants collide (p. 301). So the receiver measures and sends back a rate, and the sender enforces it per destination (pp. 301–302). Cilium's ingress-bandwidth is the rejected design: a token bucket at delivery to the pod, with no feedback (token_bucket.h:16–52).

3. Before Linux 5.18 a pod's TCP stamped every packet, and fq on the host never saw the stamps. Where were they lost, and what did the kernel need to know before it could keep them? cover letter ¶1–¶5

Answer

At the veth into the host, and at any routing hop. The field held a receive time on the way in and a departure time on the way out, and nothing said which, so forwarding zeroed it (¶1–¶3). The kernel needed to know which kind it held: one bit now marks a monotonic departure time, kept when forwarding (skbuff.h:4507–4513) and cleared at local delivery (ip_input.c:237).

The chapter on one screen

EyeQ. TCP shares bandwidth per flow, blind to tenants (p. 298). EyeQ promises each tenant endpoint a minimum and a maximum rate in the hose model (p. 299), over milliseconds: a UDP tenant averaging 2.5 Gb/s in 5 ms bursts raises its neighbour's latency more than tenfold (Fig. 2, pp. 299–300). Lasting congestion stays on the servers' own links, so per-tenant state can live in the servers (p. 300). A receiver cannot slow UDP, so EyeQ meters at the receiver every 200 µs and enforces at the sender, with per-destination limiters set by feedback (pp. 301–302). The rate comes from an RCP-style rule, \(R \leftarrow R\,(1 - \alpha (y - C)/C)\), stable for \(0 < \alpha < 2\) (pp. 302–303), and settles within 5 ms against 14 UDP senders (p. 305).

The cover letter. skb->tstamp meant a receive time or a departure time, and nothing said which, so every forwarding hop zeroed it (¶1–¶4). Lau's series marks a monotonic departure time with one bit, keeps marked times when forwarding, and clears them at local delivery (¶5, v3, v4). Since v6.11 the bit is skb->tstamp_type.

Cilium. The Bandwidth Manager enforces kubernetes.io/egress-bandwidth with an EDT rate limiter in BPF on the host's devices, over mq and fq, and ingress-bandwidth with a token bucket. BBR for pods needs kernel 5.18 and BPF host routing, and none of it runs in kind (docs). In edt.h the limit is one addition per packet, \(t_\text{last} + \text{len}/\text{rate}\), and a drop beyond a 2 s horizon (lines 89–107).

A tenant's share, over milliseconds

A tenant's guarantee is written per endpoint, and it has to hold over a few milliseconds. TCP gives each flow an equal share, so a tenant gets more by opening more flows, and a UDP tenant takes whatever it sends (p. 298).

The hose model says what is promised. If \(N\) VMs with 1 Gb/s links all send to one 1 Gb/s receiver, EyeQ promises the receiver 1 Gb/s in total, drops the excess at the senders, and splits it among them max-min (p. 299): the shapers chapter's max-min fairness. In Fig. 1 (p. 298), tenant A's flow runs at 10 Gb/s alone and falls to A's minimum of 2 when tenant B's flow arrives.

Fig. 2 (pp. 299–300) is why "over milliseconds". Of the receiver's 9 Gb/s, TCP gets 6 and UDP 3. The UDP tenant sends 10 Gb/s for 5 ms, then nothing for 15 ms: \(10 \times 5/(5+15) = 2.5\) Gb/s on average, inside its share. One burst is \(10^{10} \times 0.005 / 8 = 6.25\) MB, against 2 MB of switch buffer (p. 305). The TCP tenant's latency rises more than tenfold, and a 100 ms average sees nothing (p. 300).

With high bisection bandwidth, randomized routing and admissible traffic, lasting congestion appears only on the access links (§2.3, p. 300), so per-tenant state belongs in the host. Cilium puts its limiter there.

On the spine, "10M" is a hose-model maximum on the way out; Kubernetes has no annotation for a minimum. Your fork's bandwidth.cilium.io/priority picks one of fq's three bands, served in weighted turns (bandwidth.go:34, 45–56), which is still no minimum rate.

Detect at the receiver, enforce at the sender

Contention at a receiver happens inside the switch, where neither server can see it, and a receiver that drops excess traffic slows only senders that back off. So EyeQ measures at the receiver and enforces at the sender: "receiver-side detection, sender-side reaction" (p. 301).

The timescale comes from the switch. Two ports sending at 10 Gb/s into one have 10 Gb/s of excess, so a 1 MB shared buffer fills in \(8 \times 10^6 / 10^{10} = 800\) µs (p. 301). The receiver's rate meter is a byte counter read every 200 µs, four readings per fill. It splits the receive link among the endpoints that are receiving, \(C_i = B_i \, C / \sum_{j \in A} B_j\), so an idle tenant's share goes to the others (p. 302).

Feedback is cheap: one 64-byte host-ACK (HACK) per 10 kB received, a 16-bit rate in the IP ID field, at most 64 Mb/s on a 10 Gb/s link whatever the number of senders (p. 302). A sender creates a limiter for a destination only when feedback from it arrives.

Cilium enforces egress at the sending node, EyeQ's place, and ingress with a token bucket at the receiving node: accept() refills tokens at the rate, caps them at one second's worth, and drops the packet when they run short, just before delivery to the pod (token_bucket.h:35–52, called at local_delivery.h:155). That is a policer with a one-second bucket and no feedback. TCP backs off from it (2,471 retransmissions in the docs' 10 s test); a UDP sender would not.

One rate per receiver, in milliseconds

The receiver computes one rate \(R_i\) for every flow sent to endpoint \(i\), from its measured receive rate \(y_i\) and its share \(C_i\), and it needs no count of senders (p. 302):

\[ R_i \leftarrow R_i \left(1 - \alpha \cdot \frac{y_i - C_i}{C_i}\right). \]

Too little traffic raises \(R_i\), too much lowers it. This is a variant of RCP with one difference the authors stress: RCP divides each link among its flows max-min, so it inherits TCP's per-flow sharing, while EyeQ runs the rule once per receiving endpoint, inside that endpoint's share (p. 302). With \(N\) long flows the rule settles at \(R^* = C/N\). It is stable for \(0 < \alpha < 2\), and near \(R^*\) each step multiplies the error by \(1 - \alpha\) (pp. 302–303).

Derivation · the logistic map and the stability range

Take one link of unit capacity and \(N\) senders at rate \(R\), so \(y = NR\) and the rule is \(R[n+1] = R[n]\,(1 + \alpha - \alpha N R[n])\). Put \(b = 1 + \alpha\) and \(z = (\alpha/b)\,N R\), which is the paper's \(z = \frac{b-1}{b}\frac{R}{R^*}\) with \(R^* = 1/N\). Then \(z[n+1] = \frac{\alpha N}{b} R[n]\,(b - \alpha N R[n]) = b\,z[n]\,(1 - z[n])\), the logistic map (footnote 2, p. 302).

Its fixed point \(z^* = 1 - 1/b\) is \(R = 1/N\). The slope there is \(f'(z^*) = b(1 - 2z^*) = 2 - b = 1 - \alpha\), and a fixed point is stable when \(|f'| < 1\), that is \(0 < \alpha < 2\), the paper's \(1 < b < 3\). Near it the error shrinks by \(|1-\alpha|\) per step, so \(\alpha = 1\) is fastest in this model, which has no feedback delay. The authors saw oscillations at high \(\alpha\) in practice and chose 0.5 (p. 303).

Put numbers on the paper's test (p. 305): 14 UDP senders whose limiters all start at line rate. The first reading is \(y = 14\,C\), and the factor is \(1 - 0.5 \times 13 = -5.5\): the rule would make the rate negative, so it is clamped, "taking care to keep \(R_i\) positive" (p. 302). From there the rate grows at most by \(1 + \alpha = 1.5\) per step. Run with a 1 Mb/s floor on 10 Gb/s, the rule needs 31 steps to come within 0.01% of \(R^*\), and \(31 \times 200\) µs is 6.2 ms: the paper's "about 30 iterations" and "6ms" (p. 303). The linear part alone needs \(\ln 10^{-4} / \ln 0.5 = 13.3\) steps; the rest is the climb from the floor. Fig. 6 settles in about 5 ms (p. 305).

Cilium has no loop: a pod's rate is its annotation. It keeps EyeQ's per-endpoint root limiter and drops the per-destination limiters and the feedback that sets them.

The stamp crosses the veth

Since Linux 5.18 the kernel keeps a departure time when it forwards a packet, because the skb now says what its tstamp holds, and clears it only at delivery to a local socket. So a pod's TCP stamps reach the host's fq.

The cover letter tells the history in four short paragraphs. skb->tstamp was first a receive time for SO_TIMESTAMP (¶1), then also a departure time read by fq (¶2). Nothing told them apart, so forwarding zeroed the field (¶3). That is right for a receive time, which fq would misread, and costs throughput for a departure time on its way to "fq@phy-dev" (¶4): the talk's 0.66–1.52 against 3.96–3.98 Gbit/s.

The fix is one bit (v3), now the 2-bit skb->tstamp_type (skbuff.h:728–733, 984). TCP sets it with every stamp (tcp_output.c:1555), a UDP socket with SO_TXTIME from its clock (ip_output.c:1474). skb_clear_tstamp() zeroes the field only when no type is set (skbuff.h:4507–4513), and every forwarding path calls it: the veth's skb_scrub_packet() (skbuff.c:6298), ip_forward_finish() (ip_forward.c:79), the BPF redirects (filter.c:2153, 2227, 2331). The real clearing moved to local delivery (v4, ip_input.c:237).

The stamps lab tests it on your kernel: 1,000 UDP datagrams stamped 1 ms apart, each handed down 5 ms early, nothing on s0 that reads stamps, plain fq on the router's r1. Median lateness, captured minus stamped:

senderarriving at rtr:r0leaving rtr:r1

CLOCK_MONOTONIC−4,939 µs+12.4 µs

Monotonic and TAI stamps are honoured one hop away; a realtime stamp is cleared like a receive time. EyeQ never met this problem: its sender module sat in the hypervisor's virtual switch, below every guest's stack (§4, p. 303).

Since the cover letter. In 5.18 only TCP set the bit; UDP wrote skb->tstamp bare (v5.18 ip_output.c:1550), so a pod's SO_TXTIME stamps were still zeroed. v6.11 renamed the bit tstamp_type (4d25ca2d6801) and made it follow the socket's clock (1693c5db6ab8, May 2024). The letter's helper shipped as bpf_skb_set_tstamp().

The talk's slip. Slide 24 explains the old reset with "Ingress is CLOCK_TAI, egress is CLOCK_MONOTONIC". The receive stamp is CLOCK_REALTIME: __net_timestamp() calls ktime_get_real() (skbuff.h:4448–4451; the same in v5.15). The argument stands: realtime runs about 56 years ahead of monotonic.

A pod's limit written as a time

Cilium holds a pod to its rate by giving each of its packets a departure time no earlier than the pod's previous one plus this packet's wire length over the rate. If TCP's own time is later, it stays. fq on the card does all the waiting (edt.h:89–107).

The program meets the packet twice. Leaving the pod, cil_from_container writes the pod's endpoint id into skb->queue_mapping (bpf_lxc.c:2435, 2442). At the host's card, cil_to_netdev calls edt_sched_departure() (bpf_host.c:1666), which reads the id back (edt.h:52–62) and looks up the pod's rate and last departure time, t_last, in the cilium_throttle map:

t = max(skb->tstamp, now)                   /* TCP's departure time, if it is later */
delay  = wire_len * NSEC_PER_SEC / bps      /* bps is bytes per second */
t_next = t_last + delay
if t_next <= t:            t_last = t        /* under the rate: the packet keeps its time */
elif t_next - now >= 2 s:  drop              /* DROP_EDT_HORIZON; t_last unchanged */
else:                      t_last = t_next; skb->tstamp = t_next

On the spine, "10M" becomes \(10{,}000{,}000 / 8 = 1{,}250{,}000\) bytes per second (GetBytesPerSec(), bandwidth.go:158–164). A 1,514-byte frame costs \(1514 \times 10^9 / 1{,}250{,}000 = 1{,}211.2\) µs, so a busy pod's frames leave 1.21 ms apart: 825.6 per second, 10.0 Mbit/s on the wire and \(10 \times 1448/1514 = 9.56\) Mbit/s of TCP payload, the bench's bottleneck rate from the congestion-avoidance chapter. A pod that was idle gets one skb out at once, through the first branch, and the next waits its own length; idle time builds no credit.

This is the Carousel chapter's stamper. The pod is a flow aggregate, t_last is its last stamp, and keeping the later of TCP's time and the pod's is Carousel's "keep the largest stamp". It even has the \(\max(\text{now}, \cdot)\) that chapter asked for. fq replaces the timing wheel, and the drop at the horizon is one of Carousel's two choices.

Set it beside EyeQ's limiter (p. 304): a token bucket per endpoint, its FIFO split per CPU, tokens borrowed from a shared pool under a lock, one 50 µs timer per CPU. Cilium has no queue, timer or lock of its own: one t_last per pod, read and written with READ_ONCE() and WRITE_ONCE() (edt.h:94–106), so two CPUs can race and let one extra packet through. The queues are fq, one per transmit queue (ops.go:98–150).

BBR for pods needs the socket too

For BBR to pace a pod, two things must reach the host's fq with the packet: TCP's departure time, which needs 5.18, and the packet's socket, which needs BPF host routing (docs, "BBR for Pods"). The socket matters for UDP pods too: it is what slows them when the limiter holds their packets.

The fork checks both. It probes for the bpf_skb_set_tstamp helper, the 5.18 marker (bandwidth.go:176–186), and refuses legacy host routing because "going via host stack will orphan skb->sk" (lines 192–196). Then it sets default_qdisc to fq and tcp_congestion_control to bbr (lines 255–271). A new network namespace copies the host's congestion control when it is created (tcp_ipv4.c:3528–3534), hence the docs' "newly spawned Pods".

Why the socket? fq marks a socket it sees as fq-paced (sch_fq.c:399–401, 450–453), and then TCP's own pacing timer stays off (tcp_output.c:2819). Before 5.18, with host routing, the socket arrived and the time did not, so fq derived gaps from the socket's rate (sch_fq.c:801–812), the departure-time chapter's 2013 model: slide 22. Without host routing, ip_rcv drops the socket (ip_input.c:580–583), and TSQ stops counting at the host, as netem's orphaning hid the queue from TSQ in the small-queues chapter.

The bench shows what a UDP pod loses with its socket. A sender runs edt.h's arithmetic at 10M and offers 20 Mbit/s; the router drops the socket, as the host stack would; fq on r1 has Cilium's settings. The limiter charges t_last for every packet it stamps, including the 7,354 that fq drops, so its clock runs to the 2 s horizon. fq holds at most 100 of the pod's packets, each for 2 s: by Little's law, \(100 / 2\ \text{s} = 50\) packets per second, 0.6 Mbit/s, as measured. With the socket kept and the default 212,992-byte buffer, the sender blocks before fq fills: 10.0 Mbit/s at 86 ms. That is backpressure, the Carousel chapter's deferred completion; an 8 MB buffer collapses again. A 100 ms horizon also cures it; a 2,000-packet flow limit keeps the rate behind a 2 s standing queue.

These runs use edt.h's arithmetic in a user-space sender; none ran on a Cilium node.

Real systems

Meet the system · Cilium's Bandwidth Manager

What it is. A feature of Cilium, the BPF-based Kubernetes network plugin, that holds each pod to its kubernetes.io/egress-bandwidth by stamping departure times in BPF on the host's network devices, with fq there to honour them. It also polices ingress-bandwidth and can make BBR the pods' congestion control.

Why it exists. The older bandwidth CNI plugin shapes with TBF, which the docs advise against "due to scalability concerns in particular for multi-queue network interfaces". Daniel Borkmann added the EDT version in August 2020 (29f4654035).

The parts you need.

  • The annotation, parsed into bytes per second (pkg/endpoint/events.go:197–244, bandwidth.go:158–164) and kept in the cilium_throttle map by endpoint id and direction (bpf/lib/edt.h:14–39).
  • The BPF: cil_from_container tags each packet with its pod (bpf_lxc.c:2435), cil_to_netdev runs edt_sched_departure() (bpf_host.c:1666), accept() polices ingress (local_delivery.h:155).
  • The qdisc reconciler: mq with fq leaves, horizon 2 s, \(2^{15}\) buckets (ops.go:94–150).
  • The sysctls: default_qdisc=fq and tcp_congestion_control cubic or bbr (bandwidth.go:220–277).

Where this chapter lives inside it. The tenant learning is the annotation, a hose-model maximum per pod on egress. Detect-at-the-receiver is the split between edt_sched_departure() at the sender and accept() at the receiver; the rate loop is absent. The stamp crossing the veth is t = ctx->tstamp (edt.h:90), real only since 5.18. The limiter is edt.h:89–107 over the fq leaves. BBR for pods is the sysctl write and the checks at bandwidth.go:183, 192–196.

Back to SO_TXTIME and the ETF qdisc

The departure-time chapter watched fq honour these stamps on the sender. Here they cross a router: since v6.11 the socket's clock becomes the skb's tstamp_type (ip_output.c:1474), so fq one hop later honours CLOCK_MONOTONIC and CLOCK_TAI stamps, and the hop zeroes CLOCK_REALTIME ones. The rest is in the full card in the departure-time chapter.

Back to sch_fq

Cilium runs fq with horizon 2s buckets 32768 and leaves flow_limit at 100 (ops.go:144–145): a socketless pod's whole queue, with flows_plimit counting what the limiter stamped and fq dropped. A BPF write to skb->tstamp leaves the type at 0; since v7.2 fq reads such a value as monotonic, with a warning (sch_fq.c:577–581). The rest is in the full card in the small-queues chapter.

Back to Linux tcp_bbr

BBR for pods is this module made the default in each new pod's namespace, where ss -ti would print bbr on every line. On this laptop tcp_available_congestion_control lists reno cubic until sudo modprobe tcp_bbr. The rest is in the full card in the BBR chapter.

Hands-on · Run edt.h by hand

~30 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil, then two CSVs to check against

Task. A pod annotated "10M", idle for 100 ms, sends 1,514-byte frames at \(t = 0\), 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 5.0 ms; the third carries a TCP departure time of 4.0 ms. (a) Write each frame's departure time and t_last. (b) How much leaves at once after idle? (c) The pod offers 20 Mbit/s of UDP and loses its socket before fq: predict how far ahead t_last runs, the delivered rate under fq's 100-packet flow limit, and the delay. (d) Redo (c) with a 100 ms horizon.

Done when. Your table has five rows, and (c) and (d) are within 10% of the steady state in labs/ch12-pods/data/edt-over-rx.csv and edt-short-rx.csv.

Hint 1 · direction

Follow the six lines in the limiter learning in order. A frame compares the pod's next slot with the later of now and its own TCP time, and only one branch moves t_last to t_next.

Hint 2 · mechanism

One frame costs \(1514 \times 10^9 / 1{,}250{,}000 = 1{,}211.2\) µs. For (c), the limiter charges every frame it stamps, whether or not fq keeps it, so t_last gains \(1{,}651 \times 1.2112\ \text{ms} - 1\ \text{s}\) per second at 20 Mbit/s. Then apply Little's law to fq's queue.

Hint 3 · nearly there

Frame 3: \(t = \max(4.0, 0.2) = 4.0\) and \(t_\text{next} = 1.2112 + 1.2112 = 2.4224 \le 4.0\), so it keeps its TCP time and t_last becomes 4.0. For (c), at the horizon fq holds 100 frames, each for 2 s.

Solution 
(a) times in ms, t_last = -100 before frame 1
frame  sent  t (own time)  t_next = t_last + 1.2112  branch        leaves at  t_last after
 1     0.0   0.0           -98.789                   keep its time  0.0        0.0
 2     0.1   0.1             1.2112                  stamp          1.2112     1.2112
 3     0.2   4.0 (TCP)       2.4224                  keep its time  4.0        4.0
 4     0.3   0.3             5.2112                  stamp          5.2112     5.2112
 5     5.0   5.0             6.4224                  stamp          6.4224     6.4224
(b) one skb of any size; the next waits its own length (no credit for idle time)
(c) t_last gains 1651 x 1.2112 ms - 1 s = 1.0 s per second: the 2 s horizon after about 2 s
    fq: 100 frames x 2 s each  ->  100 / 2 s = 50 frames/s = 50 x 1514 x 8 = 0.61 Mbit/s, delay 2 s
    bench: 0.59-0.62 Mbit/s from second 2, delay 1999.7 ms
(d) t_last at most 100 ms ahead: fq holds 0.1 s x 825.6/s = 83 frames, under 100, so none dropped
    delivered 825.6/s = 10.0 Mbit/s, delay 100 ms; the limiter drops the other 825/s
    bench: 10.005 Mbit/s, 100.0 ms, 6,523 horizon drops in 8 s

Expected output. The table above. Frame 4 was handed down at 0.3 ms and leaves at 5.21, after frame 3: frame 3's TCP time moved the pod's clock to 4.0.

What this shows. The pod has one timeline. A frame whose own time is later sets t_last to that time (edt.h:95–97), so the pod's next frame, from any flow, queues behind it; Carousel's aggregate stamp only adds \(\text{len}/R\) (the limiter learning). Parts (c) and (d) are the learning on BBR for pods: the drop horizon is the pod's queue in time, and without backpressure the limiter pays for drops it cannot see.

Common wrong turn. Writing \(t_\text{next} = \max(\text{now}, t_\text{last}) + \text{len}/\text{rate}\). That delays the first frame after idle by its own 1.21 ms; edt.h lets it go and charges its length to the next one.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: a pod annotated "20M" is always backlogged with 1,514-byte frames. How far apart does edt.h stamp them, and how many can be stamped ahead before it starts dropping?

Answer

605.6 µs apart, and about 3,302 frames. "20M" is 2,500,000 bytes per second (bandwidth.go:158–164), so the delay is \(1514 \times 10^9 / 2{,}500{,}000 = 605.6\) µs (edt.h:93), and drops start 2 s ahead (edt.h:104): \(2\ \text{s} / 605.6\ \mu\text{s} = 3{,}302\) frames, 5 MB. Near miss: without backpressure, fq's 100-frame flow limit binds first.

2. Predict the value: an endpoint's meter reads \(y = 1.2\,C\) while its senders all run at 300 Mb/s. What rate does the next HACK carry with \(\alpha = 0.5\), and what would \(\alpha = 2.5\) do?

Answer

270 Mb/s: \(300 \times (1 - 0.5 \times 0.2) = 270\) (p. 302), against a fixed point of \(300/1.2 = 250\). With \(\alpha = 2.5\), \(b = 3.5\) lies outside \(1 < b < 3\) and \(|1 - \alpha| = 1.5\), so each step overshoots by more than the last and the rate never settles (pp. 302–303). Near miss: "more gain settles faster", true only up to \(\alpha = 1\).

3. Here is a symptom: on kind with bandwidthManager.enabled=true, egress annotations do nothing, and the agent logged "BPF bandwidth manager could not read procfs. Disabling the feature." What happened?

Answer

The probe could not read net.core.default_qdisc (bandwidth.go:172–175), which exists only in the first network namespace (sysctl_net_core.c:592, 845). A kind node is a container with its own, as unshare -Urn showed on the system card, so the feature turns itself off without an error (docs, "Limitations"). Near miss: missing BPF support; the programs load fine.

4. Here is a symptom: a UDP video pod annotated "10M", with a 4 MB send buffer, offers 15 Mbit/s. Viewers get 0.6 Mbit/s, two seconds late, and the node's fq shows flows_plimit climbing. What happened?

Answer

The limiter charged the pod for packets fq dropped. With 4 MB of buffer the socket never blocks before fq's 100-packet flow limit, and edt.h charges every stamp (lines 94–107), so t_last runs to the 2 s horizon. 100 packets held 2 s each is 50 per second, by Little's law, as the bench's 8 MB run showed. Fix: the default buffer (10.0 Mbit/s at 86 ms), or pacing in the application.

5. Choose and defend: a tenant's pods suffer from a neighbour's bursty UDP pods on other nodes. Do you set kubernetes.io/ingress-bandwidth on the tenant's pods, or egress-bandwidth on the neighbour's?

Answer

Egress, on the neighbour's pods. A limit at the receiver controls only flows that back off on drops (p. 301); UDP does not, and by the time Cilium's ingress bucket drops a packet at delivery (token_bucket.h:35–52) the burst has crossed both nodes' cards. Egress acts before the packets leave (bpf_host.c:1666). The cost: an annotation per noisy pod, with a fixed rate and no feedback loop like EyeQ's (p. 302).

Recap

A tenant's share must hold per endpoint and over milliseconds, because 5 ms UDP bursts hide inside a 100 ms average. Only a sender can stop UDP, so EyeQ meters at the receiver and limits at the sender with a rate rule that settles in about 6 ms. Cilium keeps the sender's limiter without the loop: edt.h stamps each pod packet at \(t_\text{last} + \text{len}/\text{rate}\), and fq on the host's card waits. TCP's own stamps reach that fq only since 5.18, which marks a monotonic departure time. BBR for pods also needs the socket, and so does a UDP pod above its limit.
