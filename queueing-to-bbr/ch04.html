Chapter 4 · before you read

Shapers and fair schedulers

~5 min before · tc-tbf(8), tc-htb(8) and pp. 231–242 (~1.5 h) · ~22 min after · HOST MATH
One 10 Mbit/s uplink shared by two customers, A and B. A token bucket caps the whole uplink, HTB gives each customer a guaranteed 5 Mbit/s and lets it borrow up to 10 when the other is idle, and inside a customer's class deficit round robin shares among its flows.

The question this chapter answers

Picture a small office building with one 10 Mbit/s uplink and two customers behind it, A and B. With one first-in first-out queue at the uplink, the bottleneck treats every packet the same. B starts a nightly backup with four TCP connections, and A's one connection now gets a fifth of the link, because AIMD shares between connections and knows nothing about customers. A's packets also wait behind B's queue.

The building wants three things. Cap the uplink at the 10 Mbit/s it pays for, so the queue forms in its own router and not in the provider's. Give each customer 5 Mbit/s for sure, and let a busy customer use the other half while its neighbour is idle. Inside one customer, share between flows fairly, even when one flow sends full 1500-byte frames and another sends 64-byte packets.

Linux has a tool for each: the token bucket filter (TBF), the hierarchical token bucket (HTB), and deficit round robin (DRR). The two man pages date from 2001 and 2002 and say what the knobs are. The 1995 paper says what fairness costs and proves how close DRR gets. This chapter is about the arithmetic behind all three.

Words you will meet

token bucket
A counter of permission to send. It fills at rate \(r\) up to a depth \(b\), the burst; a packet may leave only by spending tokens equal to its size.
arrival curve
An upper bound \(\alpha(t)\) on the bytes a flow sends in any window of length \(t\). A token bucket enforces \(\alpha(t) = b + rt\).
shaper
A token bucket with a queue in front of it. A packet without enough tokens waits until it has them.
policer
A token bucket with no queue. A packet without enough tokens is dropped, or re-marked.
work-conserving
A scheduler is work-conserving if the link is never idle while a packet is waiting. A shaper is not, by design.
HTB class (rate, ceil)
One node of a hierarchical token bucket tree. Its rate is a guaranteed share with its own bucket. Its ceil is a second bucket that caps it. Between the two it may borrow what its parent is not using.
quantum
The bytes a round-robin scheduler grants one queue per turn.
max-min fairness
No flow can get more without taking from a flow that has the same or less. On one link: equal shares, except that a flow wanting less gets what it wants, and the rest is shared again.
generalized processor sharing (GPS)
The ideal fluid scheduler: every backlogged flow is served at once, bit by bit, in proportion to its weight. Weighted fair queueing (WFQ) sends packets in the order they would finish under GPS.
deficit round robin (DRR)
Round robin over queues in which each queue gets a quantum of bytes per round and carries what it could not use in a deficit counter.

Chapter 4 · after you read

The questions you carried in

1. A TBF has rate \(r\) and bucket \(b\). What is the most it lets through in any window of \(t\) seconds, and what does its latency knob turn into? tc-tbf(8) ALGORITHM, PARAMETERS

Answer

At most \(b + rt\) bytes in every window of length \(t\): the bucket holds at most \(b\) tokens when the window opens and gains at most \(rt\) during it, and each byte spends one (ALGORITHM; sch_tbf.c:36). latency becomes the queue size, \(\text{limit} = r \times \text{latency} + b\), 95,268 bytes here. Near miss: reading it as the longest wait. With the bucket empty, a full queue takes \(\text{limit}/r = 76\) ms to drain.

2. When customer B is idle, customer A's class sends at 10 Mbit/s although its rate is 5. Whose tokens pay for the extra packets, and what decides how two borrowing classes split the spare? tc-htb(8) CLASSES; p. 235

Answer

The parent's. A class whose own tokens are spent, but not its ceil tokens, may borrow (sch_htb.c:538–547): its packets are charged to the parent's rate bucket and every ceil bucket above (lines 700–718). Two borrowers split the spare by DRR in proportion to quantum (lines 926–928; p. 235), and quantum defaults to rate / r2q. Near miss: "in proportion to rate", true only while quantum follows rate.

3. Why is round robin over packets unfair when packet sizes differ, and what one number per queue does DRR keep to fix it at constant cost per packet? pp. 233–237

Answer

Round robin counts packets and the link carries bytes, so 1500-byte packets get 23 times the bandwidth of 64-byte ones, \(\text{Max}/\text{Min}\) (p. 233). DRR keeps a deficit counter per queue: each round adds a quantum, head packets leave while they fit, and the rest carries over (p. 234). A backlogged flow stays within \(\text{Max}\) of \(K \cdot \text{Quantum}\) after \(K\) rounds (Theorem 4.2, p. 236), and with the quantum at least \(\text{Max}\) the work is \(O(1)\) (Theorem 4.5, p. 237).

The chapter on one screen

tc-tbf(8). A shaper: tokens arrive at a steady rate into a bucket that starts full, a packet needs tokens for its size, and without them it waits in a bounded queue (ALGORITHM). The knobs are rate, burst (the bucket), limit or latency (the queue), and an optional peakrate with a small second bucket, mtu (PARAMETERS). TBF is not work-conserving (DESCRIPTION).

tc-htb(8). A tree of such buckets. Each class has a guaranteed rate and a ceil it may reach "if its parent has bandwidth to spare"; ceil defaults to rate, which means no borrowing (CLASSES). Filters, or the default class, pick the leaf (CLASSIFICATION). Spare bandwidth is served round robin, quantum = rate / r2q bytes at a time. The section on sharing reads "FIXME".

The DRR paper. With first-come-first-serve, one aggressive flow can take the link (p. 232). Fair queueing [DKS89] sends packets in the order bit-by-bit round robin would finish them: fair, but \(O(\log n)\) per packet (p. 233). Nagle's per-flow round robin is \(O(1)\) but counts packets, so a flow can get \(\text{Max}/\text{Min}\) times another's bandwidth (p. 233). DRR carries unused quantum in a deficit counter (p. 234). The counter stays below \(\text{Max}\) (Lemma 4.1, p. 235), a backlogged flow's error after \(K\) rounds is at most \(\text{Max}\) (Theorem 4.2, p. 236), and the work is \(O(1)\) when every quantum is at least \(\text{Max}\) (Theorem 4.5, p. 237). Simulations show a misbehaving flow isolated (pp. 238–239). DRR's weak side is latency: a small packet may wait for a quantum from every other flow (p. 241).

The token-bucket bound

A token bucket with rate \(r\) and depth \(b\) lets through at most \(b + rt\) bytes in any interval of length \(t\). That line is the traffic's arrival curve. Every TBF knob either shapes that line or sizes the queue behind it.

Put numbers on the spine's uplink cap, tbf rate 10mbit burst 32kb: \(r = 1.25\) MB/s and \(b = 32{,}768\) bytes. Any 10 ms window carries at most \(32{,}768 + 12{,}500 = 45{,}268\) bytes, 3.6 times the average. Behind a 1 Gbit/s NIC (\(C = 125\) MB/s) a full bucket drains at \(C - r\), so a burst lasts \(b/(C - r) = 0.26\) ms, about 22 full frames; then packets leave at exactly \(r\).

Derivation · the bound, and how long a burst lasts

Let \(x(s)\) be the tokens at time \(s\), with \(x(s) \le b\) always. Over \([s, s+t]\) at most \(rt\) tokens arrive, and every byte sent removes one token. Tokens never go below zero, so bytes sent \(\le x(s) + rt \le b + rt\). Le Boudec and Thiran prove the same for their "leaky bucket controller" (Proposition 1.2.3, p. 11), with the arrival curve defined on p. 7.

A burst at line rate \(C\) from a full bucket: tokens fall at \(C - r\), so they last \(t^* = b/(C-r)\), and the burst carries \(Ct^* = bC/(C-r)\), slightly more than \(b\) because tokens keep arriving during it. Here \(t^* = 32{,}768 / (123.75 \times 10^6) = 0.265\) ms and \(Ct^* = 33{,}099\) bytes.

With a peak bucket (rate \(P\), depth \(M\)) the curve is \(\min(M + Pt,\ b + rt)\). The two lines cross at \(t = (b - M)/(P - r)\). For peakrate 20mbit mtu 1514 that is \(31{,}254 / (1.25 \times 10^6\ \text{B/s}) = 25\) ms: the same bucket now drains over 25 ms at 20 Mbit/s instead of in a quarter of a millisecond.

The kernel's header comment states the same bound (net/sched/sch_tbf.c:34–36) and keeps the bucket as time: toks = min_t(s64, now - q->t_c, q->buffer) (line 295) caps the credit at \(b/r\), 26.2 ms of sending here, and each packet spends its own transmission time (line 306). A packet larger than the bucket can never pay, so tbf_enqueue() drops it (line 258).

The queue is separate: tc turns latency into \(\text{limit} = r \times \text{latency} + b\) and gives it to a hidden bfifo, 95,268 bytes for 50 ms (the system card prints it).

Since the man page. tc-tbf(8) (13 December 2001) ties accuracy to the timer tick: one packet per jiffy, about 1 Mbit/s at HZ = 100. tc-htb(8) NOTES asks for a 12 KB burst at 10 Mbit/s for the same reason. Tokens are now nanoseconds and the watchdog is a high-resolution timer (net/sched/sch_api.c:633); on the bench HTB's default 1600-byte burst reached the full 10 Mbit/s.

Shaping delays, policing drops

A shaper and a policer enforce the same arrival curve and differ only in what happens to a packet without tokens: the shaper makes it wait, the policer drops it. Le Boudec and Thiran list both: data that does not conform is "discarded", or "put in a buffer" (p. 10). So a shaper is a queue, and the queue-delay chapter's argument about a standing queue applies to it.

One Reno flow for customer A, held to 5 Mbit/s five ways on the 40 ms bench (2 October 2026):

At 5 Mbit/s and 40 ms the pipe size is 16.5 frames, and Reno's window grows to pipe plus queue: 67 packets with a 50-packet pfifo, 1,017 with HTB's default leaf, a pfifo as long as the veth's txqueuelen. By Little's law, 1,000 packets draining at 413 per second wait 2.42 s. An AQM leaf removes the queue, at a price: the halved window falls below the pipe and the link idles for part of each cycle.

The policer keeps the RTT at 40 ms, but its bucket must hold a whole window: with no slower link in front of it, each RTT's window arrives as one burst. A 16 KB bucket holds 10.8 frames, below the pipe, and the cwnd stayed between 5 and 11, about 2.4 Mbit/s (measured 2.33). A 64 KB bucket reaches the rate, with five times the shaper's retransmissions.

The man page's slip. tc-tbf(8) PARAMETERS says latency "specifies the maximum amount of time a packet can sit in the TBF". With \(\text{limit} = r \times \text{latency} + b\), a packet that joins a full queue while the bucket is empty waits \(\text{limit}/r = \text{latency} + b/r\), here 76 ms; the bench measured 75 ms of backlog. The kernel's \(\text{lat} = (L - B)/R\) (sch_tbf.c:69) is one burst meeting a full bucket.

Rate, ceil and borrowing

An HTB class keeps two buckets, tokens at its rate and ctokens at its ceil, and together they set its mode (sch_htb.c:538–547). With ctokens below zero it cannot send; with tokens at or above zero it sends on its own rate; in between it may borrow. A borrowed packet goes out at the parent's level, charged to the parent's rate bucket and to every ceil bucket above (htb_charge_class(), lines 700–718).

Customer A alone, 8 s into the bench run (tc -s class show, 2 October 2026, an excerpt):

Half of A's 6,051 packets went on its own rate and half were borrowed, as a 5 Mbit/s rate under a 10 Mbit/s ceil predicts. On a leaf, lended counts packets sent on its own rate (line 711) and borrowed those sent on tokens from above (line 714), so the root's lended matches A's borrowed. The buckets are time in 64 ns ticks (include/net/pkt_sched.h:48): a fresh class's tokens: 40000 is 2.56 ms, the time 1,600 bytes take at 5 Mbit/s.

Two borrowers share the spare by DRR at the parent's level: a class sends, its deficit drops by the packet's length, and once it is negative the class gets another quantum and the turn passes (lines 926–928). The quantum is rate / r2q bytes (line 2033), clamped to 1,000–200,000 unless set (lines 2036–2042), so by default the spare follows rate, the weighting of p. 235. With rates 3 and 1 Mbit/s, the 6 Mbit/s spare split 4.5 to 1.5 with default quanta (goodput 7.17 and 2.39) and 3 to 3 with quantum 1514 on both (5.74 and 3.82).

Since the man page. tc-htb(8) LINK SHARING ALGORITHM still reads "FIXME" in iproute2 7.2.0. The kernel names the model: HTB is "another implementation of Floyd's formal sharing" (sch_htb.c:46), the link-sharing model of Floyd and Jacobson (1995).

Fair in bytes at constant cost

DRR gives each queue a quantum of bytes per round and a deficit counter for what it could not use, which removes round robin's \(\text{Max}/\text{Min}\) unfairness (p. 233). Theorem 4.2 (p. 236): "After any \(K\) rounds the difference between \(K \cdot \text{Quantum}_i\) (i.e., the bytes that flow \(i\) should have sent) and the bytes that flow \(i\) actually sends is bounded by \(\text{Max}\)." Theorem 4.5 (p. 237) gives the cost: \(O(1)\) work per packet if every quantum is at least \(\text{Max}\).

Trace Figs 2–3 (p. 235): quantum 500, queues (head first) #1 [200, 750, 20], #2 [500, 500], #3 [100, 600, 200], #4 [180, 700, 50]. Round one sends 200, 500, 100 and 180 and leaves counters of 300, 0, 400 and 320. Round two: #1 has 800 and sends 750 and 20; #2 sends 500; #3 has 900 and sends 600 and 200; #4 has 820 and sends 700 and 50. Totals 970, 1000, 900 and 930 against 1000 each, every error below \(\text{Max}\), 750.

Derivation · Theorem 4.2 in three lines (p. 236)

Let \(DC_k\) be the counter after round \(k\) and \(\text{bytes}_k\) what the flow sent in it. While the flow is backlogged, everything granted is either sent or carried: \(\text{bytes}_k + DC_k = Q + DC_{k-1}\).

Sum over \(K\) rounds; the counters telescope: \(\text{sent}_K = KQ + DC_0 - DC_K = KQ - DC_K\), since \(DC_0 = 0\).

Lemma 4.1 (p. 235) gives \(0 \le DC_K < \text{Max}\): a packet left behind is larger than the counter, and no packet is larger than \(\text{Max}\). So \(KQ - \text{Max} < \text{sent}_K \le KQ\). Two flows with the same quantum therefore differ by less than \(\text{Max}\) after any number of rounds (Corollary 4.3, p. 237).

Linux's sch_drr.c follows Fig. 4: send the head packet only if len <= cl->deficit (line 389), else add a quantum and move the class to the tail (lines 404–405); the default quantum is 1514 bytes, MTU plus link header (line 86). HTB's DRR sends first and charges after, so its deficit can go negative (sch_htb.c:926–928), and a class whose packets exceed its quantum sends one per turn whatever the quantum. The queue-delay chapter's flow queueing is DRR over hashed flows: RFC 8290 calls fq_codel "a modified Deficit Round Robin (DRR) queue scheduler" (§1; sch_fq_codel.c:300–319).

Since the paper. The journal version (IEEE/ACM ToN, June 1996) bounds any interval instead: two flows backlogged in \((t_1, t_2)\) differ in normalised service by at most \(2\,\text{Max} + Q\) (Theorem 3, p. 379).

The paper's slip. Page 237 compares the algorithms "in Figure 1". Figure 1 is the parking lot (p. 232); the comparison is Table 1 (p. 238). The journal version says "Table I".

Max-min fairness and its ideal

Max-min fairness raises the smallest allocation as far as it can go, then the next, and so on (Bertsekas and Gallager, pp. 525–526). On one link it is water-filling: raise all rates together, freeze a flow when it reaches its demand, and continue until the link is full (pp. 527–528). DKS write it as \(\mu_i = \min(\mu_\text{fair}, \rho_i)\) (p. 3).

On the spine, customer A holds 5 Mbit/s while B is busy, with three flows: a 1 Mbit/s video call and two bulk uploads. The call takes its 1 and the bulk flows split the remaining 4: (1, 2, 2). With B idle and A borrowing up to 10: (1, 4.5, 4.5). HTB applies a weighted version one level up: rates first, then the spare by quantum.

Derivation · water-filling on one link

Link capacity \(C\), demands \(\rho_1 \le \rho_2 \le \dots \le \rho_n\). Start with all flows active and fair share \(f = C/n\). If \(\rho_1 \le f\), give flow 1 its demand, remove it, and recompute \(f = (C - \rho_1)/(n-1)\) for the rest. Repeat until the smallest remaining demand exceeds \(f\); those flows all get \(f\). Each flow ends with \(\min(\rho_i, f)\), the DKS formula. Spine: \(C = 5\), demands \((1, \infty, \infty)\): \(f = 5/3 > 1\), so the call gets 1, then \(f = 4/2 = 2\).

The ideal scheduler for it is GPS, a fluid server that serves every backlogged flow at once, in proportion to its weight (Parekh and Gallager, 1993). DKS approximate the equal-weight case packet by packet: each packet gets a finish number \(F = \max(F_\text{prev}, R(t)) + P\), and the smallest goes next (p. 3). This first weighted fair queueing stays within one packet of GPS but costs \(O(\log n)\) to keep sorted (p. 233); DRR stays within \(\text{Max}\) at \(O(1)\) and pays in latency (p. 241).

Jain's fairness index from the queueing-maths chapter measures the result, within limits. In the build lab, round robin scores 0.640 and DRR 1.000. But the max-min answer (1, 2, 2) scores \(25/27 = 0.93\) on raw rates, because the index measures equality; divided by their max-min shares, the rates score 1.

Idle on purpose

A work-conserving scheduler never leaves the link idle while a packet waits. DRR is one, since its active list holds only queues with packets (p. 235), and so are fq_codel and HTB while a backlogged class can borrow up to the link rate. TBF is not: it "may throttle itself, although packets are available" (tc-tbf(8) DESCRIPTION). Nor is an HTB class whose ceil sits below what the link could give, and the default ceil is the rate (CLASSES).

On the bench, class A at rate 5 Mbit/s got 9.56 Mbit/s alone with ceil 10, and 4.77 with ceil 5 while half the link sat unused: what a customer paying for 5 Mbit/s should get.

Holding a packet back needs a clock. tbf_dequeue() arms the qdisc watchdog for the moment the head packet's deficit is paid (sch_tbf.c:322); HTB arms it for the earliest class in its per-level wait tree (htb_do_events(), sch_htb.c:749; the timer at line 993). One shaper cannot serve many flows fairly, because it "MUST NOT reorder packets" (line 328); fairness needs a queue per flow.

Why shape at all? The provider polices the building at 10 Mbit/s, and a policer starves TCP. Shaping one hop earlier moves the queue into a box you control, where DRR and an AQM manage it. The later chapters on host queues and on sending by the clock ask what a timer and a queue per shaper cost with thousands of flows on one host.

Real systems

Meet the system · HTB and TBF, built with tc and read with tc -s

What it is. Two queueing disciplines in Linux traffic control. TBF holds one queue to a token-bucket rate; HTB is a tree of classes with rates and ceils that share one link. You build them with tc and read them with tc -s.

Why it exists. To make one link act as several slower ones that lend to each other. Alexey Kuznetsov wrote TBF; Martin Devera wrote HTB in 2001–2002 to replace the CBQ qdisc (the HTB user guide, §1).

The parts you need.

  • The qdisc, htb default N r2q 10: unclassified packets go to class N, or skip shaping if N does not exist (direct_packets_stat, sch_htb.c:272–275).
  • Classes: rate, ceil, burst, cburst, quantum, prio. Inner classes lend; leaves hold a queue.
  • Filters: flower or u32 rules that map a packet to a leaf's classid.
  • Leaf queues: pfifo by default, as long as the device's txqueuelen, or any qdisc you attach, such as fq_codel.

Where this chapter lives inside it. The token-bucket bound is TBF's rate and burst, and HTB's tokens and ctokens. Shaping is the leaf queue: its backlog is the delay a shaper adds, and TBF's hidden bfifo is where latency became bytes. Borrowing is lended and borrowed. DRR is quantum and r2q between sibling classes, and fq_codel or drr inside a class. Idle on purpose is overlimits: times a class or TBF had a packet but no tokens.

Hands-on · Token buckets and DRR rounds by hand

~30 min · trace by hand · paper and pencil

Task. The uplink cap is tbf rate 10mbit burst 32kb latency 50ms behind a 1 Gbit/s NIC. (a) Find the most bytes in any 10 ms window, how long a full-bucket burst lasts at line rate, the limit tc sets, and the longest wait under sustained overload. (b) Add peakrate 20mbit mtu 1514: how long does a burst last now? (c) Customer A's flows A1, A2 and A3 send 1500-, 600- and 900-byte packets and are always backlogged. Trace three rounds of DRR with quantum 1500, then packet round robin, then DRR with quantum 500.

Done when. You have five numbers for (a) and (b), a DRR table of counters and bytes per flow per round, round robin's totals, and the round in which A1 first sends with quantum 500.

Hint 1 · direction

Everything in (a) and (b) follows from \(b + rt\), from \(\min(M + Pt,\ b + rt)\), and from \(\text{limit} = r \times \text{latency} + b\). For (c), follow Fig. 4 on p. 236: add the quantum when a queue's turn starts, send while the head packet fits, keep the rest.

Hint 2 · mechanism

Units first: in tc, 32kb is 32,768 bytes and 10mbit is 1.25 MB/s. A burst from a full bucket drains it at \(C - r\). Under sustained overload the bucket is empty, so the last byte of a full queue waits \(\text{limit}/r\).

Hint 3 · nearly there

DRR round one: A1 has 1500 and sends one packet (0 left). A2 has 1500, sends two 600s and keeps 300. A3 has 1500, sends one 900 and keeps 600. In round two, A2 starts with \(300 + 1500 = 1800\).

Solution

(a) Any 10 ms: \(32{,}768 + 1.25 \times 10^6 \times 0.01 = 45{,}268\) bytes. Burst: \(32{,}768 / (123.75 \times 10^6\ \text{B/s}) = 0.265\) ms, carrying \(125 \times 10^6\ \text{B/s} \times 0.265\ \text{ms} = 33{,}099\) bytes, about 22 frames of 1514 bytes. Limit: \(62{,}500 + 32{,}768 = 95{,}268\) bytes. Longest wait: \(95{,}268 / (1.25 \times 10^6\ \text{B/s}) = 76\) ms. (b) The peak bucket meets the rate bucket at \(t = (32{,}768 - 1{,}514) / (1.25 \times 10^6\ \text{B/s}) = 25\) ms, so the burst now lasts 25 ms at 20 Mbit/s and carries \(1{,}514 + 2.5 \times 10^6 \times 0.025 = 64{,}022\) bytes.

round 1  sent (DC left)    1500 (0)      1200 (300)     900 (600)
round 2                    1500 (0)      1800 (0)      1800 (300)
round 3                    1500 (0)      1200 (300)    1800 (0)
total after 3 rounds       4500          4200          4500        ideal 4500 each

Expected output. Check (a) on the bench with the system card's tc qdisc show dev r1 invisible: limit 95268b. The build lab's simulator gives the same totals: 4500, 4200 and 4500 for DRR, and 4500, 1800 and 2700 for round robin.

What this shows. The token-bucket learning in numbers, and Theorem 4.2 (p. 236) by hand: A2's error after three rounds is 300 bytes, its leftover counter, and no flow is ever off by \(\text{Max}\) or more. Round robin gives A2 40% of A1's bytes, the \(\text{Min}/\text{Max}\) ratio of p. 233. With quantum 500, A1 sends nothing for two of every three visits: the wasted work Theorem 4.5 (p. 237) rules out when the quantum is at least \(\text{Max}\).

Common wrong turn. Reading 32kb as 32,000 bits. tc's kb is kilobytes (1,024 bytes) and kbit is kilobits; mixing them is an error of 8 or more, and the HTB user guide calls it the most frequent tc question.

Hands-on · Build DRR in forty lines

Done when. Round robin and DRR print different shares after 1000 rounds, and your worst DRR error is below 1500 bytes.

Hint 1 · direction

Keep a deque per flow and a deficit counter per flow. The bound needs every flow backlogged for all 1000 rounds, so the starter gives each flow 30,000 packets.

Hint 2 · mechanism

Per visit: add the quantum; while the queue is not empty and its head fits in the counter, send it and subtract its size; if the queue emptied, reset the counter to zero. Append a copy of the bytes-sent dict after each round.

Hint 3 · nearly there 
dc[f] += quantum
while q[f] and q[f][0] <= dc[f]:
    dc[f] -= q[f][0]; sent[f] += q[f].popleft()
Solution 
def rr(queues, rounds):
    q = {f: deque(p) for f, p in queues.items()}; sent = {f: 0 for f in q}; trace = []
    for _ in range(rounds):
        for f in q:
            if q[f]: sent[f] += q[f].popleft()
        trace.append(dict(sent))
    return trace

def drr(queues, rounds, quantum):
    q = {f: deque(p) for f, p in queues.items()}; sent = {f: 0 for f in q}
    dc = {f: 0 for f in q}; trace = []
    for _ in range(rounds):
        for f in q:                          # the active list, in a fixed order
            if not q[f]: dc[f] = 0; continue
            dc[f] += quantum
            while q[f] and q[f][0] <= dc[f]:
                dc[f] -= q[f][0]; sent[f] += q[f].popleft()
            if not q[f]: dc[f] = 0
        trace.append(dict(sent))
    return trace

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Predict the value: the uplink TBF is rate 10mbit burst 32kb latency 50ms, saturated for minutes by a bulk upload. What limit does tc set, and what is the longest a packet waits?

Answer

95,268 bytes and about 76 ms. \(\text{limit} = r \times \text{latency} + b = 62{,}500 + 32{,}768\) (tc-tbf(8) PARAMETERS). Under sustained load the bucket is empty, so a packet that joins a full queue waits \(95{,}268 / (1.25 \times 10^6\ \text{B/s}) = 76\) ms; the bench measured 75. Near miss: 50 ms, the knob's value, which is the wait only when one burst meets a full bucket (sch_tbf.c:69).

2. Here is a symptom: after you build the two-customer tree, B's backup runs at hundreds of Mbit/s. Every class shows Sent 0 bytes, and tc -s qdisc show prints direct_packets_stat 246585. What happened?

Answer

No packet reached a class. The filters did not match, a wrong port say, and default names a class that does not exist, so htb_classify() sends every packet to the direct queue, which skips all buckets (sch_htb.c:272–275; tc-htb(8) CLASSIFICATION). The bench reproduced it at 353 Mbit/s. Near miss: a wrong rate; the counters are zero because no packet arrived.

3. Here is a symptom: A's web pages take seconds to start while A's backup runs. A's class is rate 5mbit ceil 5mbit, no leaf qdisc given, and ss -ti on the backup shows rtt:2459 on a 40 ms path with almost no loss. What is the queue, and what would you change?

Answer

HTB's default leaf, a pfifo as long as the txqueuelen: 1000 packets. The backup's window grows to pipe plus queue, and by Little's law 1,000 packets at 413 per second wait 2.42 s, with every web request behind them (the shaper-and-policer learning; Le Boudec p. 10). Give the class an AQM leaf: with fq_codel the bench's RTT fell to 44 ms. Near miss: raising ceil, which cannot help while B is busy.

4. Choose and defend: the provider enforces the building's 10 Mbit/s. Do you leave it to the provider's policer, or shape to 9.5 Mbit/s in your own router? What does each cost?

Answer

Shape, just below the provider's rate. A policer drops instead of delaying: on the bench a 5 Mbit/s policer with a 16 KB bucket gave TCP 2.33 Mbit/s. Shaping one hop earlier puts the bottleneck queue in your router, where HTB and DRR keep customers apart and an AQM keeps it short (tc-tbf(8) DESCRIPTION; Le Boudec p. 10). The cost is 5% of the link, a timer and a queue. Near miss: "the provider's policer is free"; its drops land on your customers' TCP.

5. Predict the value: rates 3 and 1 Mbit/s, ceils 10, under a 10 Mbit/s root class, both customers backlogged. How is the link split with the default r2q, and with quantum 1514 on both?

Answer

7.5 and 2.5 Mbit/s, then 6 and 4. Each class first gets its rate, and DRR at the parent's level shares the 6 Mbit/s spare in proportion to quantum (sch_htb.c:926–928; p. 235). Default quanta are rate / 10, 37,500 and 12,500 bytes, a 3:1 split of the spare; equal quanta split it evenly. Measured goodput: 7.17 / 2.39 and 5.74 / 3.82. Near miss: "in proportion to rate", true only while quantum follows rate.

6. Here is a symptom: with customer A sending alone, a colleague reads lended: 3029 borrowed: 3022 on A's class and concludes that A is lending bandwidth to B. What do the counters say?

Answer

That A sent 3,029 packets on its own 5 Mbit/s and 3,022 on tokens from the root. On a leaf, lended counts packets sent at its own level (sch_htb.c:711) and borrowed those sent on a parent's tokens (line 714); the root's lended: 3022 is the matching entry. Near miss: the user guide's word "donated" (§7), which fits a parent better than a leaf.

Recap

A token bucket allows at most \(b + rt\) bytes in any window of length \(t\). A shaper enforces it by queueing and needs an AQM leaf; a policer drops, and TCP suffers unless the bucket holds a whole window. HTB guarantees each class its rate, lets it borrow up to its ceil, and splits the spare by DRR in proportion to quantum. DRR is fair in bytes to within one maximum packet at constant cost, once the quantum is at least that packet, and approximates GPS, the fluid ideal of max-min fairness. Shapers idle the link on purpose, at the price of a timer and a queue.
